General Overview

The text opens the study of the actual Moreh Nevuchim and explains that it is being read through Rav Kapach’s Hebrew translation from the Arabic, with the teacher noting that the work is really a collection of essays sent to a student and later compiled. Rambam presents the work as a guide for a selective audience: a religious person with sound character, intellectual curiosity, and some philosophical training who is troubled by tensions between Tanakh and philosophy. He says the book explains ambiguous terms, borrowed expressions, and veiled imagery in Tanakh, but only in the form of rashei perakim, not exhaustive answers, and he compares human understanding and prophecy to flashes of light that vary from person to person.

Introductory Letter to Rabbi Yosef ben Rabbi Yehuda

Rambam writes to רבי יוסף בן רבי יהודה because the student came from afar seeking guidance, showed great diligence and intellectual desire, and had already sent him letters, poetry, and prose from Alexandria. He says he first suspected that the student’s desire exceeded his actual attainment, but after guiding him through mathematics, astronomy, and then logic, he came to trust his understanding and felt ready to reveal to him the secrets of the prophetic books. The letter presents a hierarchy of study in which the student advances from preliminary disciplines to deeper theological matters.

Rambam says he had previously restrained the student and directed him to proceed in order, because he wanted truth to be reached properly and not by accident. He describes how, during their meetings, he would explain difficult verses and sayings of the sages, and when the student departed, the loss moved him to write the treatise in chapters and send it to him and others of his level. The letter frames the Moreh as a continuation of personal teaching rather than as a general public book.

The Purpose of the Moreh Nevuchim

Rambam says the first purpose of the work is to explain terms in the prophetic books that have multiple meanings, borrowed meanings, or meanings that are uncertain. He gives examples by category, including words that can mean different things in different contexts and expressions that are used metaphorically, such as bodily language applied to God in order to signify divine providence rather than literal corporeality. He makes clear that the work is aimed not at the masses, not at beginners, and not even at scholars who know only the legal side of Torah.

The book is directed to a religious person who already believes in the truth of Torah, has achieved moral refinement, has studied philosophy, and feels pulled between philosophical reason and the surface meaning of scriptural language. Rambam says that such a person becomes perplexed, because either he abandons his intellectual growth or he abandons his faith, and either path leaves him in pain and confusion. The Moreh is meant to relieve that tension.

Veiled Imagery, Limits of Explanation, and Secret Teaching

Rambam says the second purpose of the work is to explain hidden parables and imagery in the prophetic books that are not explicitly marked as metaphors. He says that the wise person who takes them only at face value becomes confused, but when their hidden meaning is explained, he finds the right path and escapes perplexity. He therefore calls the work Moreh Nevuchim.

He also says the book cannot solve every difficulty and cannot give exhaustive treatment of every issue. In keeping with the rabbinic rule that Ma'aseh Bereishit and Ma'aseh Merkavah are to be taught only as rashei perakim to a wise student, he intentionally writes in a scattered and indirect way so that the truth emerges only partially and not in a way that violates the divine concealment of such matters. He says that even in natural philosophy the deepest principles are not taught openly.

Light, Prophecy, and Human Understanding

Rambam compares access to truth and prophecy to flashes of lightning in the dark. He describes Moshe Rabbeinu as one for whom the light is constant, like perpetual illumination, while other prophets receive intermittent flashes and some receive only a single flash in their lives, like v-yitnabu v-lo yasafu. He says that many people only obtain brief moments of insight, and others remain entirely in darkness, never seeing any light at all.

The text ends this section by excluding the masses from the discussion, since the book is not meant for them. The teacher later summarizes that the work is for those who are already formed in Torah and philosophy and who are struggling with the tension between textual understanding and intellectual truth.

Yom Ha'atzmaut and Eretz Yisrael

The speaker ends with words for Yom Ha'atzmaut and marks seventy-eight years since 1948, presenting the modern State of Eretz Yisrael as a source of joy and spiritual significance. He explains the difference between Magdil and Migdol in Birkat Hamazon as a contrast between active human effort during the week and completed wholeness on Shabbat, and he connects that idea to the duty to continue bringing about yeshuah in the life of the Jewish people.

He cites Rav Kook, Rambam, and Rav Reines to emphasize the intrinsic holiness of Eretz Yisrael, the special status of its stones and dust, and the power of love for Tzion to preserve faith and identity. He presents the ongoing life and struggle of Am Yisrael in Eretz Yisrael as a living proof of Torah and Hakadosh Baruch Hu, and he closes with a prayer for peace, ingathering, and a complete Eretz Yisrael that will move from Magdil to Migdol.
