General Overview

Rabbi Avraham ben the Rambam’s Hamespik Le'ovdei Hashem distinguishes ordinary fulfillment of Torah obligations from the higher aspiration of chasidut, and presents character traits, trust in Hashem, and contentment as interconnected. After reviewing its first nine chapters and introducing two comments on creation from Rabbi Avraham’s Torah commentary, the class begins chapter 10 on perishut: refraining from unnecessary physical pursuits so that the soul’s intellectual and spiritual capacities can develop.

Review of Chapters One Through Nine

Chapter one distinguishes the tzadik, who fulfills the basic requirements of serving Hashem, from the chasid, who goes beyond them; aspiring to extra stringency while neglecting basic duties is unbalanced. Chapter two teaches that character traits are interconnected. Chapter three describes four levels of purity in action: fulfilling commandments under social pressure, seeking human approval while also caring about Hashem, serving Hashem for reward or out of fear, and serving because obedience is right and represents the best life.

Chapter four teaches that compassion is generally preferable when a person is unsure how to act, although circumstances can require its opposite. Chapter five defines generosity as giving beyond what is required and distinguishes generosity toward others from a person’s own habits of spending or saving. Generosity can involve money, wisdom, religious faith, and influence, should be directed toward appropriate recipients, and strengthens bitachon.

Chapter six presents calmness as control over anger rather than apathy: a person can express displeasure without giving way to anger. The chapter recommends reflecting on anger’s consequences, its connection to arrogance, examples from the Torah, and practices that cultivate patience; when unsure whether to express anger, restraint is preferable. Chapter seven defines humility as an accurate recognition of one’s standing, not denial of one’s abilities or self-degradation. It applies toward both Hashem and other people, and can be developed through modest conduct, reflection on Torah examples and commandments, and examination of the sources of pride.

Chapter eight explains bitachon as trust in Hashem’s governance of the world through its ordinary natural processes, while still making appropriate efforts. It classifies situations requiring trust in both physical and spiritual matters and describes four levels of bitachon, from trust accompanied by worry to the highest level, in which a person accepts whatever comes from Hashem as good. Chapter nine presents contentment as a safeguard against envy and the pursuit of unnecessary things, and encourages seeking what is needed rather than what is merely desired.

Rabbi Avraham’s Commentary on Creation

Rabbi Avraham explains the closing words of the creation account, that everything was “very good,” as a reminder that human beings cannot fully understand the purpose of everything in the universe. Some creations have evident benefits, while others seem obscure or even harmful; human understanding has limits, but the entire creation remains good and wise. The verse therefore affirms the value of creation even where its purpose is not yet understood.

Rabbi Avraham also interprets the description of God breathing a soul into Adam as an image of the soul’s subtlety. In the framework of matter and form, the body is matter and the intellect or soul is its form; the language of breathing conveys the form’s refined nature. This interpretation connects with the chapter’s teaching that the body’s capacities depend on the soul.

The Purpose of Abstinence

Chapter 10 introduces perishut, abstaining from what is unnecessary. Rabbi Avraham presents this as an aspiration of chasidut beyond simply fulfilling what the Torah permits. The teacher notes an apparent contrast with the Rambam’s opposition to certain forms of asceticism and says that the difference will be examined as the chapter continues.

The chapter begins by distinguishing the physical body from the soul’s intellectual form. The body alone cannot function, and a person’s connection with Hashem depends on the soul’s capacity to understand and apprehend. As physical preoccupations dominate a person’s life, they obscure that capacity and make spiritual development more difficult.

Material Pursuits and the Soul

Rabbi Avraham warns that pursuing wealth, possessions, honor, food, drink, and sexual pleasure can consume a person’s time and attention. Financial ambitions often have no clear endpoint; even someone who sets a target may spend years traveling, trading, and working to reach it. Such a person may remain preoccupied with past and future dealings during free hours and even in sleep.

After accumulating wealth, a person may either hoard it and worry about protecting it or spend it on luxuries that require further planning and upkeep. Both paths can consume life in anxiety and effort, and spending may lead to renewed work to replace what was used. Rabbi Avraham interprets the sages’ statement that the unlearned become more foolish with age as a consequence of a life increasingly absorbed in material concerns, which weakens the soul’s connection to its source.

Death, Spiritual Capacity, and the Sling

Rabbi Avraham says that after death the soul loses the physical pleasures it pursued and suffers like a person who once could see but becomes blind. It also lacks access to spiritual forms of perfection that it did not develop during life. He invokes the biblical image of an enemy’s soul being cast from a sling to describe this punishment.

The teacher contrasts several interpretations of the sling image. The Talmud describes the wicked as being cast from place to place, an idea later associated with Kabbalistic accounts of kaf hakela; Ralbag instead interprets the image as the soul’s cessation of existence. The teacher is uncertain whether Rabbi Avraham describes conscious suffering after karet or a low spiritual standing in the world to come, and notes that the passage may bear on Rabbi Avraham’s and possibly the Rambam’s understanding of the soul after death.

Elisha’s Rebuke of Gehazi

Rabbi Avraham uses Elisha’s rebuke of Gehazi, who pursues Naaman’s money, to show the inappropriateness of a prophet’s student devoting himself to acquiring material possessions. He interprets Elisha’s question about whether it is a time to take money and goods as a challenge to the pursuit itself: a person’s short life is not meant to be spent accumulating physical comforts. The chapter will continue with the definition and benefits of perishut in the next class.
