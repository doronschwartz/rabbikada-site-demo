General Overview

Rabbi Avraham’s commentary on Chayei Sarah presents Be’er Lachai Ro’i as a place where Yitzchak sought solitude and spiritual growth, and explains that he moved there only after Avraham’s death because he remained devoted to caring for his father. The lesson then returns to perishut (abstinence): it begins with recognizing that physical pleasures, possessions, and status do not define a person, but lasting change requires overcoming habit. Temporary restraints may help beginners, while the goal is an inward disposition of indifference to unnecessary comforts, developed gradually rather than through sudden deprivation.

Yitzchak’s Solitude at Be’er Lachai Ro’i

Rabbi Avraham explains that Yitzchak regularly traveled from his home in the Negev to Be’er Lachai Ro’i, despite the distance, to spend time in solitude and contemplation. The verse emphasizes that Yitzchak was returning from there when he met Rivkah, because Rabbi Avraham understands that Yitzchak would not have interrupted his spiritual practice had he been on his way there.

After Avraham’s death, Yitzchak settled at Be’er Lachai Ro’i because he found it especially conducive to serving Hashem. While Avraham was alive, Yitzchak continued to care for his father, traveling between him and the well; this reflects the importance of kibbud av va’em.

The Intellectual Basis of Perishut

Rabbi Avraham teaches that true perishut is a disposition of the heart, not simply a pattern of outward behavior. A wealthy person may possess perishut if possessions do not occupy the heart, while someone living simply may lack it if constantly preoccupied with what they cannot have.

The pleasures of food, drink, and physical relations cannot be life’s ultimate purpose, Rabbi Avraham argues, because people share them with animals. Clothing, luxurious homes, and authority are also not essential to a person’s identity: fine clothing often serves to impress others, nature can be more restorative than a palace, and political power can distract a person from tending to their own spiritual development. Shlomo’s account in Kohelet of pursuing every pleasure and acquisition reinforces the conclusion that these pursuits are ultimately vain.

Habit and the Levels of Perishut

Intellectual recognition alone does not overcome habits or human nature. Rabbi Avraham compares people’s attachments to familiar comforts with the embarrassment someone may feel when forced to abandon a customary lifestyle; repeated fasting, for example, can become easier over time as the body adjusts.

For beginners, external practices such as simple clothing, plain food, fasting, or temporary separation from familiar surroundings may help weaken habitual attachment. These practices are training, not the final goal. The more developed person, like the prophets and other exemplary figures Rabbi Avraham cites, has made perishut second nature and is not troubled by whether the food, clothing, or shelter is luxurious or plain.

Rabbi Avraham also points to the Torah’s connection between affliction and atonement on Yom Kippur, and to the example of Yonadav ben Rechav’s descendants, who followed his instructions to live in tents and abstain from wine. Such examples show the value of abstinence, while the ultimate aim remains an inward freedom from dependence on unnecessary comforts.

Gradual Practical Training

Before changing habits, a person should assess how strongly they are attached to worldly pleasures and consider their established routines. Rabbi Avraham warns that habits rooted in human nature cannot be changed suddenly; rapid efforts may fail or cause inner and outer confusion.

He illustrates gradual change with food: someone accustomed to several dishes should first reduce the number by one, maintain that change until it becomes natural, and then reduce further. Only after simplifying the number of dishes should the person move toward plainer food and, later, smaller quantities, while taking health into account. The goal is to progress steadily until eating is governed by bodily need rather than craving.
