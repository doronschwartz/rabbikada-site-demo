General Overview

Rabbi Avraham’s interpretations of the weekly Torah portion present Yaakov’s wrestling as a prophetic vision enabled by solitude, explain his family’s ordering and his words to Esav, distinguish two places called Sukkot, and offer readings of the names Yaakov, Israel, and Binyamin. The lesson then turns to perishut (abstinence), explaining how solitude supports spiritual focus, how inner detachment can be measured, and what spiritual and practical benefits it brings.

Yaakov’s Wrestling Vision

Rabbi Avraham, following the Rambam’s account in the Moreh, understands Yaakov’s wrestling with the man as a prophetic vision rather than a physical encounter. He explains that Yaakov reached this vision through hitbodedut, or solitude: his physical separation from his family enabled a deeper spiritual seclusion. In response to the Ramban’s question about why Yaakov limps afterward, Rabbi Avraham argues that imagination can affect the body, just as a vivid dream can leave someone feeling pain or exhaustion. The limp is therefore an aftereffect of the prophecy, and the Torah’s account explains why the sciatic nerve is forbidden.

Yaakov’s Family Before Esav

Rabbi Avraham rejects the common explanation that Yaakov placed the maidservants and their children first, Leah and her children next, and Rachel and Yosef last simply to protect his favorites. He says the order follows the conventions of a procession, in which the less prominent come first and the most honored appear last. Yaakov presents his family to Esav in that order, with Rachel and Yosef at the end as the most prominent.

When Esav asks about the women and children, Yaakov answers only about the children. Rabbi Avraham explains that this is modest: Yaakov avoids discussing his wives without need, and identifying the children as his own allows Esav to infer that the women are their mothers. Yaakov calls the children gifts God has graciously granted him because, as Rabbi Avraham explains, the word conveys an undeserved gift.

The Two Places Called Sukkot

Rabbi Avraham disagrees with his grandfather’s view that the Sukkot where Yaakov settles is the same place as the Sukkot where the Israelites camp after leaving Egypt. He identifies Yaakov’s Sukkot as a place in or near the land of Israel, distinct from the place near Egypt. Both locations bear the same name.

Yaakov and Israel

Rabbi Avraham explains that although the Torah says Yaakov will no longer be called Yaakov but Israel, it continues to use both names, and calls him Yaakov more often. He therefore reads the change to mean that Yaakov will no longer be Yaakov alone; Israel is also his name. By contrast, the Torah stops calling Avraham and Sarah by their former names after their names change, which is why, as the speaker explains, the tradition treats those changes differently.

The speaker connects the two names to different ways of meeting life, drawing on an idea he attributes to Rabbi Sacks: Yaakov represents caution and adaptability, while Israel represents strength and assertiveness. He suggests that a person needs the judgment to know when to act in each way, rather than relying exclusively on either approach.

Binyamin’s Name

Rabbi Avraham offers a different explanation from his grandfather for the name Binyamin, which he understands as “son of days.” His grandfather interprets it as a reference to Yaakov’s limited remaining years in which to enjoy his youngest son. Rabbi Avraham instead says Yaakov names him this way because, with Rachel dying in childbirth, the passage of time and life’s experiences will raise the child. In the question period, a participant raises the familiar interpretation “son of my right hand,” and the speaker acknowledges that other explanations also appear in traditional sources.

Solitude and Prophetic Life

The lesson returns to perishut and the difficulty of pursuing it while responsible for many relationships. Rabbi Avraham points to Eliyahu’s calling of Elisha: Eliyahu places his cloak on Elisha as a sign that he will follow his prophetic path. Although Elisha asks to say goodbye to his parents, he then leaves his former work and follows Eliyahu. Rabbi Avraham also observes that the cloak-passing practice, once used among the prophets, is no longer practiced in Jewish communities but survives among Sufi Muslims.

The example illustrates that close relationships and obligations can make solitude and spiritual focus more difficult, though the speaker stresses that the principle is to seek balance rather than to imitate Elisha uncritically. The aim of perishut is to make room for a direct relationship with God.

Measuring Perishut

Rabbi Avraham says that time spent practicing perishut and the effort invested do not prove that it has become genuine. Its measure is a person’s inner response to gaining or losing material possessions: the less a possession, especially a valuable one, unsettles or excites the heart, the greater the person’s abstinence. He presents this as a matter of degree, not an all-or-nothing state.

Yaakov’s willingness to leave the wealth associated with his family illustrates this detachment, because he is pursuing a spiritual purpose. Rabbi Avraham also interprets Yaakov’s grief over Yosef as largely spiritual, rooted in the loss of divine inspiration rather than only in physical separation. Elisha’s refusal of wealth and reluctance to receive kings provide further examples. The speaker compares Rabbi Avraham’s point about purpose making it easier to relinquish possessions to the idea commonly attributed to Nietzsche that having a “why” makes it possible to bear hardship.

The Benefits of Perishut

Rabbi Avraham says perishut protects a person from many transgressions, including theft, dishonesty in business, greed, jealousy, and excessive appetite. It also reduces unnecessary social conversation and helps prevent worldly concerns from obstructing the fulfillment of positive commandments. Its spiritual benefits include reverence and love of God, purity, closeness to God, peace of mind, and joy.

It also brings practical benefits by reducing worry, envy, hostility, and disputes over possessions. Rabbi Avraham invokes Solomon’s preference for a dry piece of bread with peace over a house full of feasting and conflict, as well as the teaching in the Ethics of the Fathers that more possessions bring more worry. The lesson closes with the announcement that the next session will address questions about Torah passages that seem to conflict with perishut, followed by a break for Chanukah.
