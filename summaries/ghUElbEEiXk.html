General Overview

The lesson concludes the laws of grinding on Shabbos and introduces kneading, explaining the main permissions and disputes that govern both activities. It presents practical applications involving food preparation, household tools, medicine, noise, and honey, then defines kneading and outlines disagreements about when it begins and how the thickness of a mixture affects its status.

Review of Pickling and Grinding

Pickling foods is a rabbinic prohibition, explained by Rashi as resembling tanning because it changes the food’s texture, and by the Rambam as a rabbinic extension of cooking. Grinding, or tochen, includes crushing, mashing, and cutting food into very small pieces. The lesson reviews three permissions: performing the action in a major deviation from the usual method, relying on the principle שאין טוחן אחר טוחן when food has already been ground, and grinding certain cooked animal foods.

Grinding Food and Using Kitchen Tools

Raw meat or fish may not be cut into very small pieces when the cutting makes them edible. Cooked meat and chicken may be cut finely under the permissions discussed, while cheese, eggs, and similar foods may be mashed because they are already edible and are not considered products of the ground. However, a tool specifically designed for grinding, such as a grater or masher, may not be used; tools that only slice, such as an egg slicer, may be used.

Grinding for Immediate Consumption

The Rashba extends the immediate-consumption permission known from borrer to grinding: food may be finely cut or mashed shortly before it is eaten. The Ben Ish Chai does not rely on this permission, while Chacham Ovadia rules that it is permitted me’ikar hadin, though cutting somewhat larger pieces is preferable when practical. This approach permits examples such as mashing a banana for a baby or preparing avocado for a meal that is about to be eaten.

Partially Ground Foods and Spreading

Food that was substantially crushed before Shabbos may have its grinding completed on Shabbos when only a small amount of work remains, as with already-broken-down potatoes. The Rema also permits spreading foods, such as avocado or chocolate spread, because the prohibition of smoothing does not apply to food. Watering detached vegetables to keep them from withering is permitted for food purposes, despite a separate rabbinic restriction on certain water-related tasks.

Practical Applications and Related Restrictions

Salads should be prepared close to the meal when fine cutting raises concerns of grinding or borrer, and the lesson advises avoiding pieces that are too small unless preparing them immediately before eating. It also reviews salt and oil in salads in light of the laws of pickling. Dry mud may be removed from a person’s body because of human dignity, but removing it from clothing is different; in some circumstances, a non-Jew may be asked to remove the mud without using water.

Rabbinic Restrictions Connected to Grinding

The lesson connects a rabbinic restriction on noisy activity to the concern that people might think work began on Shabbos. Ashkenazi authorities generally restrict leaving noisy processes to operate, while Sephardi practice is described as more lenient; Rav Moshe Feinstein permits an alarm clock because setting it in advance is its ordinary purpose. The prohibition on taking medicine is also linked to grinding, since medicines were traditionally prepared by grinding plants; the broader rules are reserved for a later discussion.

Honeycomb

Removing honey directly from a honeycomb is prohibited rabbinically because it resembles harvesting, even though honey does not grow from the comb. Honey that is already outside the comb may be taken.

Introducing Kneading

Kneading, lash, is the tenth melachah in the sequence described: after grinding the seeds used for dye, they are mixed with liquid to form a paste. The activity applies to non-food materials as well as food, and generally involves using a liquid to bring previously separate particles together into a unified mass.

When Kneading Begins

Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi holds that adding the liquid and beginning the mixture already constitutes kneading, while Rabbi Yosi bar Rabbi Yehuda holds that kneading begins only with the later act of combining the ingredients. The Rambam, Rif, and most Rishonim follow Rabbi Yosi bar Rabbi Yehuda, and the Shulchan Aruch presents that as its primary ruling; the Rema rules stringently, and Sephardi authorities differ. Chacham Ovadia holds that the primary ruling does not prohibit the initial mixing alone, but the lesson emphasizes that an immediately binding mixture can constitute kneading as soon as its ingredients are combined, as with sesame seeds in water or a small amount of milk absorbed into Weetabix.

Mixture Thickness

The resulting mixture’s consistency determines the severity of kneading: a thick mixture that barely moves is a Torah-level prohibition, while a looser but still cohesive mixture is prohibited rabbinically. A very runny mixture is permitted under the principles presented. The lesson gives dough, cake batter, and hot chocolate as examples of these different consistencies.
