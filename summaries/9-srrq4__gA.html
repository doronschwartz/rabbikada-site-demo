General Overview

The lesson reviews how the Rambam interprets descriptions of divine movement and presence as metaphorical rather than physical, and emphasizes the value of regularly reviewing these ideas. It explains savav (“surrounding”) through the protective reminders of mitzvot, presents Onkelos’s approach to anthropomorphic language, and introduces three meanings of regel: foot, follower, and cause or basis.

The Meaning of Divine Dwelling and Movement

Chapter 25 explains that shachen can mean living in a place or remaining continuously present. When applied to Hashem, it refers not to physical occupation but to the continuing presence of the Shechinah or hashgachah; this is the significance of the Mishkan and Beis Hamikdash. Chapter 26 states that descriptions such as standing, sitting, going, and dwelling are metaphorical, because physical movement would imply a body. The Torah uses these expressions because דברה תורה בלשון בני אדם, “the Torah speaks in the language of human beings.”

Reviewing the Ten Verbs of Movement

The Rambam’s ten verbs convey different aspects of divine action without implying physical movement. Yarad can refer to the resting of the Shechinah, the imparting of prophecy, or punishment; alah can signal the conclusion of prophecy. Halakh can mean the withdrawal of hashgachah or the spreading of an effect, while nitzav and amad convey permanence and endurance, and yashav conveys stability.

Shachen means the continued presence of the Shechinah or hashgachah. Yatsa means that God’s words or will become manifest, ba means that a promise is fulfilled, and avar means diverting attention. The lesson stresses that these meanings require regular review because each idea builds on those that came before it.

The Meaning of Savav and the Protective Role of Mitzvot

The Rambam does not explain savav, “to surround,” in a separate chapter. In the Mishneh Torah, he explains that tefillin, tzitzit, and mezuzah serve as repeated reminders of God’s unity and a person’s purpose, helping to protect a person from sin. These mitzvot are described as the angels that surround and rescue those who fear God. The lesson also accepts interpretations involving the Shechinah or hashgachah, provided that “surrounding” does not mean that God is physically encircling people.

The mezuzah is meant to prompt reflection whenever a person enters or leaves, rather than merely to be touched or kissed. The teacher encourages stopping to look at it and remember the relationship with Hashem that it represents.

Onkelos and Anthropomorphic Language

Onkelos, a Roman convert from an aristocratic background who lived around the end of the first and beginning of the second century, translated the Torah into Aramaic. The Rambam presents him as an expert in language and as careful to remove corporeal descriptions of God. Onkelos generally renders divine movement as a revelation of created light, the Shechinah, or hashgachah: for example, “God came down” may be rendered as “God revealed Himself.”

The Rambam explains that Onkelos translates God’s words to Jacob about going down to Egypt literally because the episode occurs in a night vision; no physical descent takes place. He offers another possible explanation: the speaker may be an angel speaking as God’s messenger, as in the account where an angel tells Jacob, “I am the God of Bethel.” The Rambam notes that angels can be called Elohim and that a messenger may speak in God’s name. Both explanations preserve Onkelos’s central aim of preventing readers from imagining God as corporeal.

The Three Meanings of Regel

Chapter 28 introduces regel, which can mean a literal foot or leg, a follower, or a basis and cause. In Jacob’s words to Laban, “God blessed you leregli,” the term means that God blessed Laban because of Jacob. The lesson attributes to Dayan Ehrentreu the distinction that regel expresses a direct, necessary cause, while biglal can indicate a possible cause.

The Rambam’s discussion will next apply these meanings to the verse describing what Moshe, Aharon, and the elders saw beneath God’s feet. The question of why the Torah says “three regalim” in the account of Balaam’s donkey remains for further consideration.
