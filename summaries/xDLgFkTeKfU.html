General Overview

The Monday-night study of Hamaspik Le'ovdei Hashem continues in chapter 10 on perishut, or abstinence, alongside weekly insights from Rabbi Avraham ben the Rambam’s Torah commentary. Rabbi Avraham’s interpretations of Parashat Vayeira address Sarah’s concern about Yishmael’s influence on Yitzchak, Hagar’s fear that Yishmael may die despite the divine promise, the meaning of “where he is,” and the ambiguity of “at that time.” The chapter presents perishut as an important path to love of Hashem and defines it as an inward freedom from attachment to worldly things, rather than simply living with little.

Rabbi Avraham’s Commentary on Parashat Vayeira

Rabbi Avraham interprets Sarah’s seeing Yishmael metzachek as more than ordinary play: he says Yishmael also uses inappropriate speech and behaves like a boisterous child. Sarah fears that Yitzchak will imitate him and spend time away from developing the perfection expected of him. Rabbi Avraham also presents Sarah as feeling personally responsible for Yitzchak’s development, which helps explain her insistence that Yishmael leave.

Hagar’s fear for Yishmael does not mean that she doubts the promise that he will become a nation. Rabbi Avraham explains that a promise may be conditional, and that sin may cause it not to be fulfilled—the possibility expressed by shema yigrom hachet. The discussion raises a parallel with the Akedah: Avraham proceeds despite the promise concerning Yitzchak, and the question of how his test compares with Hagar’s concern remains open.

Rabbi Avraham reads ba’asher hu sham, “where he is,” geographically in its straightforward sense: Hashem hears Yishmael where he has been left under a bush, rather than judging him by his present spiritual level. He distinguishes vayehi ba’et hahi, “it was at that time,” as an ambiguous phrase that may refer to the preceding events or to what follows. By contrast, achar hadevarim ha’eleh, “after these things,” clearly places the events after the story already recounted. Rabbi Avraham attributes this principle to his father, the Rambam, and cites the differing dates in Devarim for Aaron’s death and the separation of the Levites.

The Importance of Perishut

A person consists of chomer, matter, and tzurah, form or consciousness. Rabbi Avraham’s teaching, as presented in the lesson, is that excessive pursuit of material concerns weakens a person’s spiritual capacity and limits the ability to enjoy olam haba. Perishut allows a person to attend to spiritual development because the pursuit of necessities leaves time and energy for reflection and service of Hashem. Yaakov’s request for bread to eat and clothing to wear illustrates reliance on what is necessary rather than a demand for luxury.

Rabbi Avraham points to prophets and pious people who chose shepherding and farming because those occupations could leave time for spiritual work and, in some cases, solitude. The lesson cites simple living among figures such as Shmuel, Achiyah, Eliyahu, Elisha, and Abba Chilkiyah, including their modest food, clothing, and homes. The Torah’s commandments also cultivate restraint, and Rabbi Avraham treats the consecration of spoils and the punishment of Achan as evidence of the value placed on abstention. He further reads the comparison between turning to other gods and loving worldly indulgences as a warning that attachment to physical pleasure can distance a person from Hashem.

The Definition of Perishut

Rabbi Avraham defines perishut as an inner condition: the heart is detached from love of worldly things and devoted instead to love of Hashem and His service. It is not measured simply by fasting, wearing coarse clothes, eating plain food, or living in solitude. A person who thinks constantly about what he is denying himself has not necessarily achieved perishut; genuine abstinence comes when spiritual aims occupy the heart and worldly attractions no longer dominate it.

A person may possess wealth, enjoy fine clothing and food, or live comfortably and still be a parush if those things came without being the focus of his pursuit. Rabbi Avraham cites Yitzchak’s prosperity, Yaakov’s wealth, and Avraham’s riches as examples. Avraham’s refusal to take even a shoelace from the king of Sedom demonstrates his lack of attachment to wealth. Rabbi Avraham also presents Avraham’s limited interest in marriage and physical beauty as evidence that worldly attractions did not govern him.

Conversely, poverty and an outwardly austere lifestyle do not by themselves constitute perishut. Someone may eat little or dress simply only because there is no alternative, or may practice external austerities while inwardly longing for worldly pleasures. The central test is whether the heart remains preoccupied with those pleasures or is directed toward spiritual growth. The lesson applies this distinction by urging people to consider whether learning and understanding Torah brings greater excitement than material gain.
