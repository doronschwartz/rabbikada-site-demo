General Overview

The lesson explains the principles of lash, the Shabbat prohibition of binding particles into a unified mixture, and applies them to practical food preparation. It emphasizes disagreements among the Talmudic sages, later authorities, and Sephardi and Ashkenazi rulings, especially regarding when ingredients may be combined and how mixtures may be stirred.

Definition and Foundational Dispute

Lash involves binding separate particles into one unified mass and applies to food and non-food substances, such as flour and water or sand and water. The central dispute is whether adding the ingredients itself is prohibited or whether the prohibition begins only when they are kneaded together. The Rambam, Rif, and Rosh follow Rabbi Yosei bar Rabbi Yehudah, while other authorities follow Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi and prohibit adding the ingredients as a Torah-level violation. The Shulchan Aruch’s primary ruling follows the lenient view, while the Rema rules stringently; the speaker says Sephardim generally follow the Shulchan Aruch’s primary ruling, though some are stringent.

Types of Mixtures and Automatic Binding

A thick mixture, blilah avah, is a Torah-level prohibition to make, while a thinner mixture, blilah rakah, is rabbinically prohibited; a mixture that does not form a unified mass is permitted. The prohibition requires a unified mixture, so placing chocolate spread between bread slices is not lash. Even according to the lenient view, adding ingredients is prohibited when they bind together automatically, as with flaxseeds and water; the speaker gives Weetabix and a small amount of milk as another example.

Materials That Do Not Form a Unified Mass

The Talmud distinguishes materials that form a fully unified mass, bar gibul, from those that do not, lo bar gibul, such as sand or ash mixed with water. Abaye holds that, according to Rabbi Yosei, adding water to a lo bar gibul substance is itself a Torah-level prohibition because further kneading makes little difference. Rav Yosef holds that actual mixing is still required; the Rambam rules that mixing such materials is only rabbinically prohibited, while most other early authorities consider it a Torah-level prohibition. The Mishnah Berurah follows Abaye, whereas the Chazon Ish and Chacham Ovadia follow Rav Yosef.

Crosswise Mixing

The permitted method called shtei va’erev uses crosswise strokes rather than the usual circular motion, creating a significant change in the way the mixture is made. Most authorities apply this method broadly, although the Rambam limits its use for certain Torah-level mixtures. This method can address the prohibition of kneading, but it does not resolve the separate prohibition—followed by Ashkenazim—against initially combining ingredients.

Sephardi and Ashkenazi Practice

Under the Shulchan Aruch’s primary ruling, Sephardim may generally combine ingredients in either order and make even a thick mixture, provided they mix it with shtei va’erev. Ashkenazim generally avoid making thick mixtures on Shabbat; for a thin mixture, they reverse the usual order of adding ingredients and use enough liquid to keep the result loose. In exceptional circumstances, Ashkenazim may make a thick mixture if the ingredients were combined before Shabbat, a baby needs that specific food, a non-Jew combined the ingredients, or the food’s taste or freshness would deteriorate if prepared beforehand.

Bran and the Rulings in the Shulchan Aruch

The Shulchan Aruch permits adding water to bran for animals and mixing it with a utensil using crosswise strokes, or transferring it between vessels, while prohibiting kneading it by hand. A second opinion, associated with the Baal HaTerumah, prohibits adding water on Shabbat and permits crosswise mixing only when the water was added beforehand. The Biur Halacha explains that this reflects the dispute over whether adding ingredients is itself prohibited, and notes the further disagreement over whether mixing lo bar gibul substances is a Torah or rabbinic prohibition.

Practical Food Examples

Making techina from ground sesame seeds and water is prohibited because the ingredients bind into a unified mass automatically. Adding water to ready-made, edible techina to thin it is generally permitted; if the techina is very thick and the added water must be worked in, it should be mixed crosswise, a method that applies to both Sephardim and Ashkenazim. Small pieces of garlic or chometz can generally be mixed into techina without a change, but very finely ground garlic may require crosswise mixing.

For aubergine with oil, Sephardim may make a thick mixture using crosswise strokes, while Ashkenazim generally need to make it before Shabbat or rely on an exceptional circumstance; for a thin mixture, Ashkenazim reverse the order of ingredients and crosswise mixing is praiseworthy. Thick mayonnaise does not begin binding with the other ingredients until they are mixed, so it can be combined first and then stirred crosswise. Fenugreek paste cannot be made by adding water to the seeds on Shabbat because it binds and expands immediately.

Ordinary lettuce salads can be dressed normally because the pieces do not form a unified mass. Very finely cut or mashed ingredients, such as potatoes, avocado, eggs, liver, or tuna mixed with oil or mayonnaise, may form a thick mixture and require the applicable rules; Ashkenazim generally prepare thick versions before Shabbat, unless an exception applies. Lemon and avocado should generally be made loose enough to form a thin mixture, while cornflakes and similar cereals can usually be mixed with milk unless a large quantity of small, crushed flakes binds immediately; Weetabix and porridge require ample liquid and crosswise mixing.
