General Overview

Rabbi Avraham’s interpretations of Toldot and Vayetzei connect the patriarchs’ choices to perishut, or inner detachment from physical pleasures. He teaches that external practices such as fasting and simple living can help cultivate this quality, but they have no value on their own and must be pursued gradually, with care for health, humility, and personal obligations. He also explains that family and communal responsibilities can make this path difficult, so spiritual development must be balanced with the duties one owes others.

Simple Food and Spiritual Priorities in Toldot

Rabbi Avraham rejects Ibn Ezra’s suggestion that Yaakov cooked lentils because his family had become poor. He explains that Yaakov chose simple foods as an expression of perishut, limiting meat and rich dishes despite his family’s wealth. Rabbi Avraham also reads Yitzchak’s naming of the well Esek as a sign that dealing with the dispute over it was an unusual distraction from the patriarchs’ usual focus on spiritual matters.

The Meaning of “Pegiah” in Vayetzei

Rabbi Avraham explains that pegiah means reaching or encountering. Prayer is called pegiah because it is an encounter with the divine, while the same word can refer to killing because an attacker must approach the person.

Yaakov’s Vow and Tithing

Yaakov asks for only bread to eat and clothes to wear, then vows to give a tithe of whatever God provides. Rabbi Avraham connects this to the principle that prayer should seek essentials rather than luxuries, and adds that a person should give ma’aser from whatever they receive, however little. He also praises a Midrash that counts Ephraim and Menashe among Yaakov’s children, excludes four firstborns, and identifies Levi as the tithe from the remaining ten.

Yaakov’s Deliberate Actions

The phrase describing Yaakov as lifting his feet indicates that he travels on foot, rather than riding an animal. Rabbi Avraham also reads it as showing that Yaakov’s movement is directed by conscious will: the mind should decide and guide the body, rather than actions happening automatically and being justified afterward.

Yaakov’s Love for Rachel

Rabbi Avraham argues that the seven years Yaakov works for Rachel seem short because his love is not merely sensual. If his desire were focused on physical pleasure, waiting would feel especially long; instead, his attention is fixed on the meaningful union he is preparing for.

The Name Dinah

Rabbi Avraham gives two explanations for Leah naming her daughter Dinah. The name may reflect the judgment associated with the birth of a girl in a society that prizes sons, or it may anticipate the suffering and divine judgment connected to the later episode with Shechem.

Lavan’s Pursuit of Yaakov

Rabbi Avraham, following his grandfather Rabbi Maimon, distinguishes radifah, general pursuit, from delikah, pursuit driven by anger. Yaakov’s use of delikah conveys the hostility and fury with which Lavan pursued him.

Yaakov’s Oath and Idolatry

Lavan invokes both the God of Avraham and the god of Nachor, combining the true God with idolatry. Yaakov avoids repeating Lavan’s wording and swears by the “Fear of his father Yitzchak,” distancing himself from any suggestion that he accepts Lavan’s religious beliefs.

Fasting as Preparation for Perishut

Rabbi Avraham presents fasting as a possible preparation for perishut, not as a goal in itself. He cautions against fasting in harmful heat, overeating before or after a fast, or spending the day preoccupied with food; instead, fasting should accompany Torah study, prayer, reflection, and attention to what one reads. These practices can help cultivate inner detachment when pursued carefully, but fasting can also harm the body, unsettle the emotions, or feed spiritual arrogance.

Gradual Change and the Rambam’s Golden Mean

Rabbi Avraham applies the same cautions to other forms of restraint, including simpler clothing, reduced sensual indulgence, and more modest housing. Changes should be gradual and should not damage a person physically, emotionally, or spiritually. This accords with the Rambam’s teaching in the Eight Chapters: extreme practices are not inherently virtuous, but may serve temporarily as a means of correcting one’s character and developing inner perishut.

Responsibilities to Community and Family

Large communal roles and extensive family responsibilities can make perishut difficult by consuming time and attention. Rabbi Avraham advises those seeking this path to avoid leadership where possible, while recognizing that figures such as David had developed spiritual discipline before taking on public duties and received divine assistance. A person should limit unnecessary social ties but must still meet obligations to a spouse, children, parents, and others in their care; neglecting those duties for ascetic aims is a greater wrong than the spiritual benefit such withdrawal might bring.

Spiritual Preparation Before Marriage

Rabbi Avraham says that a person should cultivate spiritual discipline before marriage, since family responsibilities can limit the time available for religious pursuits. He cites the Sages’ concern that marriage brings substantial responsibilities and notes that Yaakov married at an advanced age, while Eliyahu and Elisha did not marry. This is not an excuse to disregard other religious obligations; the choice must be weighed with care.
