General Overview

Rambam explains the meanings of avar and ba and applies them to passages about God’s presence and speech. He offers four readings of God passing before Moshe, while insisting that the verse cannot describe a physical movement by God, who has no body. He also explains how ba can describe God’s presence resting among people or the fulfillment of prophecy.

The Five Meanings of Avar

Rambam identifies five meanings of avar: physically passing, proclaiming through a voice, experiencing a prophetic vision, overreaching a boundary, and diverting or turning away from one thing to another. These meanings provide different ways to understand the verse about God passing before Moshe and calling out.

Four Interpretations of God Passing Before Moshe

Rambam’s preferred interpretation takes avar to mean “divert.” Moshe asks to understand God intimately, but God tells him that he cannot see God’s face and can only apprehend God’s “back”—the way God acts in the world, expressed through the thirteen attributes of mercy.

On the straightforward reading of Onkelos, a created manifestation of God’s glory or Shechinah physically passes before Moshe, after which God calls out the attributes. A second reading of Onkelos understands the passing as a prophetic vision experienced in Moshe’s mind rather than as a physical event.

Rambam’s fourth interpretation follows Onkelos’s method of supplying words omitted from the verse, but adds “voice” rather than “glory” or Shechinah. The voice of God passes before Moshe and calls out, making the repeated “Hashem” a doubled expression, as in God calling “Moshe, Moshe.” Rambam supports the idea of a voice calling with other biblical examples in which a voice speaks or calls.

Rambam’s Conclusion on the Four Readings

Rambam allows readers to choose among the four interpretations, which range from an entirely intellectual prophetic experience to a created physical manifestation. Rav Makbili sees the interpretations as a progression from Rambam’s most intellectual reading to the most physical, with the prophetic vision and voice readings between them.

Rambam’s essential point is that God cannot literally pass before Moshe, because God has no body and is not subject to movement. The “face” and “back” describe the limits of human understanding, not physical features of God.

The Passage and Its Interpretive Questions

The four readings also shape the interpretation of the surrounding verses. The covering of Moshe protects him during an encounter with God’s glory, prophetic vision, or voice; in Rambam’s preferred reading, it protects him from intellectual danger as he is redirected from seeking God’s essence to understanding God’s actions.

The meaning of “face” depends on the interpretation: in Rambam’s preferred reading it is God’s face, while in the other readings it is Moshe’s face. The first three interpretations read the verse as saying that God passed and then called; the fourth makes the voice pass and call. The ta’amim appear to fit the fourth reading more naturally, though Ibn Ezra raises the difficulty that they do not fit the other readings as neatly.

Counting the Thirteen Attributes

The counting of the thirteen attributes is subject to disagreement. The Ben Ish Chai, following the Ari z”l, counts the two occurrences of “Hashem” as separate attributes, while Chacham Ovadia advises against counting on one’s fingers because of the disagreement among the Rishonim.

In the readings presented by Rambam and Onkelos, the first “Hashem” belongs to the verse’s introductory wording rather than to the attributes themselves. The lecturer therefore recommends reciting the attributes without assigning them numbered positions.

Other Uses of Avar

Rabbi Sacks explains aveirah, meaning transgression, through the sense of overreaching: a person goes beyond the proper boundaries. The saying of the Sages that one who overlooks others’ faults is forgiven for his own can also be understood through avar as either setting aside one’s middot or diverting from strict judgment.

Clarifying the Protective Covering

In response to a question, the lecturer explains that Moshe needs protection throughout the process of being redirected from seeking God’s face to understanding God’s back. Once that process is complete, the protection can be removed, and Moshe can attend to God’s attributes and actions without seeking to comprehend God’s essence.

The Meanings of Ba

Rambam gives ba, “come,” several uses. It can mean arriving at a place or entering a defined place; Onkelos distinguishes the two with different Aramaic terms. It can also mean that something happens, including the occurrence of what is actually a privation, such as evil or darkness, which Rambam regards as a lack rather than a created entity.

When ba refers to God, it does not describe physical travel. It means either that God’s Shechinah rests in a place, as in the revelation at Sinai or the future Temple, or that God’s promises are fulfilled through the prophets.

The Connection to the Previous Chapter

Rav Makbili connects chapter 22 to the preceding chapter because the word ba appears later in the same biblical passage about Moshe. The lecturer also suggests that the chapter’s examples show that Rambam, like Onkelos and Saadia Gaon, sometimes supplies words needed to make an interpretation clear.

The Torah’s use of bi’ah for sexual relations refers to physically entering, corresponding to the sense of ba as entering a defined place. The next chapters address yetzi’ah, another word for going, halikhah, and Shechinah.
