General Overview

Rabbi Avraham defines humility as a true recognition of a person’s status without exaggeration, whether before God or before other people, and he rejects self-degradation as a form of humility. He presents three paths to humility: practical training through association and conduct, meditation on the Torah and its mitzvot, and intellectual analysis that shows arrogance makes no sense in relation to bodily pleasures, power and wealth, or even wisdom and prophecy. He then extends the same argument to religious arrogance, saying that even the righteous must remember the full burden of the mitzvot, the ease with which sin and imperfection enter human life, and the greater humility required of those who draw near to Hashem.

Intellectual Analysis of Humility

Rabbi Avraham says the prophets and intellectuals remain humble because they long to rise toward perfection and closeness to Hashem, while recognizing that material existence prevents human beings from fully attaining their goal. He explains that the greater a person’s wisdom or spiritual attainment, the more the person sees how easily such levels are lost and how little human beings know compared with what remains above them. He illustrates this with the humility of Rabbi Akiva, who answered a rude challenger by saying that he wished even the shepherd’s wisdom could be his, and with the general principle that true knowledge produces humility rather than pride.

Comparison with Angels, Celestial Bodies, and Divine Perfection

Rabbi Avraham argues that the angels are the highest created beings and that the celestial bodies are also above human beings in degree, so a person who compares himself upward is led to humility. He says that human perfection is small beside the perfection of the celestial bodies, and both are far below the angels, while even the prophets and the world to come are reached only through higher intermediaries. He concludes that if a person compares himself to the complete perfection of Hashem, the result is extreme humility, as reflected in verses from Tehillim, Iyov, and the words recited in Ne’ilah that the mighty, the servants of Hashem, the wise, and the understanding are as nothing before Him.

Religious Arrogance and the Limits of Human Merit

Rabbi Avraham warns that righteous people also fall into arrogance when they become proud of their observance and think they have already reached the required level. He says that the Torah obligates a person to all the mitzvot, that one cannot cancel failures with good deeds, and that even the smallest lapses matter, as shown by David’s fear over his sins and by Kohelet’s statement that there is no righteous person on earth who does good and does not sin. He explains that a person should remember failures only when pride arises, not in order to become broken or despairing, and he insists that the purpose is to prevent self-exaltation, not to deny the real good a person has achieved.

Humility Before Hashem

Rabbi Avraham says that humility before Hashem is the duty of every creature with knowledge, and he cites the angels, the celestial bodies, the prophets, Avraham, David, Iyov, and Moshe as models of submission and self-effacement before God. He explains that the king of Israel must be especially humble in prayer so that all people will learn humility from him, and he connects this to the bowing required in Tefillah and to Shlomo’s posture in prayer. He ends by citing David’s willingness to humble himself before Hashem and by pointing ahead to the next part of the chapter, which will deal with the limits of humility, humility toward other people, and the places where a person must not act humbly.
