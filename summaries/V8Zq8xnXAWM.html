General Overview

The text continues the laws of Borer in Hilchos Shabbos and then begins Se'if Daled and Se'if Heh with several practical principles. It states that on Shabbos one must take the ochel from the pesolet, not the reverse, even when taking out the waste is easier, and it distinguishes Shabbos from Yom Tov, where the less burdensome option is preferred. It also presents major exceptions and debates: selecting immediately before eating, removing a fly or other object from liquid, taking out a whole mixture together, and the leniencies for bones in fish or chicken when they are part of a natural unit. It then begins the opening laws of Se'if Chet, where soaking or rubbing certain seeds to remove waste is forbidden, but placing them in a sieve for their usual use is permitted, and it explains that some forms of inevitable separation are not treated as Borer when the act is a natural one.

Se'if Daled: Taking Waste from Food

The Shulchan Aruch states that one who separates pesolet from ochel is liable even with one hand. This means that taking shells out of pistachios is forbidden, because the shells are pesolet and the nuts are ochel. The Rama adds that this is true even when there is more food than waste and it is easier to remove the waste; on Shabbos quantity and convenience do not change the rule, unlike Yom Tov, where less tircha is preferred.

The text then records a dispute about whether removing waste immediately before eating counts as derech achila. Rabbi Yitzchak Abulafia permits taking out waste at the last moment before putting the food in the mouth, while Rabbi Yom Tov Tzahalon forbids it. The Bi'ur Halacha and Birkei Yosef accept the stricter view and conclude that one should not remove the waste even immediately before eating unless other leniencies apply.

Solid and Liquid Mixtures

A second principle is that there is no Borer with solids and liquids according to the lenient view brought from the Maharitatz. A fly in soup or tea may be removed, because a single visible object in a liquid is treated as clearly discernible rather than as a true ta'aroves. The Taz disagrees and treats it as a mixture, but the accepted Sephardi position is lenient. The text explains that even according to the stricter approach, one may remove the fly together with a little liquid around it, because that is removing the whole local mixture rather than separating waste from food.

Bones in Fish and Chicken

The text then gives a major practical application for fish, chicken, and similar foods. The Ma'amar Mordechai forbids removing the spine from a large fish before serving, and he also forbids throwing away bones unless they still have some meat on them and are handled as part of eating. The Bi'ur Halacha explains this leniency by saying that fish or chicken with bones is a single natural unit, a guf echad, so removing the bones is considered tikun okhel be'alma rather than Borer. Based on this, the text says that bones may be removed from fish or chicken when they are part of the natural food itself, especially when done close to eating, and some authorities extend this even to taking them out a few minutes beforehand.

Se'if Heh: Lupine Beans

The text quotes the Gemara that one who separates turmosin from their waste is liable. The Rambam explains that in the early stages of cooking the shell sweetens the beans, so the shell is temporarily more like ochel and the beans themselves are more like pesolet. For that reason, removing the beans from their shells in that situation is treated as Borer. The passage notes that other explanations exist in Rashi, but they are less practical.

Se'if Chet: Soaking Seeds and Natural Separation

The text then turns to Se'if Chet, which forbids soaking certain seeds in water or rubbing them in order to remove waste, because that is treated as Borer. It then permits placing them in a sieve or similar utensil for feeding animals, even though the waste falls through, because the separation happens in a way that is not considered a deliberate act of Borer. The Bi'ur Halacha explains that this follows the Ran, who says that when separation happens incidentally through a normal action, it is not treated as psik reishei in the usual sense.

The same logic is applied to common practices such as putting rice in a salt shaker to help the salt flow, or removing a tea bag from a cup. These acts are permitted when one is simply doing the normal act of pouring salt or removing the tea bag, even though some separation happens automatically. But leaving the tea bag in a way that intentionally drains the liquid from it is forbidden, because that is a direct use of the bag to separate liquid from solids.
