General Overview

Rambam’s analysis of yetzi’ah (“going out”) and halichah (“going”) distinguishes physical movement from the spreading or manifestation of nonphysical things. When these verbs refer to God, they describe the manifestation or withdrawal of divine providence rather than physical movement. The lesson connects this language to the idea that a person’s pursuit of God affects the experience of providence, and it presents ethical conduct as a way to draw closer to God.

The Meaning of Yetzi’ah

Yetzi’ah has two meanings: a physical entity leaving one place for another, whether living or inanimate, and the spreading or becoming public of something nonphysical. Examples of the first include people leaving a city and fire spreading; examples of the second include words or Torah becoming known and sunlight appearing.

Rambam explains that when yetzi’ah refers to God, it means the manifestation of something, not physical movement. Thus, הנה ה' יוצא ממקומו means that God’s word—an expression of God’s will—is becoming manifest. Scripture describes God’s will as a word by analogy with kings, who carry out their intentions through speech, although God needs neither speech nor an instrument.

Shuvah, Divine Providence, and Return to One’s Place

Rambam explains that shuvah (“return”) can describe the opposite of divine yetzi’ah: the withdrawal of God’s manifestation and providence. In אלכה ואשובה אל מקומי, God’s “return” to His place means a return to aloofness and the removal of providence. The lesson connects this with the verse ועזבתים והסתרתי פני מהם והיה לאכול ומצאוהו רעות רבות וצרות: when people turn away from God, they are left vulnerable to chance, and what happens to them—good or bad—occurs randomly.

The lesson emphasizes that, in Rambam’s account, this is not necessarily a direct punishment: a person outside divine providence may still experience good fortune, but that good is contingent. It also explains that yetzi’ah may contrast with ba when someone leaves one place for another, as in Moshe’s “going out and coming in,” or with shuvah when someone leaves and then returns to the original place, as in the raven’s flight from the ark and back.

The lesson applies the idea of “returning to one’s place” to Avraham after his exchange with God over Sedom. The teacher suggests that ואברהם שב למקומו can mean that Avraham returns to his human status after unusually challenging the divine; this is the teacher’s interpretation, not one explicitly stated by Rambam. The class also clarifies that makom can mean a special status in reference to God and a person’s relative status in other contexts.

The Meanings of Halichah

Halichah has four meanings. It can describe living creatures moving from place to place, subtler physical things such as water or fire moving, and the spread of something nonphysical, such as a voice. Its fourth meaning is a person’s moral conduct, as in והלכת בדרכיו and לכו ונלכה באור ה'.

When halichah refers to God, Rambam explains that it describes nonphysical action: either the spreading of something or the withdrawal of providence. The image of walking away represents turning away from a person, just as Scripture describes God as hiding His face when people turn away from Him. On this reading, אלך ואשובה אל מקומי expresses God’s withdrawal of providence and return to aloofness.

God’s Anger and Miriam’s Tzaraat

Rambam reads ויחר אף ה' בם וילך, “God became angry with them and went,” as carrying both meanings of divine halichah. God’s going signifies the withdrawal of providence, while the anger’s spreading signifies an effect that reaches the people involved. The lesson proposes that Miriam’s tzaraat may be a random consequence of the withdrawal of providence, rather than a directly assigned punishment; it presents this as the teacher’s interpretation of Rambam’s account.

Ethical Conduct and the Lesson’s Conclusion

The lesson concludes that walking in God’s ways means living uprightly and morally. It presents such conduct as drawing a person toward divine providence, in contrast to turning away from God and being left to chance. The next lesson is scheduled to address chapter 25 and the meaning of Shechinah.
