General Overview

Rambam interprets verbs of movement and dwelling applied to God as nonphysical descriptions of divine providence, presence, or effects. He teaches that the Torah uses anthropomorphic language because people initially understand physical terms more readily, although such terms must ultimately be understood metaphorically. The session concludes by reviewing the meanings of several motion verbs and postponing the remaining review to the next lesson.

Review of Chapters 23 and 24

The verbs yatzah (“to go out”) and halakh (“to go”) can describe physical movement, but when applied to God they refer to the manifestation or withdrawal of His will and hashgachah, or to the spread of an effect from Him. Thus, God’s departure from Miriam and Aaron can mean both the withdrawal of providence and the spread of His anger, manifested in Miriam’s tzara’at. The lecturer clarifies that the withdrawal of hashgachah may leave a person exposed to randomness, but it does not prevent God from directing punishment toward someone; halakh can also describe a person’s moral conduct in following God’s way.

The Meaning of Dwelling and the Shechinah

Rambam gives shakhan two meanings: dwelling within a general area while moving around within it, and the continued presence of something attached to another thing, even when neither is a living creature or the attachment is not to a physical place. The biblical image of a cloud resting on a day illustrates the second meaning. When the verb refers to God, it does not indicate physical residence; it refers to the continued presence of either the Shechinah or hashgachah.

Rambam’s examples of God dwelling on Mount Sinai and among the Israelites refer to the Shechinah, which he describes as created light. This may be understood as physical light, but Rambam’s preferred interpretation is a light experienced in prophetic vision. In the blessing for Joseph, the “will of the One who dwells at the bush” refers to the hashgachah Moses experienced at the burning bush, which the blessing asks God to grant Joseph as well.

The Mishkan is named for its movable dwelling, while the permanent Temples are called Mikdash. The sanctuary itself is not holy simply because of its physical structure: its significance lies in God’s Shechinah or hashgachah, connected to the people’s service and their seeking a relationship with Him. Rambam accordingly teaches that reverence for the Temple is directed not toward its stones but toward the One who commanded that it be revered. The lecturer applies this principle to the Kotel and explains that corruption among priests and the people’s conduct diminished the divine presence in the Second Temple.

Why the Torah Uses Anthropomorphic Language

Rambam invokes the principle that the Torah speaks in human language: people initially understand existence through physical forms, so the Torah describes God using familiar human terms. Such language makes God’s existence and involvement intelligible at an initial level, but it is not the final understanding. The lecturer notes that Rambam applies this principle to nonlegal interpretation, although the Talmud uses it in legal contexts; Rabbeinu Bachya ibn Paquda and the Kuzari also apply it beyond that context.

The Torah uses descriptions that people initially regard as positive, such as movement, but not descriptions they readily recognize as deficiencies, such as eating, drinking, sleeping, illness, or deceit. In truth, movement is no more appropriate to God than eating: both depend on needs, and movement implies physicality. Anything that moves has size and is divisible, while God has no body; therefore, God neither moves nor rests, since rest applies only to something capable of motion. All the motion verbs discussed in earlier chapters must consequently be understood nonliterally, and people must move beyond the physical conceptions they acquire in childhood.

Review of Motion Verbs

The session briefly reviews the verbs yarad, which can refer to the resting of the Shechinah, the imparting of prophecy, or punishment; alah, which can refer to the end of prophecy; and nitzav, which can indicate permanence. The verb savav remains to be studied, and the review of the motion verbs continues in the next lesson.
