General Overview

Rabbi Avraham ben HaRambam’s explanation of Yitzchak’s blessings connects the material details in Parashat Toledot to the chapter’s teaching on perishut. Yitzchak seeks food and wine to restore his strength for a prophetic blessing, while Yaakov recognizes the blessing’s spiritual purpose. The material prosperity promised to Yaakov is a means to intellectual and spiritual distinction, not the ultimate reward; Esav, by contrast, receives a blessing focused on material life.

Perishut and Its Practice

Perishut is an inward freedom from preoccupation with material pleasures, not simply the act of abstaining from them. A wealthy person can be a parush if possessions do not occupy the mind, while a poor person may not be one if deprived pleasures remain a constant concern. Rabbi Avraham teaches that people should first recognize intellectually that food, clothing, houses, and other worldly pleasures are temporary and shared with animals, then work to bring that understanding into the heart.

The practical training begins gradually. A person identifies their present level and reduces indulgences in measured steps, since moving too quickly can harm physical, emotional, and spiritual well-being. Abstention and fasting can help break habits, but the ultimate goal is to reach a state in which material gains and losses no longer disturb the person.

Further Questions About Perishut

The next part of the chapter addresses fasting, the need to proceed slowly, the ways social ties and dependents can make perishut harder, and the signs and benefits of attaining it. Rabbi Avraham also considers objections, including the Torah’s material blessings and the garments of the Kohanim. The lecture postpones these topics, along with a comparison between Rabbi Avraham’s teaching and Rambam’s criticism of extreme abstinence, for later study.

Questions Raised by Yitzchak’s Blessing

Rabbi Avraham identifies two apparent contradictions between perishut and the story of Yitzchak’s blessings. Yitzchak asks Esav to bring him desirable food before blessing him, and Yaakov adds wine even though Yitzchak did not request it. The blessings themselves promise dew, fertile land, abundant produce, power over other nations, and authority over family members.

The narrative emphasizes Yitzchak’s age, failing eyesight, and awareness that he may soon die. It also repeatedly refers to his soul blessing the recipient. Rivka shortens this wording when she tells Yaakov what she heard, while Yaakov and later Esav refer explicitly to Yitzchak’s soul.

Yitzchak’s Physical Needs and Prophetic Blessing

Rabbi Avraham explains that the soul is a person’s true identity and the source of intellectual and spiritual development, but it remains deeply connected to the body during life. Physical illness, weakness, sadness, or lethargy can therefore hinder thought and spiritual attainment. Prophecy requires a state of joy and vitality, which is why the sages teach that prophecy does not rest amid sadness or idleness, and why prophets used music to help attain the required state.

Yitzchak’s failing eyesight signals, in Rabbi Avraham’s interpretation, that his body and senses are weakened. He wants to give more than an ordinary blessing: he seeks a prophetic blessing, and needs food to restore his physical condition and gladden his soul. Rabbi Avraham suggests that Yitzchak’s repeated references to his imminent death may reflect a sudden sense of decline.

Yitzchak’s reaction after learning that he has blessed Yaakov confirms, for Rabbi Avraham, that the blessing is prophetic rather than accidental. Although Yitzchak is distressed, he declares that Yaakov will remain blessed. He understands that the blessing came through divine providence and that its recipient must be worthy of it.

Yaakov, Rivka, and Esav’s Understanding

Rabbi Avraham sees Yaakov’s references to Yitzchak’s soul as evidence that Yaakov independently understands the blessing’s prophetic nature. Rivka does not repeat the phrase exactly when she recounts Yitzchak’s request, and Rabbi Avraham says that she does not emphasize its full significance, though he allows that she may simply have shortened the wording. Yaakov nevertheless understands that his father’s request for special food points to a moment of prophetic blessing.

Yaakov adds wine because wine can gladden and invigorate the soul, not because Yitzchak seeks intoxication. Esav also understands that his father intends to give a prophetic blessing once he hears Yitzchak say so. Rabbi Avraham maintains that Esav possesses genuine spiritual perfection, though less than Yaakov’s, and that Esav’s longing for the blessing reflects its importance to him. He cautions against treating the stark contrast in Midrashic portrayals of Yaakov and Esav as a complete account of Esav’s character.

Rabbi Avraham offers two explanations for why Yitzchak asks Esav to obtain the food. The request may create closeness between father and son and help make Esav worthy of a blessing; alternatively, freshly hunted game may suit Yitzchak’s condition and restore his strength more effectively.

The Purpose of Yaakov’s Material Blessings

Rabbi Avraham explains that the material promises in Yaakov’s blessing are means rather than ends. The Torah’s material benefits enable a person to live without constant concern for basic needs and to devote time to intellectual, ethical, and spiritual development. With that support, Yaakov can become distinguished and worthy of respect and authority.

The blessing’s promise that nations will serve Yaakov therefore reflects his spiritual and intellectual stature, not merely wealth or political power. The material provisions support the development that leads to this stature. Rabbi Avraham relates this reading to Rambam’s teaching that worldly well-being facilitates the pursuit of human perfection rather than serving as the ultimate reward.

Bilaam’s Blessing and the Divine Presence

Rabbi Avraham compares Yitzchak’s blessing with Bilaam’s blessing of Israel. He interprets the words about Yaakov’s tents as referring to the people’s homes, and the words about Israel’s dwelling places as referring to the Mishkan and the Temple, where the Shechinah rests among Israel. In his reading, material well-being and the Divine Presence accompany Israel’s spiritual distinction.

The phrase promising blessing to those who bless Israel and a curse to those who curse Israel follows from the presence of the Shechinah among the people. Rabbi Avraham sees Yitzchak’s and Bilaam’s formulations as conveying the same principle, with Yitzchak giving a shorter version. He rejects interpretations that assign a deeper difference to the order of the words simply because one blessing mentions blessing first and the other mentions cursing first.

Esav’s Blessing and the Closing Interpretation

Yitzchak’s blessing to Yaakov names material benefits first, then describes authority and respect; when he later recounts the blessing to Esav, he puts Yaakov’s authority first and the material support afterward. Rabbi Avraham takes this change in order as evidence that authority and spiritual distinction are the central outcome, while produce and wealth serve only as support.

Esav’s blessing, by contrast, centers on material prosperity, life by the sword, and service to his brother. Rabbi Avraham concludes that Yaakov receives material means to support spiritual development, whereas Esav receives a blessing focused on worldly benefit.
