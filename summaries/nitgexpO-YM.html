General Overview

The shiur examines the Rambam’s laws of Yom Kippur in Hilchot Shevitat Asor, focusing on the Torah’s commands to rest from work and afflict oneself. The Rambam treats Yom Kippur primarily as the tenth day on which one must rest and fast, while its atonement is addressed in Hilchot Teshuvah. The first chapter explains the prohibitions of work, eating, and drinking, compares Yom Kippur with Shabbat, addresses the status of the other bodily restrictions, and presents the Rambam’s distinctive view of adding time before and after Yom Kippur.

The Rambam’s Structure of Yom Kippur

The Rambam calls the section Hilchot Shevitat Asor, “the laws of resting on the tenth,” rather than Hilchot Yom Kippur. He does so because the atonement of Yom Kippur has already been explained in Hilchot Teshuvah, whereas this section deals with the day’s practical laws of resting and fasting. The section contains three chapters and four mitzvot: positive and negative commandments to rest from work, and positive and negative commandments to afflict oneself by refraining from eating and drinking.

The Torah’s Sources for Yom Kippur

The Torah presents Yom Kippur in three places: Acharei Mot and Emor in Vayikra, and Pinchas in Bamidbar. These passages repeatedly command, “ועניתם את נפשותיכם,” and prohibit work through the words “וכל מלאכה לא תעשו.” They also describe the day’s atonement, but the Rambam does not address that subject here because it belongs to Hilchot Teshuvah. The repeated commands establish the positive obligation to afflict oneself, the prohibition against failing to do so, and the positive and negative commandments concerning rest from work.

Resting from Work

The Rambam begins the first chapter with the commandment to rest from all work on the tenth day of the seventh month. Anyone who works on Yom Kippur neglects a positive commandment and violates a negative commandment; intentional work incurs karet, while accidental work requires a fixed sin offering when the Temple is standing. The Rambam bases the prohibition on “ובעשור לחדש השביעי הזה כל מלאכה לא תעשו.”

Yom Kippur and Shabbat

The Rambam states that the laws governing work on Yom Kippur parallel those of Shabbat. Work that carries sekilah on Shabbat carries karet on Yom Kippur, and an accidental violation requiring a sin offering on Shabbat requires the same offering on Yom Kippur. Rabbinic restrictions, muktzeh, prohibited speech, and other Shabbat limitations also apply on Yom Kippur, with the primary difference being that intentional violation of Shabbat carries sekilah, while intentional violation of Yom Kippur carries karet.

Rabbi Meir Simcha HaKohen of Dvinsk, in the Or Sameach, explains the Rambam’s unusual wording: when intentional violation is discussed, the Rambam calls the day “the tenth,” rather than Yom Kippur. A person who deliberately violates Yom Kippur has, in a spiritual sense, removed himself from the status of the holy day, whereas accidental or rabbinic violations still occur within Yom Kippur and its atoning character.

Preparing Food on Yom Kippur

The Talmud permits certain food preparations on an ordinary Yom Kippur afternoon, including removing wilted leaves from vegetables, cracking nuts, and opening pomegranates. These actions are allowed מפני עגמת נפש, so that a person will not become distressed while anticipating the evening meal. The permission does not apply when Yom Kippur falls on Shabbat, and the established practice in Babylonia and the western lands was already to refrain from these actions and treat Yom Kippur like Shabbat in every respect.

The commentators debate how these actions avoid the prohibitions of borer and dash. Some explain that the leaves are not entirely inedible, that they belong to the same type as the edible leaves, or that they are visibly identifiable rather than part of a true mixture. Regarding nuts, the explanation is that they may be crushed without fully extracting the kernels before the fast ends.

Fasting and Afflicting Oneself

The Rambam interprets “ועניתם את נפשותיכם” as a positive commandment to abstain from eating and drinking. The Sages understand affliction of the soul to refer to fasting because human life depends upon food and drink, unlike other possible interpretations such as exposure to heat. Anyone who eats or drinks on Yom Kippur neglects a positive commandment and violates a negative commandment, incurring karet intentionally and requiring a sin offering for an accidental violation.

The Other Four Restrictions

The Rambam also prohibits washing, anointing, wearing footwear, and marital relations on Yom Kippur. He states that these restrictions are included in the day’s complete rest, but only eating and drinking carry karet and a sin offering. A person who violates one of the other restrictions receives makkat mardut, generally understood as rabbinic lashes.

Many Rishonim, including the Rosh and the Tur, classify all four additional restrictions as rabbinic. The Rambam’s position is debated because he refers to them as transmitted through oral tradition and cites verses that appear to give them biblical authority, while his commentary on the Mishnah and Sefer HaMitzvot clearly treat only eating and drinking as biblical. The dispute remains whether the Rambam ultimately regarded the four restrictions as biblical or rabbinic.

The Duration of Yom Kippur

The prohibitions of work, eating, drinking, and the other afflictions apply both during the day and at night. The Rambam also requires adding from the weekday to the holiness of Yom Kippur at both its beginning and its end, based on “מערב עד ערב תשבתו שבתכם.” He understands this addition to apply specifically to fasting and the other afflictions, not to the prohibition of work.

According to the Rambam, there is no general obligation of tosafot Shabbat before and after every Shabbat and Yom Tov. Other Rishonim and the Shulchan Aruch extend the principle of adding from the weekday to the holy day to Shabbat and Yom Tov generally. The amount of added time has no fixed measure, though the practical minimum is commonly treated as approximately two minutes, with communities adopting longer periods.

Women Who Continue Eating Until Nightfall

The Rambam rules that women who continue eating and drinking until dark because they do not know about the obligation of adding time are not forcibly stopped. He explains that it is preferable for them to violate the requirement unintentionally rather than intentionally, since one cannot supervise every household. The Maggid Mishneh and the Rema distinguish between an explicitly written Torah commandment, which must be enforced even when people may not listen, and a law derived through interpretation, where this principle of allowing unintentional error applies.

The Rambam’s wording also reflects his view that a doubtful biblical prohibition is treated leniently by Torah law, with stringency imposed only rabbinically. Therefore, during the uncertain period between sunset and nightfall, he permits the women to continue eating rather than requiring active intervention.

Remaining Chapters

The second chapter addresses the permitted quantities of eating and drinking on Yom Kippur and the laws governing sick people. The third chapter deals with washing, anointing, footwear, marital relations, and immersion in a mikveh under particular circumstances.
