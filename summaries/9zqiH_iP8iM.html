General Overview

The introduction to the Moreh presents the book as a guide for people who are torn between the plain sense of Tanakh and philosophical inquiry, and it insists that the deepest matters cannot always be explained fully or directly. Rambam teaches that Tanakh uses different kinds of meshalim: some are built so that every word carries meaning, while others use many details only to shape the parable as a whole or to hide the deeper point. He then gives advice on how to read the book carefully, judge him favorably, avoid misusing his words, and understand that he writes for the benefit of the few who are ready for the truth even if many others object.

Two Kinds of Meshalim

Rambam states that there are two kinds of prophetic analogies in Tanakh. In one kind, every word in the mashal adds meaning to the nimshal, and in the other, many words serve only to beautify the parable or to conceal the deeper meaning more effectively. He says readers must understand this distinction so they do not demand a separate explanation for every detail where only the general idea is intended.

Yaakov’s Ladder

The dream of Yaakov in Parashat Vayetzei is presented as an example of a mashal in which every element has significance. The ladder, its placement on the earth, its reaching the heavens, the angels ascending and descending, and Hashem standing above it all each convey a separate aspect of the deeper meaning. The lesson is that physical and spiritual reality are connected, that human actions rise upward and celestial influences descend downward, and that Hashem oversees the entire system.

The Adulterous Woman in Mishlei

The long passage in Mishlei about the simple young man and the seductive woman is presented as an example of a mashal in which the details serve the overall warning. The point of the story is to caution against following bodily pleasures and desires, which Shlomo Hamelech compares to the deceptive pull of a harlot and a married woman. Rambam explains that this mashal teaches that a person should not live according to animalistic impulses and should not confuse material desire with true human perfection.

Do Not Force Every Detail

Rambam says that once the general meaning of a mashal is clear, the reader should not chase every detail for an independent nimshal. He warns that doing so either distracts from the main point or leads to interpretations that were never intended. He adds that many people wrongly force their own ideas into verses and expressions in Tanakh, and he instructs the reader to focus on the overall intention of the mashal rather than inventing meanings for every image.

How to Learn the Book

Rambam advises the reader to compare chapters with one another, to pay attention to every term, and to assume that the sequence and wording of the book are deliberate. He says nothing is placed out of order without purpose, and the reader should not approach the book with preconceived notions. He also says that repeated study will clarify many of the difficult issues in Torah.

Judge Favorably and Do Not Misuse the Book

Rambam warns readers not to repeat anything from the book except what is already explicit in the words of earlier and recognized sages of Toratenu. He also asks them not to rush to accuse him of meanings he did not intend. If a reader benefits from some parts of the book, that is enough; if not, the reader may ignore it. If something appears problematic, he asks that it be interpreted in the most favorable possible way, even if the explanation is strained.

Why He Wrote the Book

Rambam says he wrote the Moreh reluctantly because the matter was necessary and because people needed guidance. He describes the book as intended to help the few capable readers who are wise in Torah and troubled by philosophical confusion, even if many others will reject it. He prefers to say what only one serious person will understand rather than flatter thousands of ignorant readers, and he values helping that one person rise from confusion more than pleasing the many.

Reasons for Contradictions in Books

Rambam begins his final introduction by explaining that contradictions in books arise for seven different reasons. The first reason is that an author may gather sayings from different people without identifying who said what, so different opinions appear side by side. The second reason is that an author may later change his mind, leaving both earlier and later views in the same work. The third reason is that some statements are literal while others are meshalim, so they only seem contradictory when read superficially.

Apparent Contradictions in Scripture

Rambam says that some verses seem to contradict one another only because one or both are not meant literally, such as verses about seeing Hashem or about Hashem as a “man of war.” He also explains that some contradictions disappear when a hidden condition is supplied, as in the Gemara’s resolution of Al t'an kesil ke'ilto and Anei kesil ke'ilto, where one case applies to Divrei Torah and another to ordinary matters. He says that the remaining reasons will be explained later and that his own book contains only the fifth and seventh kinds of contradiction.
