The Three Verbs of Nearness

Rambam examines karav (“draw near”), naga (“touch”), and nagash (“approach”). Each can refer either to physical proximity or to intellectual apprehension. Abarbanel distinguishes their spatial meanings as increasing degrees of closeness: karav means drawing near, nagash means coming nearer, and naga means touching. He notes that nagash is used for a person approaching another person, while karav and naga can also describe nonhuman things.

Physical and Intellectual Nearness

Rambam gives biblical examples of physical approach and touch, including Moses nearing the camp, Pharaoh drawing near, and Judah approaching Joseph. He also explains that these verbs can express intellectual apprehension rather than bodily movement: Babylon’s wrongdoing “reaches heaven,” difficult cases are brought near to Moses for his attention, and Abraham approaches Hashem in prophecy when pleading for Sodom.

Nearness to Hashem

When these terms describe a relationship between Hashem and created beings, Rambam says they refer to intellectual apprehension, not physical distance. Because Hashem has no body and occupies no space, spatial ideas such as proximity, separation, and touch do not apply to Him. Abarbanel adds that Scripture does not describe a human being as touching Hashem: such complete closeness exceeds the limits of human apprehension.

Rambam interprets verses about Hashem being close to those who call upon Him as referring to knowledge, relationship, and providence. A person who seeks Hashem and understands His ways places himself under divine providence; the verse about desiring closeness to God likewise follows the pursuit of understanding. Being close to Hashem means greater apprehension of Him, not being physically nearer in one place than another.

Rambam allows that a reader who cannot yet accept this interpretation may understand certain verses as referring to a created manifestation of divine glory, as Saadia Gaon does. He insists, however, that this is not the primary interpretation: physical location, even the highest sphere, does not make anyone nearer to Hashem. The verse about Jacob’s ladder reaching heaven therefore describes prophetic apprehension rather than physical contact with God.

Divine Touch and the Limits of Apprehension

When Scripture portrays Hashem as touching something, Rambam explains that the image can mean that Hashem issues a command or brings about an effect. Thus, Hashem touching the mountains means commanding them to smoke, and the expression in Job about touching Job means bringing affliction upon him.

The teacher connects this interpretation to verses warning against touching Mount Sinai or the tree of knowledge. Beyond their straightforward physical meaning, such language can warn against reaching beyond one’s intellectual capacity. The account of the four who entered the orchard illustrates the danger: excessive intellectual inquiry can lead to death, madness, or loss of faith.

Cleaving to Hashem and the Sages

The teacher asks why Rambam does not include davek, “cleave,” among the three verbs. The Talmud in Ketubot already asks how a person can cleave to the Divine Presence and answers that supporting Torah scholars, including through marriage or financial assistance, is regarded as cleaving to it. The teacher explains that the verse says all Israel cleaves to Hashem, so the Talmud does not interpret it as a level of intellectual apprehension that only some people attain; instead, connection to the sages provides a way to cleave to Hashem through His representatives in the world.

The Three Meanings of “Fill”

In Chapter 19, Rambam explains that male (“fill”) has three meanings. It can describe one physical thing filling another, such as water filling a pitcher; it can describe a period of time reaching completion; or it can mean attaining fullness, excellence, or perfection. Biblical descriptions of people being filled with blessing or wisdom use this third sense. The teacher also notes that Rambam sometimes quotes verses from memory, which can lead to small variations in wording.

Divine Glory, the Mishkan, and Sacred Places

Rambam interprets “the whole earth is filled with His glory” to mean that the universe displays Hashem’s perfection and wisdom, not that Hashem physically fills space. Likewise, the statement that Hashem’s glory fills the Mishkan means that the Mishkan provides a setting in which people can perceive the divine more clearly. Rambam again permits the view that a created manifestation of divine glory filled the Mishkan, but maintains that the deeper meaning is the manifestation of divine excellence.

The teacher applies this interpretation to the Western Wall. Its significance does not mean that Hashem is spatially closer there or that prayers automatically become more effective. Rather, a person may experience the divine more deeply at a place associated with the Temple, and that heightened apprehension can shape prayer and providence.
