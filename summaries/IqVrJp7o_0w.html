General Overview

The lecture continues the study of the Rambam’s Moreh Nevuchim, focusing on chapters 17 and 18 and especially on chapter 17’s treatment of chomer (matter), tzurah (form), and he'eder (privation or deficiency). The Rambam teaches that profound theological and scientific ideas should sometimes be conveyed through metaphors and withheld from those unable to understand them properly. The lecture explains that deficiency drives physical change and human development, connects this principle to the sequence of the surrounding chapters, and applies it to material desires, intellectual growth, and the careful transmission of knowledge.

The Connection to Chapter 16

Chapter 16 explains that tzur can mean a boulder, sharp stone, quarry, or, figuratively, the source from which something originates. When applied to Hashem, tzur means the ultimate source and cause of all existence. The phrase venitzavt al hatzur is therefore understood as an instruction to stand firmly in contemplation of Hashem as the source of all being, thereby attaining greater knowledge of Him.

The Purpose of Chapter 17

Chapter 17 appears at first to interrupt the sequence of chapters dealing with biblical terms. Chapters 18–20 return to terms concerning drawing near, fullness, and exaltation, while chapter 17 instead addresses the concealment of esoteric teachings. The Rambam argues that not only theological subjects but also advanced scientific subjects should sometimes be kept from the masses, since misunderstood knowledge can be harmful.

Metaphors in Science and Torah

The Rambam states that ancient philosophers presented discussions of the origins and foundations of existence through riddles and metaphors. Plato, for example, described matter as female and form as male rather than explaining these concepts directly. The lecture explains that the Tanach similarly uses terms such as throne or heaven to convey deeper ideas like majesty, permanence, and endurance, because direct presentation of lofty concepts may lead unprepared people to misunderstand them.

Matter, Form, and Privation

The Rambam identifies three foundations of all generated and perishable physical things: chomer, tzurah, and he'eder. Chomer is matter, tzurah is the form or defining structure that matter receives, and he'eder is the deficiency or privation connected with matter. Without privation, matter would not acquire a new form; once a deficiency is fulfilled through the acquisition of a form, another deficiency emerges, continuing the process of change.

Aristotle’s Explanation of Change

The lecture presents two Aristotelian principles underlying the Rambam’s explanation. First, things that are perfect do not change; second, physical things are composites of matter and form. Since change nevertheless occurs, privation must serve as the factor that creates movement from one form to another. The speaker explains that Aristotle used this reasoning to argue that the universe was eternal, although the lecture notes that later scientific theories reject Aristotle’s account of the universe’s origins.

Examples of Privation

An unmusical person who sees someone playing the violin develops a deficiency—the desire to possess that ability—and acquires musical form through study. Once the person learns the violin, a new deficiency may arise, such as the desire to play the trumpet. A piece of metal similarly moves from an unfinished shape toward a statue because its existing condition contains a deficiency that invites a further form.

Privation and Human Desire

The same pattern applies to human beings, who constantly seek to fill perceived deficiencies. The verse אוהב כסף לא ישבע כסף expresses the idea that a person who loves money will never be satisfied with money, and Rabbi Avraham explains that money represents material desire generally. Once a person reaches one goal, a new desire emerges, making contentment difficult without deliberate work on one’s character.

Intellectual Development

Human privation is not limited to physical or material desires. People also possess a natural drive to develop intellectually, morally, and spiritually, and the Rambam considers the failure to respond to this need for intellectual development a form of evil. The lecture connects this process to histapkut, or contentment, while emphasizing that the natural human tendency is to seek further growth rather than remain static.

The Deeper Connection to Hashem

The lecture explains that chapter 17 continues chapter 16 through the relationship between tzur and tzurah. Although tzurah represents the masculine form that acts upon matter, the word is grammatically feminine because it receives its direction from Hashem, who is the ultimate tzur. Hashem stands above matter, form, and privation as the source who directs the combination of these elements into the physical world.

The Careful Transmission of Knowledge

The Rambam’s discussion of he'eder also explains why knowledge must be taught gradually and selectively. Human beings have an appetite for understanding, but providing too much intellectual material too quickly can overwhelm or harm them, just as overeating after a fast can cause illness. The teacher must therefore present profound ideas carefully, using metaphor and allowing the student’s intellectual deficiency to be filled in stages.

The Chapter’s Final Teaching

The Rambam concludes that if philosophers used metaphors and concealed scientific concepts that were relatively safe, Torah scholars must be even more cautious when teaching theological matters. They must avoid presenting ideas in ways that the masses cannot understand or will interpret incorrectly. The lecture connects this caution to the Rambam’s earlier image of approaching the burning bush carefully.

Questions on Free Will and Divine Order

The participants consider whether privation is also necessary for free will, since a world containing only matter and form would leave no room for growth, choice, or development. The discussion also presents the Rambam’s framework as a possible synthesis between Aristotelian science and belief in divine direction: the natural structure of matter, form, and privation operates within an order ultimately governed by Hashem.
