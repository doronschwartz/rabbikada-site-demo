General Overview

The class resumes the study of Shabbat law in Siman 321, focusing first on pickling and then beginning the laws of tochen, or grinding. Pickling is rabbinically prohibited under explanations associated with either tanning or cooking, while grinding is prohibited in several forms but permits certain major changes in method and the further grinding of materials already ground. The class also addresses practical cases involving salads, salt solutions, fruit, and cooked foods.

Return to Shabbat Study and Siman 321

The class returns to the Shulchan Aruch after a break for studying the Rambam’s laws of Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot, fasts, and Tisha B’Av. The ongoing Shabbat-law project has covered Simanim 242–320, including carrying, muktzeh, building, trapping, tying, cooking, selecting, and squeezing. Siman 321 covers pickling in sections 2–6, tochen in sections 7–12, and lash from section 14 onward; the class begins with pickling and then turns to grinding.

Pickling on Shabbat

The Gemara prohibits making a salt-water solution for pickling and salting several vegetables that are commonly pickled. Rashi and many other Rishonim explain the prohibition as a rabbinic extension of me’abed, tanning, because salt changes the texture of vegetables; the Rambam understands it as a rabbinic extension of bishul, cooking. The Shulchan Aruch presents both explanations.

A solution containing two-thirds salt is prohibited even in a small amount, while a less concentrated solution may be made in a small quantity for immediate eating; making a large quantity is prohibited. Salting four or five pieces of vegetables commonly pickled is also prohibited, though me’ikar hadin a person may salt a small number or dip pieces individually. An Ashkenazi custom developed to avoid salting vegetables altogether and to dip them in salt instead. Pickling even one piece for later use is prohibited, as is salting meat or a cooked egg to preserve it.

Practical Applications of Pickling Rules

Salting a salad that includes vegetables commonly pickled is acceptable, in the speaker’s view, when the salt is added together with oil or lemon. The speaker considers salting a mixed salad by itself permissible when the salad as a whole is not a pickling preparation, but says a salad primarily made of pickled vegetables should be dressed with oil or lemon along with the salt. Vinegar can also be used for pickling and is subject to the same concern.

Chacham Ovadia permits adding sugar to fruit for eating on Shabbat, since fruit are neither cooked nor tanned in the relevant sense; preparing fruit for the following day is not permitted. He is uncertain whether a partly pickled food may be returned to a pickling solution on Shabbat and recommends avoiding it. Pieces dipped individually in a solution may be gathered together afterward, according to the cited ruling.

Tochen: Grinding, Crushing, and Mashing

The class begins tochen, the melachah of grinding, which includes grinding, crushing, mashing, and cutting produce into very small pieces. A major shinui—using the back of a knife, fork, or spoon—permits crushing or mashing, such as preparing mashed banana or avocado; the food is placed on an ordinary surface rather than processed with a specialized grinding tool. Specialized graters are generally muktzeh, with an exception for a cheese grater to be addressed later.

The principle אין טוחן אחר טוחן means that an item already ground may be ground again, so bread and processed salt may be broken or crushed further. Cutting vegetables into very small pieces is also considered tochen, although the precise size is unclear and the class advises against cutting them too finely. Cooked meat and fish may be cut into small pieces, and cooked eggs may be mashed, based on the combination of their being edible and not directly classified as produce grown from the ground; adding mayonnaise raises a separate question of lash.
