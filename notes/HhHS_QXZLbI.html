Where we are

The Rabbi opened by calling Siman 11 probably the hardest siman of the course, because the Rishonim argue about so many details of one short sugyah. Last week was Siman 10, Seifim 5-7 (a man suspected of being with a non-Jewish woman, and what follows today). Seif 4 of Siman 11 deals with the laws of witnesses and is a separate sugyah, left for later. Seifim 1-3 are this whole sugyah.

His plan: today learn the Gemara properly and go through the main Rishonim up to the Rif and the Rambam; next time finish the remaining views, then the Shulhan Arukh, the Rema and the nosei kelim. The key, he said, is to get the sugyah and the shitot clear first, otherwise the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema cannot be understood. He asked the class to write down five questions and test each Rishon against them.

The Mishnah in Yevamot (24b)

The Mishnah says: a man who was suspected with a married woman, and they made him divorce her (hotsiuha), even if he married her he must divorce her (yotsi). Two terms are used throughout:

  • The baal is the husband, the first man.
  • The nitan is the man suspected with her (the Gemara's example is a travelling peddler, the rokhel, who behaves suspiciously with the wife at home).

Two things in the Mishnah are open. What does "they made him divorce" mean, and does "yotsi" refer to the husband or to the nitan? Rashi reads yotsi as the husband.

The Gemara: Rav against Rav Sheshet

Rav reads the Mishnah as speaking only of edei tumah: witnesses to the forbidden act itself. The beit din forced the husband out only because of this, so even if the nitan married her and even if they have children, he must divorce her.

Rav Sheshet objects from two beraitot:

  1. If the nitan was forced out, she married another man and was divorced, and the nitan then married her, he need not divorce her.
  2. This applies when there are children (banim); without children, he must.

If the case were edei tumah, why would an intervening marriage or children help? So these beraitot must speak of cases without edei tumah, and then the beit din still forces her out.

Rav's two answers (the Gemara gives both):

  • The beraitot are read to fit his view. Without edei tumah the husband is never forced, but lehatehilah the nitan should not marry her even after an intervening marriage. The Mishnah, by saying hotsiuha, teaches that with edei tumah even children do not help.
  • The second answer is that the beraitot follow Rebbe, while our Mishnah follows the Rabbanan. Rebbe holds that edei davar mekhoar (witnesses to a suspicious sign) is already enough. The Rabbanan, and Rav, require edei tumah. The beraitot give examples of signs: the peddler has just left the house and the woman is fixing her belt, or something suspicious is found next to the bed.

So Rav Sheshet reads the Mishnah like Rebbe: edei davar mekhoar is enough to force out, and the nitan who married her must still leave.

The conclusion: halakhah like Rav and like Rebbe

The Gemara first asks how the halakhah can follow both, then settles it with a compromise:

  • A kol delo pasik (a rumour that did not stop) together with edei davar mekhoar: the halakhah is like Rebbe, and she goes out.
  • A rumour that stopped, or no rumour, even with edei davar mekhoar: the halakhah is like Rav, and no one is forced out until there are edei tumah.

The Rabbi gave the basic definition of a kol delo pasik: the town talks about it for a day and a half without a break. If someone works to stop it, it does not count, and it counts only where there are no enemies behind it. What exactly "enemies" means is a separate discussion, which he left for the end.

The five questions

He asked the class to carry these through every shitah:

  1. What does edim mean? Almost every Rishon says edei tumah. The Maharam understands it differently, which comes next time.
  2. When we rule like Rebbe for a kol delo pasik, is the rumour enough on its own, or do we also need davar mekhoar? Tosafot and others hold that the rumour alone is stronger than a sign and is enough. The other reading is that the rumour must come together with a suspicious sign. He said the Rif and the Rambam are explicit in requiring both.
  3. When the Gemara says "she goes out" (tetse), is it said of the husband, of the nitan, or of both? He said this is perhaps the key question. (He worked on it aloud and left one sub-point to come back to.)
  4. Do the two beraitot (the leniencies of an intervening marriage and of children) still apply lehalakhah when we follow Rebbe?
  5. Whose are the banim in the beraita: the children of the nitan, or of the first husband? Rashi reads them as the husband's.

He also noted one point from the Gemara in Nedarim. Several cases there seem to show that a husband would be forced out on a suspicious sign, and the Gemara defends the people involved by saying it was not really suspicious. One could conclude from this that a husband can be forced out without edei tumah. The answer given is that this is not the reading: the husband is never forced without edei tumah, and Nedarim is only about the husband's own conduct as a baal nefesh, not strict law.

The Rishonim so far

1. The Bahag. Edei tumah means real biah, as the Bahag puts it. (The Rabbi added that in the sugyah in Makkot, most Rishonim say you need not see penetration; it is enough to see them lying together.) The husband is never forced to divorce without edei tumah. The nitan is forced out when there is davar mekhoar with a kol delo pasik, following Rebbe. The Bahag does not bring the two beraitot, so there are no leniencies: the nitan goes out even with children or an intervening marriage.

2. The Sheiltot. Read as it stands, he says that where there is a davar mekhoar with two witnesses and a kol delo pasik, they force out both the husband and the nitan, even without edei tumah. So he disagrees with the Geonim and holds that a husband can be forced out on a sign. He also does not bring the beraitot. The Rashba is unsure whether he accepts them. The Maharam and the Rosh each read the Sheiltot differently, to be seen next time.

3. Rabbenu Tam (and Tosafot). The husband is never forced without edei tumah. For the nitan, a kol delo pasik alone is enough, with no separate sign needed, since the rumour itself is the proof. Tosafot compare it to the idea in Siman 6 of a kol that is sufficient on its own. Here the two beraitot are lehalakhah, and the banim are the husband's. The Rabbi explained why. If we force out the nitan because of a rumour, the first husband's children might be suspected of coming from the other man, so the beraita protects them.

4. The Rif and the Rambam. The Rabbi called the Rif the clearest of all. We rule like Rebbe only when there is both a kol delo pasik and a davar mekhoar. The husband always needs edei tumah. The nitan goes out with both elements together. The two beraitot are accepted, so lehatehilah he should not marry her, but if he did and they have children, or she married another man in between, he need not divorce her. The banim are the nitan's. When the Rif speaks of forcing the nitan out, the worry is about the children of that marriage, though the Rabbi noted this is a pegam, a flaw like ben niddah in Siman 4, and not mamzerut. The Rambam will be seen next time.

Practical summary

  • A husband is not forced out on suspicion alone. For the husband the baseline is edei tumah, unless one follows the Sheiltot or the later views.
  • A kol delo pasik is a rumour of a day and a half with no break, no one suppressing it, and no enemies behind it.
  • The sugyah rules like Rav and like Rebbe: Rebbe where there is a continuing rumour with a suspicious sign, Rav otherwise.
  • Almost all Rishonim understand edim as edei tumah.
  • Whether the rumour needs a sign, who goes out, whether the two beraitot stand, and whose banim they are: these five points separate every shitah.
