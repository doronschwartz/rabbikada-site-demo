A brief review:

  • Chapter 1: thanks to Hashem for the ability to write the book.
  • Chapter 2: people fail to find the truth because they are lazy or do not know what they are looking for. Many flip-flop from one belief to another, so Rav Saadiah felt he had to write this book.
  • Chapter 3: why does Hashem allow doubt? Because humans must work through a process to reach any result. Eliminate only some options and you are left with doubt. "Wise people are patient." This book, for example, "is going to take a few years to learn."

  • Chapter 5: the three sources of knowledge: the senses, self-evident reason (truth is good, falsehood is bad) and inference (from smoke we infer fire; from eating and excretion, the body's powers; from the moon, a whole science). Then a fourth: הגדה נאמנה, verified, authentic tradition, which is not only the Torah but includes it. The Tanakh itself supports the other three. Part 1: Before We Begin: The Kuzari Argument

Before Chapter 6, the Rabbi presents a famous argument usually credited to the Kuzari of Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi, written about two centuries after Rav Saadiah. It is known as the Kuzari argument for the revelation at Mount Sinai. Many books have been written for and against it, but in one minute it goes like this.

The Torah says, in Shemot and in many places in Devarim, אתם ראיתם כי מן השמים דברתי עמכם, "you saw that I spoke to you from heaven." How could someone deny the book? The easiest way is to say it is fiction, made up by someone later.

But consider the difference. If I am a charismatic, trusted person, I might convince you of something happening far away. "You'll write in your book that you were told that in Australia dragons grow on trees," and pass it on to your children. It is almost impossible, though, however charismatic I am, to convince you that you yourself saw the fire and smoke and heard God at Sinai, when you did not.

Perhaps someone could use hypnosis. But the same book says there were 600,000 men aged twenty to sixty. Add the men under twenty and over sixty, and the women, and we have roughly two million people, a figure Rav Saadiah himself uses. "It's easy for me to hypnotise ten of you here. It's going to be quite hard for me to hypnotise two million."

There is a counter-argument (perhaps someone came later and said, "this is what was given to your grandfathers"), but that is "a different discussion. It's not for now."

"That's always known as the Kuzari argument. In truth, Saadiah said it before him, in a more subtle way." He hints at it in several places in this chapter.

Part 2: The Question: Who Permits Us to Philosophise?

Chapter 6 opens with a question people have asked for centuries and still ask today. What right do we have to analyse our beliefs? Would it not be better to keep quiet, ask no questions and have simple faith?

The question is sharp because of Rav Saadiah's aim. He wants us to become as certain of our beliefs as we are that two plus two equals four. That is the meaning of the title: emunot, beliefs, are to become deot, firmly founded knowledge. Who gives permission for that?

Many people say philosophy leads to heresy. This is exactly the view Rav Saadiah addresses. He was criticised for this book in his own lifetime, and later too, for example by Rabbi Moshe Taku, one of the Baalei HaTosafot.

His first answer: popular ignorance

Rav Saadiah's answer is "beautiful, simple and direct." This, he says, is what ignorant people say, and he compares it to the popular superstitions of his day:

  • the belief in his city that whoever goes to India becomes rich;
  • the belief that a lunar eclipse happens because a dragon swallows the moon;
  • a folk belief that if a man's camel is not slaughtered on his grave, he will have to walk to the Day of Judgement.

"You may be laughing at this, and Saadiah himself laughs at this." The people who say that inquiry is forbidden and leads to heresy are the same kind of people who believe such things.

Part 3: But Did the Sages Not Forbid It?

Then comes the serious objection. Did Hazal not warn us against speculation? The Mishnah in Hagigah (2:1) says: whoever gazes into four things, it would have been better had he not been created: what is above, what is below, what is before and what is after.

Mistakel does not mean a passing thought. It means to gaze: to analyse and speculate deeply. So it seems Hazal forbid us to inquire about God, the beginning of time and the end of time.

The Rabbi notes that Hazal themselves discuss these subjects in many places in the Talmud, such as the end of days, so there must be caveats. But Rav Saadiah takes a different approach.

The Tanakh commands inquiry

It cannot be that Hazal forbade inquiry, because the reliable tradition itself commands it:

  • Yeshayahu (40:21): הלא תדעו הלא תשמעו... הלא הבינותם מוסדות הארץ, "do you not know, do you not hear... have you not understood the foundations of the earth?"
  • Iyov's friends said to one another (34:4): משפט נבחרה לנו נדעה בינינו מה טוב, "let us choose for ourselves what is just; let us know among ourselves what is good."

The Rabbi raised a difficulty with the second proof. Iyov's friends were grappling with God's providence, why the righteous suffer, not with the beginning and end of the world. Even so, understanding God's providence is a key to understanding God. If philosophy were banned, this would be banned too, and the whole book of Iyov is about understanding God.

What the Sages really warned against

What Hazal warned against is relying on speculation alone: each person working out the beginning of time, eternity and God from his own ideas, with nothing else. Why?

  1. You might succeed, or you might fail.
  2. Even if you succeed, it takes time, and until then you are without the truth, without Torah. "Remember, it's a process. Didn't we learn in Chapter 3?"
  3. Even if you find the truth quickly, you are not protected from doubt. You may lose your faith and move to another belief, the flip-flopping that concerned him in Chapter 2.

Rav Saadiah's thesis

The Rabbi sums up the chapter's thesis: we are commanded to philosophise once we have established our foundations from the reliable tradition. First you take the foundations from the Tanakh; then you use your mind to verify what you have been told.

That raises a question Rav Saadiah himself will ask: if the intellect can reach the truth, why do we need the tradition at all? "The answer is obvious," but we will come to it. Meanwhile his approach is clear: philosophy is important when it is joined to a foundation of what our beliefs are.

Part 4: Two Purposes of Inquiry

"May God set you on the straight path, the one studying this book." We inquire for two reasons:

  1. To verify for ourselves what the prophets have told us, by analysis and reason.
  2. To answer those who deny our Torah. This comes straight from Pirkei Avot (2:14): דע מה שתשיב לאפיקורוס, "know what to answer the heretic."

"What we are doing in this next couple of years together, studying this book, is essentially these two things."

Hashem has already told us everything through the prophets, after confirming them with signs and miracles, and commanded us to accept their words. But when we inquire properly, we will find for ourselves the same truth the prophets told us. And we have been promised that if we study the Torah properly and philosophise properly, we will have satisfying answers for those who question our faith.

Rav Saadiah brings verses for the foundations. On the beginning of the world and God's unity (Yeshayahu 44:6): אני ראשון ואני אחרון ומבלעדי אין אלהים, "I am the first and I am the last, and besides Me there is no God." And (44:7): "who is like Me... since I set the ancient people." The Rabbi's paraphrase: "You have that in the prophets. Now go and verify it, go and analyse and inquire about the beginning of time, once you have that verse fixed in your head."

Part 5: "Do Not Fear": Reading Yeshayahu 44:8

Rav Saadiah now digresses to show that the Torah removes our fear of those who argue against us. He reads a verse phrase by phrase:

אל תפחדו ואל תרהו הלא מאז השמעתיך והגדתי ואתם עדי היש אלוה מבלעדי ואין צור בל ידעתי

"Do not fear and do not be afraid. Have I not told you from of old and declared it? You are My witnesses. Is there a God besides Me? There is no Rock that I do not know."

Phrase

Rav Saadiah's reading

אל תפחדו, do not fear

Pahad is fear of something physical: how many your opponents are, how strong. As in "and you fear continually all day because of the fury of the oppressor" (Yeshayahu 51:13).

ואל תרהו, do not be afraid

From yirah (the heh and alef interchange). This is fear of their words, their arguments, as in "whoever feared the word of Hashem" (Shemot 9:20).

השמעתיך והגדתי, I told you and declared

The prophecies of the past have come true, and the new ones will too.

ואתם עדי, you are My witnesses

You saw the miracles.

היש אלוה מבלעדי, is there a God besides Me?

Perhaps you fear that what I promised will not come true. That could only happen if there were another creator. Since I alone am God, it will come true.

ואין צור בל ידעתי, there is no Rock I do not know

Sur here means the great thinkers.

"You are My witnesses": the manna

"This is where we get to the Kuzari now." You are My witnesses: you saw the plagues in Egypt, the splitting of the sea, the revelation at Sinai. But Rav Saadiah says he has a stronger miracle than all of these. "I love what Saadiah is going to do now. Saadiah is pragmatic." The greatest miracle is the manna.

It was continuous. The plagues, the sea and Sinai were each one-off events. The manna came every day for forty years.

There is no other explanation. Rav Saadiah challenges anyone to suggest another way to feed close to two million people in the desert for forty years, except with food from the Creator.

The philosophers would have copied it. Philosophers in his day were not wealthy. "Still nowadays, academics is not the best paid job." They needed support and had to ask others for help. If there had been the slightest way to repeat this miracle, the ancient philosophers would have used it to feed their students and teach them wisdom all day, instead of asking for help.

The children would never have accepted a lie. This is the key line. Had the manna not been true, Bnei Yisrael would never have agreed to it. Imagine parents telling their children, "We ate manna in the desert for forty years," and the children answering, "What are you talking about? You had fields and gardens. You had a livelihood."

"This is really strong. You can't lie to people. You can't lie to your children."

"There are many miracles, but for me, I love this miracle, because it's continuous and pragmatic."

"There is no Rock I do not know"

The simple meaning of sur is God. Rav Saadiah reads it differently: sur refers to great men, as in "look to the rock from which you were hewn... look to Avraham your father" (Yeshayahu 51:1-2), where Avraham is called a rock. He brings another verse too: "You turn back the edge (sur) of his sword" (Tehillim 89:44).

So the verse means: I know all the great thinkers. There is no philosopher whose thoughts I do not know, and I am giving you the answers to all their arguments. No one can bring a convincing proof against our Torah. "Wow, very confident, right? Saadiah is extremely confident."

Part 6: If Reason Can Get There, Why Do We Need Miracles?

"With this root, may Hashem help us, we go out to inquire." Once we have established our foundations from the prophets, we can begin the journey of analysis.

Now Rav Saadiah's own question. If we can reach all of this through the intellect, why did Hashem need prophets and miracles at all? The Rabbi put the question to the class. Someone answered that you would have to go through the whole process, and many people would not. "Fantastic. This is Saadiah following his own theory. This is why the book is important to follow, because each bit builds on the last."

Rav Saadiah gives two reasons:

  1. Time. Even those capable of philosophy need time. Without the prophets and the Torah, there would be a period when we did not have the truth.
  2. Failure. Some will never finish. People get fed up, drop out, or are overwhelmed by doubts and confusion.

So Hashem spared us all this. He sent His prophets and showed us with our own eyes. Again, the Rabbi notes, this is the Kuzari argument: "you saw that I spoke to you from heaven" (Shemot 20:19), and "so that the people hear when I speak with you, and believe in you forever" (Shemot 19:9). "He gives it to us on a golden plate and says, here it is. Now work it out yourself."

Which source of knowledge?

How do we know the Torah is true? Through sense perception. "You saw the miracles. You heard the prophets." So we are obliged to believe because of what we saw and what we heard. If it takes someone a long time to reach the truth intellectually, that is fine, because he already has it through the verified tradition.

Even someone who cannot philosophise at all can still be complete in his faith. "How beautiful": all people, men, women and children, are equal in what their senses show them. The Rabbi added that this is why the Torah often mentions women and children when it describes miracles.

The thousand coins

Rav Saadiah gives an example to make this "closer to you." A man has a thousand coins. He gives:

  • five people 22 coins each;
  • six people 16 and two-thirds each;
  • seven people 14 and two-sevenths each;
  • eight people 12 and three-eighths each;
  • nine people 10 and one-ninth each.

He wants to show everyone that he has 500 coins left. He could ask them to add up all the fractions, but the quickest proof is to weigh the coins in front of them. They see it with their own eyes, and they must accept it. Then, in their own time, anyone who wants to can do the arithmetic. (The Rabbi asked a mathematically minded participant to check the numbers, since some editions apparently have a different figure.)

A second example: a doctor can look at the symptoms and tell you at once what is wrong, while someone else takes years to study the whole of medicine and understand how it all works.

Miracles before Sinai

Finally, Rav Saadiah notes that miracles were also shown before Bnei Yisrael, such as to Noah and Avraham. Whoever saw them was obliged to believe, because of what he sensed.

That completes Chapter 6.

Summary

  1. The question: may we analyse our beliefs, or is simple faith better? Rav Saadiah's first answer: the objection comes from popular ignorance.
  2. What Hazal warned against was relying on speculation alone, which is slow, uncertain and leaves us open to doubt. The Tanakh itself calls on us to inquire.
  3. The thesis: take the foundations from the reliable tradition, then use reason to verify them.
  4. Two purposes: to verify the prophets' teaching for ourselves, and to know how to answer those who deny it.
  5. "You are My witnesses": the strongest miracle is the manna, continuous for forty years, impossible to repeat and impossible to fake before one's own children. Rav Saadiah made the "Kuzari argument" before the Kuzari.
  6. Why miracles, if reason can get there? Because reason takes time and not everyone succeeds. The miracles give everyone the truth through their senses at once. Then we can work it out at leisure, like weighing the coins first and doing the sums later.
