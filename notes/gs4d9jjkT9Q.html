Topic: The ninth principle: the Torah will never be replaced or changed Source: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek, and Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah, chapter 9 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's monthly Sunday-night series in Wembley on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, January 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two parts to the principle

This class completed the second group of principles, those about revelation. The Rambam writes that the Torah of Moshe will not be abrogated, no other law will come from God, and nothing may be added to it or taken from it, "neither in the written nor the oral law".

The Rabbi divided the principle into two parts: 1. Judaism is eternal. It has not been replaced by any later religion, and here the Rambam has Christianity and Islam in mind. 2. The Torah does not change from within. Its laws are not added to or taken away.

The two do not have to go together, he noted, but the Rambam sees them as one idea. The Ani Maamin ("this Torah will not be changed") and the Yigdal ("God will never amend nor exchange His law") mostly reflect the first part.

Part one: "a new covenant"?

Although the Rambam lived among Muslims, he knew Christian doctrine well. The very idea of a "New Testament" contradicts this principle, which is why, the Rabbi said, Jews find the term "Old Testament" offensive.

He read the main Christian proof text, Yirmiyahu 31: "Behold, days are coming... when I will make a new covenant (brit hadashah) with the house of Israel and the house of Judah." The Christian reading is that this foretells a new law that makes the old one obsolete.

First he showed that the prophets often speak of Israel breaking the covenant without God abandoning it. Yehezkel says "you have broken My covenant" and then "I will remember My covenant... an everlasting covenant". The curses of Behukotai, which we read quietly, end: "Even so, when they are in the land of their enemies, I will not reject them... to break My covenant with them."

Then he gave five points from the classic commentaries on the passage, such as the Radak and the Mesudat David: 1. The covenant is with the house of Israel and the house of Judah, not with all the nations. 2. "They shall all know Me", with no need to teach one another. That has not happened, then or now. 3. By the time of Jesus, the house of Israel, the ten tribes, had long been exiled. The Radak calls this one of the strongest answers. 4. "I will write it on their hearts": the same Torah, placed within them, not a new one. 5. The passage describes the days of Mashiah, when the tribes return, all know God, and Israel is firmly bound to the covenant. Malakhi, the last of the prophets, says: "Remember the Torah of Moshe My servant... behold I send you Eliyahu the prophet."

He added that the "virgin" proof from Yeshayahu fails too: alma means a young woman, while a virgin is betulah. With a smile he told of a missionary at his door who walked off after ten minutes. And theologically: if God revealed Himself to a nation of about two million, why would He cancel that through a revelation to one person?

A God of all humanity, one revelation

He then set a "spot the difference" exercise using two editions of a passage from Rabbi Sacks's book The Dignity of Difference. The first edition said that God has spoken to mankind through Judaism to the Jews, Christianity to Christians and Islam to Muslims. After an outcry, the revised edition said that God made a covenant with a singular people, which does not exclude other peoples finding their own relationship with God within the Noahide laws.

The Rabbi said he fully agrees with the second version. We want non-Jews to find God through the seven Noahide laws, and the Temple was "a house of prayer for all peoples". But there was one public, national revelation, and to say that God revealed different religions to different peoples undermines this principle. "There is no concept of moral relativism in Judaism."

Someone suggested that the "new covenant" might refer to the priesthood after the Temple. He answered that the Kohanim are always called the house of Levi, never the house of Judah, and that everyone knowing God did not happen then either.

Part two: the Torah does not bend to the times

The Torah does not change according to the values of the society around us. The Rabbi said we are lucky to live in a free and developed society and should enjoy it, but freedom brings the danger of reading the Torah through Western glasses. He described two ways people escape the tension: - Reform: whatever does not fit Western values is dropped. - Total isolation: the outside world is rejected entirely.

He called both a way of avoiding the conflict. The balanced path, which he said Rav Kook stresses again and again, is a synthesis. Even within Modern Orthodoxy, he preferred to look at the world through Torah glasses, rather than at the Torah through Western glasses.

Where the two truly cannot be reconciled, the Torah does not change. His example was homosexuality. "There are times when we come to a dead end": Orthodox Jews "absolutely cannot reconcile the West's promotion of homosexuality with our Torah."

He added that we do not wait to understand everything before keeping the Torah. "Taste and see that God is good." At Sinai we said "we will do" before "we will understand". The beauty of Shabbat is understood by living it.

The Rambam and Rabbi Yosef Albo

The Rambam's source (Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 9:1) is "you shall not add to it nor take away from it" and "it is not in heaven". A prophet who claims, even with signs and wonders, that God sent him to add or remove a misvah, or that the misvot were only for a time, is a false prophet.

Rabbi Yosef Albo, in the 15th century, first gives reasons: God does not change, the nation endures, and truth does not change. But then he argues with the Rambam. God did change a law: Adam was given only plants to eat, and after the Flood Noah was permitted meat. Albo suggests that people needed an outlet for violence, and it was better to kill animals than people. People change, so the law could too. How then would we know? Only through a prophet greater than Moshe, which cannot happen, or a revelation at least as public as Sinai. "I see no evidence or necessity," Albo concludes, that this should be a principle of faith.

So for the Rambam the Torah cannot change. For Albo it almost certainly will not, but it could in theory.

And when Mashiah comes?

There are startling sources. A Midrash speaks of a new Torah in the days of Mashiah. The Talmud (Kiddushin) says that mamzerim will be pure in the time to come. In explaining the halachic meaning of a mamzer, the Rabbi stressed that a child born outside marriage is not a mamzer and is "absolutely perfect". A Midrash on "Hashem matir asurim" even says forbidden things will be permitted. He noted that such Midrashim are not all authoritative, and academics argue that they help explain why so many fell for Shabbetai Sevi, who cancelled Tisha BeAv.

He ended with two points: - Laws that seem irrelevant, such as slavery, still carry their principle. He understood the laws of slavery as a warning against abusing power, which is as relevant as ever. - Rabbinic laws do not change the Torah. The Rambam says the Rabbis may not add to the Torah, but they may build fences around it, as long as they say so. A rabbi who announced that chicken with milk is forbidden by the Torah would transgress "do not add". That is why we always ask whether a prohibition is from the Torah or from the Rabbis.

Summary

  • The ninth principle has two parts: no other religion replaces the Torah, and the Torah itself does not change.
  • The "new covenant" of Yirmiyahu speaks of the days of Mashiah, when the same Torah is written on our hearts.
  • We respect other faiths within the Noahide laws, but there was one national revelation.
  • The Torah is not reinterpreted to fit the values of the day; balance means looking at the world through Torah eyes.
  • Rabbi Yosef Albo held that change was possible in theory; rabbinic laws are fences, never additions to the Torah.
