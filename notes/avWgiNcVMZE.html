Topic: Why Hilchot Shabbat matters, how the course will run, spending money to honour Shabbat, and washing clothes before Shabbat

Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 242:1-2, with the Mishnah Berurah

Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 1 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (recorded around 2013-15, exact date not stated).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why a group should learn Hilchot Shabbat

The Rabbi opens with the last paragraph of the Mishnah Berurah's introduction to Hilchot Shabbat. It says that anyone who fears the word of Hashem should arrange groups to learn these laws, so that people do not stumble on Shabbat. The Rabbi notes two points: Moshe Rabbeinu did this for Israel, and it brings people closer to Hashem. So the class will meet every Monday night and work through Hilchot Shabbat from beginning to end, so that people know what is permitted and what is forbidden. Often something we think is forbidden is permitted, and the other way round.

He gives a brief word of thanks to the participants, to Rabbi Bassous for backing and encouraging the shiur, and to his parents.

Five reasons Shabbat deserves so much attention

Why is Shabbat singled out, when people are far less careful about other laws? The Rabbi offers five reasons.

First, Shabbat is the foundation of faith. It recalls that Hashem created the world in six days and rested on the seventh. The Gemara says that someone who desecrates Shabbat is as if he denied the whole Torah, because he denies the Creation that Shabbat commemorates. Idolatry has the same status.

Second, Shabbat has a great number of laws. At Marah, before Sinai, Israel was given Shabbat, Parah Adumah and Dinim (civil law). The Maharal explains that these prepare us for three parts of learning. Shabbat, a few verses that become a whole tractate, trains breadth of knowledge (beki'ut). Civil law trains analysis, telling one case from another (iyun). Parah Adumah, which even Shelomoh HaMelekh could not fathom, stands for depth. This is the sense of Hokhmah, Binah and Daat. Hokhmah is knowing the fact, Binah is deriving one thing from another, and Daat is going deep. Shabbat is the example of a subject with so many details that we must keep going over it.

Third, the Ben Ish Hai on the word "Zakhor" (remember the Shabbat day). During the week we do the 39 melakhot so often that they become our nature. The Torah tells us to remember that Shabbat is different, and to step out of our weekday habits.

Fourth, the Ramban says that learning the laws of Shabbat during the week fulfils the misvah of remembering Shabbat. We might have thought "Zakhor" was only Kiddush on Friday night.

Fifth, the Zohar says that all the days of the week are blessed from Shabbat. It looks as if we earn during the six days and close the shop on the seventh. The Zohar teaches the reverse: Shabbat is the source of the blessing for the whole week. The better we keep it, the more we are fit to receive that blessing.

The two parts of Hilchot Shabbat, and how the course will run

Hilchot Shabbat has two main parts. One is the 39 melakhot, learned from the work of the Mishkan, which the Torah places next to the commandment of Shabbat. Whatever was done to build the Mishkan is what is forbidden on Shabbat. The other is Kavod and Oneg Shabbat, honouring and enjoying Shabbat. Kavod is done before Shabbat (for example buying for Shabbat) and Oneg on Shabbat itself (good food and a pleasant atmosphere). The course starts with Kavod and Oneg because it is easier and comes up every week.

The method: read the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema inside, with the Mishnah Berurah, and add the practical rulings for Sephardim from the Ben Ish Hai, the Kaf HaHayim, Rav Ovadia Yoseph and Hakham Ben Zion where the topics arise. The Shulhan Arukh runs from Siman 242 to 344, about 100 simanim. The Rabbi will not follow the order of the book. He will take a subject, such as a melakhah, and gather every law on it from different simanim, so that the picture is complete.

He also explains the page. The Shulhan Arukh is by the Beit Yoseph (the authority for Sephardim) and the Rema (for Ashkenazim). The Mishnah Berurah, written about a hundred years ago by the Hafetz Hayim, sifts the many Acharonim and gives only what we need to act. The Biur Halakhah goes deeper, and the Shaar HaSiyun gives sources.

Siman 242: spending money to honour Shabbat

The Mishnah Berurah introduces Oneg Shabbat as a misvah from the Prophets ("and you shall call Shabbat a delight"), though the Rabbi explains it is at least rooted in the Torah's "Mikra Kodesh", which Hazal read as clean clothes and good food and drink. The authorities argue whether Oneg is from the Torah or rabbinic, but the Rabbi says it makes no practical difference: it is very important, and Hazal said that whoever enjoys Shabbat receives a boundless inheritance.

The Shulhan Arukh says that even someone who takes charity should honour Shabbat from his own money. There seem to be three statements in the Gemara. One says to spend as much as possible. A second says that a small fish is enough. A third says "make your Shabbat like a weekday and do not depend on people." The Mishnah Berurah reconciles them like this:

  • A person who has means should spend generously.
  • Someone with the money for only two meals should not take charity for the third. He should eat two meals and not depend on others.
  • Someone who is already receiving three meals (from his own money or from charity) and has a little money left should spend it on honouring Shabbat, even if that means he may have to ask for help later in the week.

A person should also be careful during the week so that he has money for Shabbat. The Mishnah Berurah cites the teaching that a person's income is fixed at Rosh Hashanah, apart from what he spends on Shabbat, Yom Tov and Torah learning, which is repaid. The Rabbi adds that people who spend carelessly on luxuries can end up in real trouble.

Further points:

  • It is good to have at least two dishes on Shabbat, for example egg salad and chicken.
  • It is good to eat fish at all three meals, not only at Seudah Shelishit. But Oneg means enjoyment: if someone dislikes fish, he does not have to force himself. If sellers raise the price of fish by more than a third, stop buying it for a few weeks, because you can enjoy Shabbat with other foods.
  • If someone can borrow, he should. The Rabbi adds that this depends on having some plan for repaying and on the person's level of bitahon. Someone with no plan should think carefully and use his own judgement.
  • A questioner asked about credit cards. The Mishnah Berurah allows borrowing with interest for Shabbat or a misvah meal, but only in a permitted way (for example from a non-Jew). Some Acharonim are more lenient, but the Mishnah Berurah is not.
  • If you owe a worker his wages (which must be paid within the same day or night, about 12 hours), pay him first rather than spending that money on Shabbat, unless he agrees to wait. He may, for example, postpone collecting a repaired item until he has the money.

Washing clothes before Shabbat

Ezra enacted that clothes be washed on Thursday. There are two readings. One is that there is no duty to wash, but if you do, do it on Thursday and not Friday, which is for preparing for Shabbat. The other, which the Mishnah Berurah follows, is that the enactment is also to have clean clothes. This means clothes that are stained or that have become sweaty, such as underwear and shirts. A suit that looks clean need not be washed every week.

The Ben Ish Hai says Thursday is not exact, and Wednesday is also fine, and Thursday night is also acceptable. Today we have machines, so does the rule still apply? A ruling the Rabbi cites says that if you have both a washing machine and a dryer, you may wash on Friday, as it takes little effort. If you must hang the washing out, it is better to do it on Thursday.

Practical summary

  • Learning Hilchot Shabbat matters because Shabbat is the foundation of faith, has many laws, and brings blessing to the whole week.
  • Spend as much as you can to honour Shabbat. Do not take charity for a third meal if you only have the money for two, but if you have three meals and a little money, spend it.
  • Aim for at least two dishes at each meal and fish at all three meals, unless you dislike it or the price has risen sharply.
  • Borrow for Shabbat if you can realistically repay. Any interest must be taken in a permitted way.
  • Pay a worker on time before spending on Shabbat.
  • Wash sweaty or dirty clothes on Wednesday or Thursday rather than Friday, unless you have a washing machine and a dryer.
