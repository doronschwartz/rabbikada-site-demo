Topic: The Three Weeks, the Nine Days, and Tisha BeAv falling straight after Shabbat Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Tisha BeAv, with the rulings of Rav Ovadia Yosef Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 6 July 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Mourning that builds up

A few days before Rosh Hodesh Av, the Rabbi explained the shape of this period. Mourning for a relative is most intense at the start and eases over the year. With the Temple it is the opposite: we have to build our understanding of what we have lost, so the restrictions grow stronger as Tisha BeAv approaches.

The Three Weeks. For Sephardim very little is halachically forbidden. The Shulhan Arukh's main concern is to avoid unnecessary danger, giving the example of a teacher who used to strike pupils (as was then common) and refrains during these weeks. Swimming is fine. The only issue is swimming somewhere risky without a lifeguard when you are not a strong swimmer. Some also avoid saying Shehehiyanu.

The Nine Days

That year Rosh Hodesh Av fell on Shabbat and the fast on Sunday, so the restricted days were really Sunday to Friday. What is restricted is anything done for joy, entertainment or luxury beyond what is needed.

  • Building. Building for joy is out; a family that needs an extension or building work in a new home may go ahead, even starting during the Nine Days.
  • Weddings wait until afterwards.
  • Shopping. New clothes, shoes or a car as an upgrade should wait, unless the item is on sale and the price will go up, or you genuinely need it now.
  • Social events. Barbecues and parties should not take place.

He addressed the Euro 2020 final, which England might reach that Sunday. Perhaps one can follow the game, but a gathering to watch it with drinks and laughter is not appropriate, even if it comes once in a lifetime. Someone remarked that it might be a sad night anyway. He answered that the joy lies in the excitement itself, not the result: "if you were just interested in England winning then you would just hear the results at the end."

Meat and wine. From the Gemara, meat and wine are forbidden only at the final meal before the fast. The custom to refrain during the Nine Days is old; even the Rambam, who rarely records customs, mentions it. Because it is a custom, it gives way in need:

  • A pregnant woman, someone weak or someone ill who needs meat may eat it.
  • If you made a berakhah on meat by mistake, eat a little rather than let the berakhah be in vain.
  • Shabbat leftovers that cannot be frozen or given to children and would otherwise be thrown away may be eaten, as long as you did not cook extra on purpose.

No "week in which Tisha BeAv falls." Tisha BeAv can only fall on Sunday, Tuesday, Thursday or Shabbat. In 2021 it fell on Sunday, so there was no such week, and for Sephardim laundry, haircuts, shaving and washing were permitted until the Friday before.

Seudah mafseket on Shabbat

Normally the last meal before the fast has its own laws. When it falls on Shabbat, none of these apply. The Shulhan Arukh, following the Rambam and the Gemara, allows even a feast like King Shelomoh's: meat, wine, friends and a zimmun. Even someone who will not fast still eats this meal.

The hour between Shabbat and Tisha BeAv

"This is the most complicated hour we have in the Jewish calendar." The time between sunset and nightfall is always a doubt, which is why Shabbat lasts 25 hours. That week in London, sunset was at 9.09pm and nightfall at 10.11pm. In that hour it might still be Shabbat, or it might already be Tisha BeAv, so we must respect both.

The key is the difference between active and passive:

  • Stop eating and drinking at sunset. Stopping is passive, so it does not take away from Shabbat. Birkat HaMazon, even with a zimmun, may be said afterwards.
  • Do not take off leather shoes, sit on the floor or change out of Shabbat clothes until nightfall. Those are active steps against Shabbat. Someone who was already wearing slippers at home simply carries on.
  • Wash hands only up to the knuckles after the bathroom.
  • No social greetings from sunset.
  • Learning Torah. Beyond what is permitted on Tisha BeAv, one should not learn. Learning the sad subjects (Iyov, parts of Yirmiyahu, the laws of mourning) is fine, but not as a way of getting into the mood of Tisha BeAv while it may still be Shabbat.

"Until nine o'clock it's Shabbat... you sing zemirot, it's happy. And then an hour later we're expected to sit on the floor and to cry and to mourn." Torah sometimes asks that range of emotions of us.

At nightfall. Say Barukh hamavdil bein kodesh lehol before doing any weekday activity, then change and remove leather shoes. There is no Havdalah over wine and no spices, since spices are meant to restore the soul. Only the berakhah on the fire is said, usually in synagogue; women at home who are not going say it themselves. Men add Attah Honantanu in Arvit. Havdalah over wine is made on Sunday night, without spices or fire.

Someone who must eat on the fast, on doctor's advice, makes Havdalah over grape juice first. Family members can listen, but they must hear Havdalah again on Sunday night.

The fast itself

There are five main restrictions: eating and drinking, washing, anointing, leather shoes and marital relations. Tisha BeAv adds learning Torah and greeting, plus the custom not to work.

  • Greeting. Rav Ovadia Yosef allows "good morning" or "good night". What is forbidden is sheelat shalom, the social "how are you, how is the family".
  • Who may eat. Someone bedridden, or in pain to the point of not functioning, may eat and drink what is needed. Anyone with Covid symptoms or a positive test should not fast. Daily pills may be taken with a little water if needed.
  • Pregnant and nursing women. The Shulhan Arukh rules they fast. The Rabbi told how Rav Sadka Hussein, sent from Aleppo to Baghdad in 1743 after a plague killed many of its hakhamim, instituted that they do not fast, because of the heat and their condition. There is what to rely on, but it is disputed, so they should seek rabbinic guidance.
  • Washing. Only washing for pleasure is forbidden. Hand sanitiser in public, washing dirty hands, and getting hands wet while cooking for after the fast are all fine.
  • Creams for pleasure are forbidden; medical creams and deodorant are fine.
  • Leather shoes. There is room to allow them after midday for someone who must travel or meet non-Jews. Children, who do not fast, are still taught not to wear leather shoes, since it is public.

In the questions he rejected the idea that everything relaxes after midday. "There's no basis for that." Only the custom of sitting low ends at midday, and even that is a custom kept mainly by the young and able. Women may bake for after the fast.

What to think about

Torah is restricted, but someone who would otherwise waste the day is probably better off opening a book. He told of the Rogachover Gaon, who said he could not stop himself learning even on Tisha BeAv.

The day is for keeping our minds on the exile. One thing the Rabbi said we should mourn is the loss of the Sanhedrin. With a Sanhedrin, the Hakhamim could interpret Torah for the times within its principles. Since the Talmud, the last authority accepted by all Israel, we can no longer do that. His example was the second day of Yom Tov: a custom from when the calendar was uncertain, which he was sure a Sanhedrin today would change, but which we must keep.

He ended with the words golah (exile) and geulah (redemption), almost the same letters. Exile is often the prelude to redemption.

Practical summary

  • Three Weeks: avoid needless danger. Nine Days: avoid entertainment, luxury and social gatherings.
  • Not eating meat is a custom: it gives way for illness, pregnancy, waste of Shabbat leftovers, or a berakhah already made.
  • When Tisha BeAv follows Shabbat: stop eating at sunset, but wait until nightfall to change, remove shoes or sit low.
  • At nightfall say Barukh hamavdil; the fire berakhah only; Havdalah on wine on Sunday night.
  • Greetings like "good morning" are permitted; pregnant and nursing women should ask a rav.
