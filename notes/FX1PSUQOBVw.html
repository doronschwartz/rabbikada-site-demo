Topic: Karet and the fate of the wicked in the Rambam's view of Olam HaBa, and why the World to Come is not spelled out in the Torah Sources: The Rambam, Hilchot Teshuvah 8:1 and 3:6 Shiur: The second class of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on the soul at Jackie Benjamin House, Monday 13 January 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Recap: what Olam HaBa is

Dayan Kada began with a longer recap than usual, since half the group had not been there the week before and several people had texted with questions. The series sets out the Rambam's view of the afterlife: Olam HaBa, Mashiah, the resurrection, reincarnation and more, terms people use without knowing how they fit together.

Olam HaBa is not physical. There is no eating, drinking or body. Rabbinic images such as the great feast of the Leviathan are metaphors for deeper things.

The reward of a misvah is the misvah. Judaism does not have arbitrary reward and punishment; even "reward" is the wrong word. A better word is consequence. Each misvah and each hour of Torah study refines a person's moral and intellectual identity, and the more refined a person is, the more able he is to enjoy Olam HaBa.

His analogy: think of someone you admire and imagine meeting them. The thrill of the first few minutes normally fades; in Olam HaBa it never does. And the more similar you are to the one at the centre of the stadium, the closer your seat. Asked whether being made in God's image means we are already there, he said that part of us is divine and part is dust, and life is the struggle and the choice between them. Eating, sleeping and other pleasures are not the enemy: done in the right framework, they become part of a good life.

Two perfections. Moral perfection comes through the misvot, and we need revelation to know what good deeds are. Intellectual perfection comes through Torah and through thinking about Hashem. A person who denies God has no entrance to the stadium at all; how can he enjoy what he never believed in?

Asked about non-Jews, he said that a non-Jew who keeps the seven Noahide laws has a share in the World to Come. Many authorities place him further back, but the Rambam holds a non-Jew can reach the same level; the Jew simply has more misvot and more guidance. The Rambam also adds, as a minority view, that the Noahide laws must be kept because God commanded them.

A digression: is faith a leap?

A question about whether belief in God is a "leap of faith" drew a long and spirited exchange. Dayan Kada rejected the idea firmly. Emunah has been mistranslated as "belief" under Christian influence. "Neeman" in the verse said about Avraham means loyal, unswerving. Judaism has no leap of faith: things have to make sense, and one believes because, after thinking it through, it is true beyond reasonable doubt, which leads to loyalty to Hashem. No one can prove God exists, he agreed, but that is not the same as believing something that makes no sense.

What of the misvot whose reasons we do not know? If ninety of a hundred laws make sense, it is reasonable to keep the other ten from the same source. And according to the Rambam every misvah has a reason; a hok is one whose reason is hard to see.

Why not create us ready-made?

If Olam HaBa is the ultimate good, why not create us already in it? Because goodness that is earned cannot be compared with goodness that is granted. More deeply, Hashem is intrinsically good. When we choose good, we become intrinsically good ourselves, more like Hashem, and therefore closer.

What happens to the wicked?

For the purposes of the class, "wicked" meant someone who denies God, adding that many such people are lovely people. The Rambam (Hilchot Teshuvah 8:1) writes that the good stored for the righteous is the life of the World to Come, life without death and good without evil. "The retribution of the wicked is that they will not merit this life; rather they will be cut off and die."

This is karet: the person dies and his identity ceases. The Rishonim asked the obvious question. What then stops a person from indulging every desire for 120 years? He dies, and is not even there to know what he missed.

Someone asked whether such a person would rise at the resurrection. No, he said. But he stressed that very few people fall into this category. The Mishnah lists those who lose their share, but it takes something extremely serious, and teshuvah is possible for everything, even the most egregious sins. "The vast majority of people will have a portion in the World to Come."

"Judged forever"

Yet the Rambam also writes (Hilchot Teshuvah 3:6) that these people "are cut off, and they are judged for their great wickedness and sins forever". How can someone who no longer exists be judged forever?

The class worked towards an answer, and Dayan Kada drew it together: the person's identity ceases to exist, but an element of his nefesh remains, conscious enough to know what it is missing, and that is the punishment that endures. "It all comes together."

Then a harder question. Hashem is good; that is a fundamental principle, as fundamental as His being one and without a body. So why would a good God keep someone in such a state forever? Why not simply end him?

The answer is human nature. If the worst outcome were simply to cease, there would be nothing to stop a person living however he liked and then disappearing. Hashem wants us to earn the good by choosing it, and real choice needs real consequences.

Someone pressed: why should finite sins bring an infinite consequence? Dayan Kada acknowledged the question and said he was not greatly troubled by it. One answer offered in the room was that we, in bodies, cannot really grasp infinity; we are warned, but only after death do we understand what it means.

Why is Olam HaBa not in the Torah?

Someone said the subject frightened her: if it is so important, why does the Torah not warn us explicitly? Rav Saadiah Gaon has a whole essay on this. Dayan Kada gave the answer he prefers, Rabbi Sacks's: too much focus on the afterlife leads people to neglect this world. Hashem wants us to live fully here, morally and intellectually, and to make this world the best it can be; the consequences follow. We give up our lives only to avoid murder, idolatry and forbidden relations. A religion fixated on the next world, he said, can make this life seem worthless, and martyrdom attractive.

The group argued the point. One participant suggested a different answer: a focus on reward would turn every good deed into self-interest, rather than helping someone because it is right. Dayan Kada welcomed it. We do good because it is good, and the better life afterwards is the result, not the motive. Asked how we know about the World to Come at all, he answered: the oral tradition, just as the Torah never says what tefillin are.

At the end, someone said that when something goes wrong after a sin, she sometimes thinks "better now than later". He approved: the Gemara says that one who suffers should examine his deeds. How Hashem intervenes in individual lives, he said, is a subject for another class.

Summary

  • For the Rambam, Olam HaBa is purely spiritual, and a person's place in it is the consequence of the moral and intellectual refinement he has achieved.
  • Those who deny God, and a few other very grave cases, are cut off (karet), but teshuvah is always possible and most people have a share.
  • "Judged forever" means that an element of the soul remains, aware of what it has lost.
  • A good God allows this because without real consequences there is no real choice.
  • The Torah does not spell out Olam HaBa so that we live fully in this world and do good for its own sake.
