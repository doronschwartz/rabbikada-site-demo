Class Notes — Rabbi Daniel Kada

Sefer: מורה נבוכים / Moreh Nebuchim — The Guide for the Perplexed Author: רבינו משה בן מימון — Rambam (Maimonides) This Class: Background, biography, context, translations, controversies, and structure of the Moreh

Context: Why Now?

  • Over the past 4–5 years, Tuesday evenings have covered many aspects of the Rambam's writings:
  • Sefer Madda, Hilchot De'ot, Hilchot Talmud Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah
  • Letters: Iggeret HaShmad, letter on resurrection, letter to Rabbi Ovadyah the convert
  • Introductions: to Mishneh Torah, to Pirkei Avot (Eight Chapters), to Perek Chelek (Sanhedrin)
  • All of these trace back to one source: the Moreh Nevuchim
  • The Moreh is where Rambam's philosophy and theology ultimately originates
  • It's a daunting book — but it's time to deal with "the real stuff"
  • Expected timeline: 2–3 years, going through systematically chapter by chapter

Biography of the Rambam

Birth and Early Years

  • Born 14th of Nisan, either 1135 or 1138 (evidence now leans toward 1138)
  • Born in Cordoba, Spain
  • Father: Rabbi Maimon ben Yosef — a Dayan in Spain
  • Rabbi Maimon was a student of the Ri Migash (Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash, 1077–1141, Seville)
  • The Ri Migash was a student of the Rif (Rabbi Yitzchak Alfasi, 1013–1103, Algeria/Fez)
  • Almost unbroken chain: Rif → Ri Migash → Rabbi Maimon → Rambam
  • Rambam writes: "In very few places I disagreed with the Rif, because normally the Rif is always correct"
  • Also exposed to philosophy from a young age — through the rise of the Islamic school of the Mutakallimun
  • Mutakallimun = from the Arabic word "kalam" (the word / literalism)
  • Developed in the 8th century → Greek works (Aristotle, medical texts) translated into Arabic
  • Both Islamic and Jewish thinkers benefited from this

Leaving Cordoba (1148)

  • Around age 10 (or 13) — family leaves Cordoba due to the Almohad persecution (Islamic Crusaders)
  • Sojourned around the Iberian Peninsula for 12 years (1148–1160)
  • Spent time in Seville — met a student of Ibn Bajjah (expert astronomer, quoted in the Moreh)
  • Developed interests in astronomy and other disciplines during this period

Fez (1160–1165)

  • Family moves to Fez for five years — father still alive
  • Big question: why move to Fez where the Almohads were in control?
  • Unclear whether he could live a full Jewish life there
  • Wrote the famous Iggeret HaShmad (Essay on Forced Conversions) — in his early 20s
  • Written with confidence and sarcasm
  • Some suggest a hint that he himself lived a crypto life — Rav Shilat completely rejects this
  • At age 23: begins writing his Commentary on the Mishnah (first major rabbinic work)
  • Completes it seven years later in Egypt at age 30
  • Already gaining a high reputation in his 20s

Israel (1165)

  • In May 1165 — arrives in Akko with his father and brother David
  • Stormy voyage — Rambam promised:
  • Two days of fasting for the whole family
  • One day of personal meditation and seclusion
  • Travels from Akko → Yerushalayim → Hevron
  • Spends approximately one year in Israel
  • Then leaves for Egypt (reasons unclear — perhaps financial, perhaps too difficult)
  • Question from the shiur: How can he live in Egypt? Torah says don't go back there!
  • The Radbaz has a teshuvah with 3–4 answers on this

Egypt — Fustat (1166 onwards)

  • Settles in Fustat (old Cairo) — approximately 4,000 Jews there at the time
  • Spends the rest of his life here
  • Brother David — the businessman of the family
  • Traveled to India, Singapore, and other Far Eastern locations for trade
  • 1177: Shipwreck — David is lost at sea
  • Rambam mourns deeply:

"If not for the Torah which is my delight, and for the scientific matters which let me forget my sorrow — I would have perished in my affliction" (יגעתי בעוני)

  • Some suggest David had been financing Rambam → after his death, Rambam turned to practicing medicine
  • Rabbi Avraham (Rambam's son) names his elder son David — most likely after this uncle

Major Works Written in Egypt

  • Mishneh Torah — halakhic magnum opus, written in Hebrew (took 10 years)
  • Written to be like a "replica of the Mishnah" — just as Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi wrote the Mishnah because Torah was being forgotten
  • Sefer HaMitzvot — enumeration of 613 mitzvot, written in Arabic
  • Many had tried before him — Rambam says they all got it wrong
  • Includes 14 rules for how to properly enumerate the mitzvot
  • Suggestion: read through Sefer HaMitzvot over the two days of Shavuot
  • Commentary on the Mishnah — written in Arabic, translated by others into Hebrew
  • Medical works — 10 books on medicine
  • Most of Rambam's works are in Arabic — the Mishneh Torah is the notable exception (in Hebrew)

Court Physician

  • Eventually becomes physician at the court of Saladin (Muslim ruler)
  • Personal physician to Saladin's son Al-Afdal
  • Had to commute from Fustat to the other side of Cairo daily
  • Writes in a letter: "I barely have time to study — only on Shabbat"
  • By the time he comes home in the evening → people waiting at his door for medical consultations

Death

  • Dies 20th of Tevet, 1204
  • Buried in Teveria (Tiberias)

The Moreh Nevuchim — Background

When Was It Written?

  • Began: 1185 — completed: 1191 (5–6 years)
  • Rambam dies in 1204 → written in the last ~20 years of his life
  • If born 1138 → he begins writing it around age 47
  • Some ideas intended from much earlier:
  • In his Commentary on Mishnah Sanhedrin (age ~23–30): mentions intending to write a book on prophecy → never writes a separate book → incorporates it into the Moreh
  • Also mentions intending to write a book explaining the drashot of the Chachamim → decides against it ("if I explain too clearly it's not good; if I use more allegories, what's the point?")

Who Was It Written For?

  • Primarily for his student: Yosef ben Yehuda ibn Shimon
  • Had been studying under Rambam in Fustat
  • Moved to Aleppo → Rambam decided to write this for him
  • But also intended for a wider audience:
  • Clear indications throughout that he wants philosophically trained people to read it
  • In some places explicitly writes that certain ideas should reach the masses
  • Example: the danger of believing in God's physicality → "teach this to the children, teach this to the women"

Purpose

  • Navigate the conflict between Aristotelian philosophy and Torah tradition
  • For the person who is both well-versed in science/philosophy AND in Torah
  • Deals with: God's essence, unity, incorporeality, knowledge, revelation, prophecy, evil, mitzvot, providence, prayer

The Original Name

  • Rambam writes in Arabic — does NOT call it "Moreh Nevuchim"
  • Original Arabic name: דלאלת אלחאירין — Dalalat al-Ha'irin
  • Meaning: "Direction for those who are confused / lacking direction / misled / trapped"

Translations

Shmuel ibn Tibbon (1165–1232)

  • Translates from Arabic to Hebrew
  • Completed the translation in 1204 — the year of Rambam's death
  • Rambam writes to him in 1199: don't translate word for word
  • "Good translators don't get stuck on word-for-word translation"
  • Similar approach to Aryeh Kaplan (translator of Derech Hashem, the Living Torah Chumash, etc.)
  • Despite this advice, some say ibn Tibbon was too literal
  • Ibn Tibbon is the son of Yehuda ibn Tibbon — who translated:
  • Emunot VeDeot (Saadia Gaon) from Arabic to Hebrew (the "Amar Yehuda" at the start of each essay)
  • Chovot HaLevavot (Rabbeinu Bachya ibn Pakuda) from Arabic to Hebrew
  • The ibn Tibbon dynasty made these Arabic works part of Hebrew literature — incredible contribution

The Name "Moreh Nevuchim"

  • Shmuel ibn Tibbon coins the Hebrew name — not the Rambam
  • The word נבוכים appears twice in Tanach:
  • Shemot 14:3 — Pharaoh says about Bnei Yisrael: כי נבוכים הם בארץ — "they are trapped/confused in the land"
  • Esther 3:15 — והעיר שושן נבוכה — "the city of Shushan was confused"
  • In both cases: the confusion leads to the ultimate redemption (Yam Suf / Purim)
  • Perhaps ibn Tibbon's message: this guide will lead the perplexed from confusion to clarity, just as nevuchim led to geulah

Yehuda Alcharizi (Toledo)

  • Second translation — only 9 years after ibn Tibbon
  • Felt ibn Tibbon was too literal → his translation was more free
  • Rabbi Avraham (Rambam's son) preferred ibn Tibbon's translation
  • Makes sense: Rambam and ibn Tibbon had been communicating directly
  • Ibn Tibbon's version became the accepted one

Latin Translation (1224)

  • Translated into Latin — now accessible to Christians (Dominicans, monks)
  • This directly leads to controversy (see below)

Modern Translations

  • Rabbi Yosef Kapach — great Yemenite leader, translated from Arabic to Hebrew
  • Dr. Michael Schwartz — Israeli professor, more accessible modern Hebrew from Arabic
  • Friedlander — translated from Arabic directly to English (red book: "The Guide for the Perplexed")
  • Same Friedlander who translated Emunot VeDeot from Arabic to English

Controversies Around the Moreh

During Rambam's Lifetime

  • Not much controversy about the Moreh specifically during his life
  • He completes it in 1191, dies in 1204 — doesn't have time to spread widely
  • He DID have controversy about resurrection (techiyat hametim) → wrote Ma'amar Techiyat HaMetim

Controversy #1 — After His Death (Early 1200s)

  • Already in Egypt: Daniel HaBavli challenges both the Mishneh Torah and the Moreh
  • Rabbi Avraham (Rambam's son) writes Milchamot Hashem ("Wars of Hashem") — defending his father
  • Issues included:
  • Rambam's conception of angels
  • His views on prophecy and miracles
  • His statement that he would reinterpret Bereishit if Aristotle had proven the universe was eternal
  • Controversy spreads to Europe — France, Provence

The Burning of 1232 (Montpellier)

  • Agitators showed selections from the Latin translation to the Dominicans
  • Selections were deemed heretical
  • Public burning of the Moreh ordered in Montpellier, 1232
  • A few years later: burning of the Talmud — whether connected, only Hashem knows
  • The Jews would never have done this alone — it was under Dominican oversight

Controversy #2 — 1305

  • Indirectly about the Rambam — not a direct attack on the Moreh
  • Dispute between the Rashba and Abba Mari
  • Issue: people were misunderstanding the Moreh → Rashba proposed a ban on studying philosophy under age 25
  • Many disagreed → a major fight
  • Not the Rambam's fault — but the Moreh was indirectly implicated

Later Critics

  • Rav Yaakov Emden (1700s): wrote that the Moreh cannot have been written by the Rambam
  • "The person who wrote the Mishneh Torah cannot be the same person who wrote this — it must be someone else"
  • Too much "heresy" within it
  • Still, even with critics in every generation → Rambam won
  • Approximately two-thirds of the Shulchan Aruch follows the Rambam's rulings
  • In philosophy: he is the most eminent Jewish philosopher

Structure of the Moreh

Introduction

  • Written to his student Yosef ben Yehuda ibn Shimon
  • To be studied after Pesach

Part One (70+ chapters)

Main theme: God — What God Is and Isn't

  • Strong, elaborate rejection of anthropomorphism
  • All literal physical descriptions of God in Tanach are to be reinterpreted
  • This is the source for everything we've learned elsewhere (Madda, Sanhedrin, etc.)
  • Negative theology — you cannot describe what God IS, only what God is NOT
  • How can you talk about God being "compassionate" if He is infinite and indivisible?
  • When we say Hashem is רחום → we mean He is "not not rachum"
  • Unity of God — philosophical elaboration
  • Importance of intellect in human perfection and understanding God
  • Loosely corresponds to the first five of the 13 Principles (God exists, is one, has no body, is first, no idol worship)
  • Though the fourth principle (God is first) is dealt with in Part Two

Part Two (50+ chapters)

Main theme: The Universe and God's Relationship with It

  • Is the world eternal? — the major question
  • Aristotle's dominant view: the universe is eternal (no beginning)
  • Now vindicated by the Big Bang (Hubble 1929, confirmed in the 1960s) — but Rambam had to grapple with this head-on
  • Rambam rejects Aristotle's view — not because the Torah says so, but because he believes it wasn't philosophically sound
  • Aristotelian cosmology — the spheres having intelligence (complex topic)
  • Conflict between Bereishit and Aristotelian/Platonic views of creation
  • Prophecy — nature, levels, and mechanics of prophetic experience
  • Miracles — what they are and how they operate
  • Angels — conceived as forces of nature, not winged beings

Part Three (50+ chapters)

Main theme: Evil, Ethics, and Mitzvot

  • Why is there evil? — categorized into three types
  • Hashgachah Pratit (Divine Providence) — what it means, how it operates
  • Reasons for the Mitzvot (Ta'amei HaMitzvot)
  • Underlying theme in many mitzvot: weaning us off Avodah Zarah (idol worship)
  • Prayer, ethics, and the purpose of human existence

Key Takeaways

  1. The Moreh is the source of Rambam's philosophy — everything in Mishneh Torah, Commentary on the Mishnah, and the letters traces back here.
  2. Written in Arabic (Dalalat al-Ha'irin) — the Hebrew name "Moreh Nevuchim" is from the translator ibn Tibbon, not from Rambam.
  3. Written for his student Yosef ben Yehuda — but intended for a wider audience.
  4. Purpose: navigate the conflict between Aristotelian philosophy and Torah tradition.
  5. Three parts: God Himself → God and the Universe → God and Humanity.
  6. Controversial from shortly after Rambam's death — but ultimately Rambam won.
  7. A journey of 2–3 years ahead — brick by brick, chapter by chapter.
