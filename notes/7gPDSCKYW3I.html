These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi opened Siman 13, the last major siman of Hilchot Ishut. It is long but not so difficult, and a few shorter simanim follow. It has two main issues:

  • Havhanah (Seifim 1-10): knowing who the child's father is. A woman who is divorced or widowed must wait before remarrying. This is where the waiting period written in every get today comes from.
  • Meuberet umeneket haveirah (Seif 11 to the end): a woman who is pregnant or nursing when she is divorced, and how long she must wait. This is for next time.

Today he covered Seif 1 in full, then Seifim 2 and 3, and glanced at Seif 4.

The sugyah in Yevamot (41a)

The Mishnah says a woman who is widowed or divorced may not marry for three months, so that the yavam and the haluzah can be sorted out and so that the father is known. For a widow the waiting is clear: she might need yibum or halizah, and one does not do halizah where yibum is impossible. What interests us is the waiting between two marriages. The Mishnah gives three views:

Tanna

Rule

The Tanna Kama

All women wait, in all circumstances, even if divorced from erusin only, and even if she cannot conceive. She may not even be betrothed to another man.

Rabbi Yehudah

Only where there is a real concern of pregnancy. A woman who was only betrothed may be betrothed to another, except in Yehudah, where some had relations before nisuin.

Rabbi Yosi

All may be betrothed, except a widow, because of her mourning.

All three agree that the concern of havhanah exists. They differ on how far to take it.

Why? Shmuel against Rava

Shmuel says the reason is lehavhin ben zera lezera: to know whose child it is, with a hint (asmakhta) from "to be your God and the God of your seed after you". We need to know exactly who each person's father is. He applies it even to a convert couple, who also wait three months: there it distinguishes a child conceived in holiness from one who was not.

Rava says it is a gezerah, a precaution against real problems. If she remarries within three months, a child may be thought to be the second husband's when he is the first's. Then, for example, a daughter might marry a son of the first husband, or if the second husband dies leaving "a child" that is really not his, his wife might remarry without halizah.

The Braita calls this waiting takanat havlad, for the child. How does Rava fit that? The Gemara answers that it is still for the child's sake: so that he not come to a forbidden relationship later.

The Rabbi noted that Shmuel's verse is only an asmakhta, and that Rava's is a clear gezerat Hazal. The plain understanding is that the waiting is rabbinic on both views.

The Rambam: takanah and gezerah

The Rambam uses Shmuel's wording, but he calls it a gezerat hakhamim and says it applies even where she cannot conceive, such as an aylonit or an arusah. The Radbaz concludes from this that the Rambam holds a Torah-level law where she can bear children, and a rabbinic gezerah in the other cases. The Rabbi said he does not read the Rambam that way. The Hida (in the Pithei Teshuvah) understands that the Rambam adds "gezerah" to teach that even a woman who cannot bear children must wait.

The Rabbi then brought what he called a beautiful insight, which he said he had checked: in the Mishneh Torah the Rambam uses three different words for rabbinic enactments.

  • Gezerah: normally negative. Do not do this, or something bad may follow. Here, wait three months, or problems may arise.
  • Takanah: normally positive: reading the Megillah, lighting the Hanukkah lights, taking the lulav all seven days (in the Beit HaMikdash). A positive enactment for a benefit.
  • Hanhagah: a practice or custom.

So the basic law here is a takanah: for the benefit of the child, who will know his father. Where a woman can conceive, the Rabbanan said to wait. Then they also made it a gezerah for all cases, even where no child can come, so that the rule will not be confused. When the Rambam says "gezerat hakhamim", he does not mean that before that it was not rabbinic. He means that this is now a gezerah on top of the takanah. He noted that the Gemara itself is precise in this way: it calls it takanat havlad, as a takanah for the child with a gezerah element, so that problems do not come out. Later in the siman the Rambam again calls it a takanah.

The halakhah: three months, in all cases

The Gemara has a doubt among the three Tannaim, but concludes that the halakhah is like the Tanna Kama, as the Gemara in Ketubot also says: she must wait in all circumstances. The Rabbi gave the cases: a 75-year-old woman, a woman using contraception, a husband who is infertile, or a woman who took a pregnancy test. In all of them she waits three months. There are some situations where we can rely on a pregnancy test, but these are in other parts of the siman and a different matter.

How are three months counted? Three months here means 90 days, not three Hebrew months. The 90 days exclude the day of the divorce (or death) and the day of the new marriage, so the total is 92. On a certificate, "92 days" counts the day of the get as day one, so the wedding may take place on the 92nd day, which is 91 days after the get. He compared it to a brit milah: not eight days after birth, but the eighth day.

When do the 90 days begin: the writing or the giving?

The get may be written on one day and given on another, especially through a shaliah. Do we count from the writing (mishat ketivah) or from the giving (mishat netinah)? The argument for the writing: once the get is written they may not have relations, and even yihud is forbidden. If they did, it would be a get yashan.

The Gemara has Rav and Shmuel disagree. The Gemara concludes that the halakhah follows Shmuel, which is the writing. But not all Rishonim had this version of the Gemara:

From

The Rif, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh

The writing (ketivah)

The Rosh and the Rema

The giving (netinah), like Rav

So for the Sefaradim the count is from the writing, and for the Ashkenazim from the giving.

Engagement during the waiting period

The Mishnah says she may not even be betrothed (erusin). A shiddukh does not mean going on a date: it means an agreement, what we would call an engagement party. The Beit Yoseph writes that such an agreement is allowed only if the hatan takes an oath (shavuah) that he will not enter her house until the three months are over. It is interesting that he is so strict, when the real fear is relations, which would also be a problem of niddah if they happen before marriage. The Rabbi could not say why.

The Rema says one may make a shiddukh, but he must not enter her house, and does not mention the oath. The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel read the Rema and the Beit Yoseph as agreeing: an oath is needed. The Helkat Mehokek says it applies also to a nursing woman. One view says the Rema only means: no yihud, and if there is no problem of yihud it is fine. The Arukh HaShulhan does not take that on.

In practice. If this came up today, the Rabbi would have the hatan accept upon himself very clearly that he will not enter her house, even with guards or in a manner that avoids yihud, which would still be problematic. In practice, he said, this is not really done nowadays.

Seif 2: after halizah

If the yavam did halizah, she does not wait three further months from that date. She counts three months from the husband's death. The Gemara explains: if we rely on the halizah to free her, we are saying there was no concern that they had relations, since otherwise yibum would not have been permitted at all. If we allow yibum after three months, then kol sheken we allow her to marry another.

But if the husband died, and a month later the yavam also died, before any halizah, she must count three months from the yavam's death. In that case, we have not relied on halizah, and the concern of the yavam having had relations with her remains.

The Pithei Teshuvah notes that this applies even today, when the Ashkenazim for many centuries and the Sefaradim in recent times do not do yibum: if a man died childless and his brother did halizah, she counts from the death; if the brother who was to do halizah died first, she waits another three months.

Seif 3: pregnant by another man, then betrothed

The Rabbi called Seifim 3 and 4 straightforward. In Seif 3 a woman had relations with Reuven outside marriage, became pregnant, and then was betrothed (kiddushin) to Shimon. She still waits, although here the father is known. The Rosh gives the plain reason, that it is the same takanah and it makes no difference. The Helkat Mehokek adds his own: people who see her marry Shimon, without knowing about Reuven, will think she is pregnant by Shimon and is breaking the 90-day waiting. He is not concerned about who people think the father is, only that they should not think she violates the takanah. The Rabbi presented this with some hesitation. In Seif 4, where the same husband remarries his own wife, everyone knows, so there is no problem.

Seifim 5-10 deal with pregnancy outside marriage and how long she waits. From Seif 11 to the end is the second takanah, for a pregnant or nursing woman, for next time.

Practical summary

  • A divorced or widowed woman waits three months before remarrying (or even being betrothed), for the child's sake and as a precaution.
  • The waiting applies in all cases, even a woman who cannot conceive, contraception, an infertile husband, or a pregnancy test (with exceptions later in the siman).
  • Three months means 90 days not counting the day of the get and the day of the wedding, so 92 days in all.
  • Sefaradim count from the writing of the get; Ashkenazim from the giving.
  • Where the yavam did halizah, she counts from the husband's death; if he died before halizah, from his death.
  • An engagement party during the waiting is allowed only with the hatan's clear commitment not to enter her house, and in practice it is not generally done.
