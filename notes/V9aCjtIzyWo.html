Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 301, seifim 15-20 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 20 June 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seifim 15-16: An artificial foot and supports

After Shavuot the Rabbi returned to Siman 301. This class draws practical rulings about walking sticks, wheelchairs and guide dogs from principles in the Shulhan Arukh.

Seif 15: a person with an amputated leg may not go out with a kav, a hollow wooden foot into which he puts his stump. He does not walk with it; he still uses his stick. It is only there so that he does not look amputated. Why is it not a takhshit? Because an adornment is something that beautifies you, not something that just stops you looking odd.

Prosthetic legs today. The Rabbi said he had looked and not found this discussed. If a modern prosthetic gives any help in walking, stability or comfort, it is part of the body and permitted, just as a walking stick is for someone who needs it. A purely cosmetic one would be a problem by the plain Gemara.

Seif 16: someone who cannot walk and pushes himself along on hand supports may not use them on Shabbat, since they are not fixed to him and may fall, and he will carry them. Leather pads sewn onto the thighs, for someone who moves on his thighs, are permitted, since they are permanently attached.

The Rema: Slippers, bare feet and a reminder of Shabbat

The Rema permits wooden clogs. On slippers there are two opinions; some forbid them because they slip off. The Rabbi said today's slippers, and crocs worn with the strap at the back, are fairly stable. Rav Ovadia Yosef permits them, since this is a doubt in a rabbinic law. Slippers that keep falling off should not be worn out.

The Rema also says not to walk barefoot where people do not normally do so, for the honour of Shabbat. And, quoting the Kolbo, one should not go out on Shabbat in exactly one's weekday clothes, so as to have a reminder (zekher) that it is Shabbat.

This matters most on holiday, walking to the beach on a hot Shabbat afternoon in shorts and crocs, or keeping a second day of Yom Tov in Israel, where everything around you is weekday. "It's very easy to forget." Someone once wrote that one should wear a tie on the second day in Israel; the Rabbi thought Yom Tov clothes are enough, but agreed that where one is likely to forget, one should have something to remind oneself. A special tie for Shabbat would do. Asked whether this binds Sefaradim, he said the Kolbo, an anonymous European work of the 1300s, quoted by the Rema and not by the Shulhan Arukh, is not binding, but it is good practice.

Seif 17: Walking sticks

A lame person who cannot walk without a stick may go out with it, even if it is not tied to him, since he needs it to walk: it is like his shoe. If he can walk without it and only uses it for extra support, it is forbidden. Mishnah Berurah 64: an elderly man who walks around his house without a stick and takes one outside for support may not.

Three questions: 1. I can walk short distances, but not the twenty minutes to shul without my stick. 2. I am going to climb a mountain where I need walking poles, but I can walk to the foot of it without them. 3. It is icy and I am afraid of slipping.

On the third, the Mishnah Berurah brings the Taz who permits a stick, since without it one would be afraid to walk, though the Mishnah Berurah is unsure. The Rabbi said we can rely on the Taz, and by extension the same applies to the first two cases: whatever enables you to go out may be used, even for stretches where you do not strictly need it. Someone added that an elderly person who could manage without a cane, but is genuinely afraid to go out without it, is similar. The Rabbi agreed, provided it is an honest reflection: "Am I using it just because it's more comfortable, or am I genuinely afraid?"

A stick in the grass. The Biur Halacha asks about a stick with a pointed end making holes in a lawn, a derivative of ploughing (horesh). It is a pesik reisha, inevitable. The Biur Halacha is unsure, but for Sefaradim it is permitted. Making a hole deliberately to plant in would be a Torah prohibition, but holes made this way while walking are only rabbinic, for three reasons: it is done unusually; it is not done for the purpose of the Mishkan (planting); and it is destructive. A pesik reisha that brings no benefit (delo niha leh) in a rabbinic prohibition is permitted.

He gave a common case: a holiday-home fridge with a light. An LED light is rabbinic for us; if the kitchen light is on so the fridge light gives no benefit, one may open the fridge. Ashkenazim are strict. One should not rely on this every week; use a magnet or the like. If the kitchen is dark and the light helps you, it is not permitted.

Wheelchairs

  • Pushing yourself in a manual wheelchair is like a walking stick: permitted.
  • A Jew pushing another person where there is no eruv is a Torah prohibition. The principle that a living person carries himself, which makes carrying a toddler who can walk only rabbinic, does not apply to someone who cannot walk.
  • A non-Jewish carer. One may normally ask a non-Jew to do a rabbinic prohibition for a misvah, but not a Torah one. Rav Ovadia Yosef permits asking a non-Jew to push the wheelchair, as a double doubt: perhaps we have no true Reshut HaRabbim today; and even if we do, the Baal HaItur holds one may ask a non-Jew to do a Torah prohibition for a misvah. That opinion is normally only relied on in pressing cases, such as a fallen eruv pole or the shul lights going out on Friday night. The Rabbi told of advising an elderly rabbi in a wheelchair, spending summer in a place without an eruv, that his carer may push him to the minyan and to friends for lunch, since it is a misvah and oneg Shabbat.

Seif 18: The blind, and guide dogs

A blind person may not go out with a stick, since he can physically walk; he needs only to know where. This comes from the Gemara in Beisah. "It's a difficult halacha, but that's the reality."

A guide dog raises two issues. Mukseh: animals are normally Mukseh, but Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach writes that a guide dog is set aside for guiding, so it is not. The lead: Rav Shlomo Zalman was unsure. Rav Ovadia Yosef, in Yabia Omer, rules that since this is how the dog is used, the lead is like the dog's garment, and it is permitted. So a blind person may not use a stick but may use a guide dog. A blind congregant in Holland Park used to walk half an hour with his guide dog, which sat discreetly at the back of the shul.

Seifim 19-20: Chains, masks and stilts

Seif 19: a prisoner may go out in chains on his feet, since they are fixed to him. Likewise a band to hold up a long beard.

Seif 20: one may not go out in a costume donkey that looks as if you are riding it, on stilts, or in a scary mask. These are not clothing in the normal way, and not adornments: "Takhshit is something which brings status to me", not something that makes people laugh. Next week: plasters and other clothes.

Practical summary

  • A walking stick is permitted if you cannot manage your journey without it, including on icy days or for a walk you could not complete otherwise; not if it is only for comfort.
  • Push yourself in a manual wheelchair freely; another Jew may not push you without an eruv, but a non-Jewish carer may, following Rav Ovadia Yosef.
  • A blind person may not use a cane outside an eruv but may use a guide dog.
  • Stable slippers may be worn out; wear something on Shabbat that differs from your weekday clothes.
  • Purely cosmetic items, costumes, masks and stilts may not be worn outside an eruv.
