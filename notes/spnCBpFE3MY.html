A word of caution

The Rabbi warned at the outset that this is a complex subject without black and white answers. How binding is a minhag? Does something become obligatory because our parents, grandparents or community did it? And what does minhag mean today, when Jews of every background live and marry side by side?

He opened with a story recorded by the Maharshal, some 500 years ago. A rabbi found a community meticulous about its customs but careless about basic halacha. The only way he could change things was to write in their minhag book: "there is a minhag to keep Shabbat", "there is a minhag to honour one's parents". The Maharshal, discussing the custom of covering one's head, sighs that people give more weight to small things than to the important laws. So minhag never trumps what comes from the Torah (deoraita) or from the Hakhamim (derabbanan). Those are binding without question. The subject of the class is post-Talmudic custom.

Yet minhag is a large part of our identity. The question is sharpest today, in Israel and outside it, where Sefaradim and Ashkenazim of every community live together and intermarry, and people ask why there cannot be one body of practice. He divided the class into three: personal customs, family customs and communal customs.

Personal customs: like a vow

The Torah makes vows binding, and the Gemara speaks of a rabbinic level too. The Shulhan Arukh (Yoreh Deah 214) rules that someone who knows a thing is permitted and chooses to treat it as forbidden has, in effect, accepted a vow. His examples: fasting in the days before Rosh Hashanah, not eating meat from the 17th of Tammuz, a Sefaradi who decides to refrain from kitniyot on Pesah, or a woman who chooses to hear the shofar. (He stressed that women are exempt from shofar, a positive time-bound misvah, though many choose to hear it.)

Such a custom binds when three conditions are met:

  1. You knew it was not required. Someone who took it on thinking it was halacha needs nothing to stop. A Sefaradi who was taught by Ashkenazi teachers to avoid kitniyot, and later learns that Sefaradim eat them, has made no vow.
  2. You meant it permanently, or did it three times.
  3. You did not say "beli neder", without a vow.

If all three apply, stopping requires hatarat nedarim, annulment before three people. Missing one year for a good reason, such as a woman with a new baby who cannot get to shul, is not giving it up and needs nothing. This, he said, is the real place for saying "beli neder": when taking on a good practice you know is not required. The hatarat nedarim before Rosh Hashanah specifically includes good practices accepted without saying beli neder.

Family customs do not pass to children

The Gemara (Pesahim 50b) tells of the people of Beishan, who did not travel on Fridays so as never to risk Shabbat. Their children told Rabbi Yohanan that it was too hard for them, and he answered that their fathers had already accepted it for them, citing "do not forsake the teaching of your mother".

That seems to make a parent's custom binding on the children. But another Gemara (Hullin 105a) has Mar Ukva say that compared with his father he was "vinegar, son of wine": his father would not eat dairy on the same day as meat, while he waited only until the next meal. He clearly did not keep his father's stringency. So most authorities read Beishan as a place, not a family. This is how Sefaria translates it too.

A parent's personal stringencies, then, do not bind the children. Rav Moshe Feinstein told a man who had taken on not eating gebrokts that his children, when they set up their own homes, are not bound by it.

Communal customs

A custom of a place does bind future generations. The Mishnah says work on Erev Pesah depends on local custom. The Rishonim give two sources for this power: the Rosh sees it as a communal vow; one of the Geonim derives it from "do not move your neighbour's boundary". Either way, almost everyone holds it is rabbinic, so the Hida writes that where you are unsure whether something is your minhag, you may be lenient. "Obviously do your research," the Rabbi added, about the kitniyot question he hears every Pesah.

The accepted view in the Aharonim is that the obligation rests on the community and its descendants who identify with it, not on the geography. The Rivash upheld a town enactment that a marriage performed without the communal leaders and a minyan would not take effect, and said it binds future generations.

A community may also follow a minority view. The Mishnah tells of Rabbi Yosei HaGelili's town, which ate poultry with dairy, and Rabbi Eliezer's, which did the preparations for a brit milah on Shabbat. The Rashba allowed communities that accepted the Rambam to follow him even where he is in the minority. That is why Yemenite Jews follow the Rambam, most Sefaradim the Shulhan Arukh, and Ashkenazim the Rema.

When people move

An individual who settles permanently in a new place takes on its customs, both its leniencies and its stringencies. The Mishnah in Pesahim says not to act differently from the local community, to avoid dispute.

What about a whole community? Rabbi Yoseph Karo was asked about Ashkenazim who settled in Pleven in Bulgaria, near the Sefaradi community of Nikopol, over the checking of lungs. He ruled that even a whole community, even one that becomes the majority, follows the existing local custom, and that the Ashkenazim there were not even permitted to be stringent, because it would cause dispute. The Maharashdam of Salonika disagreed: a community arriving together may keep its own customs. Where the earlier community has disappeared altogether, later authorities, including the Mishnah Berurah, rule that its customs no longer bind newcomers.

England and Israel

Medieval England was Ashkenazi, but after the expulsion of 1290 there was no community until the Spanish and Portuguese Jews arrived under Cromwell, and Bevis Marks opened in 1701. So by the Shulhan Arukh's view, the established minhag here is Sefaradi, and Ashkenazim who came later "would have had to" follow it. It did not happen. But the Rabbi believed that Ashkenazim here who wish to become Sefaradi, through marriage or identity, are fully entitled to. As a sign of that history, he mentioned that the London Beth Din still follows the Spanish and Portuguese spelling of London and the Thames in its gittin.

Rav Ovadia Yoseph held that in Israel, where Rabbi Yoseph Karo was the authority of the land, Ashkenazim should follow Sefaradi practice. Rav Uziel had hoped instead for one shared halacha. Neither vision came about. The Rabbi's own view was that keeping separate customs door to door "doesn't make much sense anymore", and that the question needs the attention of the gedolim. He also noted that Rav Ovadia allows someone whose family kept a custom, such as avoiding kitniyot, to annul it through hatarat nedarim.

Asked whether an individual who moves may keep his old custom, he said no: the Mishnah teaches not to stand out from the community.

Practical summary

  • A stringency you took on knowingly, permanently and without "beli neder" binds like a rabbinic vow; to stop, you need hatarat nedarim.
  • If you took it on believing it was required, you may drop it once you learn otherwise.
  • A parent's personal stringencies do not bind the children.
  • A community's customs bind its descendants; someone who settles permanently elsewhere follows the local custom.
  • Where you are unsure whether something is your minhag, you may be lenient.
