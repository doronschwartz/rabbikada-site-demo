These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi opened with a short review of Siman 14, on aveilut and marriage. A mourner waits until the sheloshim are over before marrying, whether for a parent or a sibling. A man whose wife has died waits three regalim, and here Rosh HaShanah and Yom Kippur do not count. There are exceptions, for example a man who has not yet fulfilled the misvah of having children, or who needs someone to care for his children.

The class then began Siman 15, the forbidden relatives (arayot). Until now we learned the classical arayot: a married woman, a niddah, a mamzer and the nations. Now we turn to the family. The Rabbi warned that there are many small details and some points will need repeating.

The three kinds of arayot

  1. Blood relatives. A mother, a sister, a granddaughter.
  2. Connected through a blood relative. The wife of your father. She is not your blood relative, but she is connected to one.
  3. Forbidden by a combination. A woman and her daughter, or a woman and her sister. Each one alone has no connection to you, but the two together create the prohibition.

Of all the arayot, only a man's wife's sister can become permitted again. If he divorces his wife, he may marry her sister. If she dies, he may. But a wife's daughter stays forbidden for life, even after the divorce or her mother's death.

In the verses, the Torah does not mention a daughter, only a granddaughter, and Hazal learned that a daughter is included. It names a father's brother's wife but not a mother's brother's wife, which is only rabbinic.

Is it a hok or a mishpat?

The Rabbi asked the class whether the arayot are a hok (a law without a clear reason) or a mishpat (a law that people accept by reason). Some said mishpat, as the nations accept it. Others replied that some of them are hard to explain, and that we find the forefathers marrying two sisters.

The Rabbi brought the Rambam in Shemonah Perakim. Hazal say one should not say "I do not want to eat pork, it disgusts me", but rather "I would like to, but what can I do, Hashem forbade it." The Rambam asks: should one say that about murder? A truly virtuous person has no desire at all for wrong. His answer: for a mishpat the philosophers are right and one should remove even the wish. For a hok one says "I would like to, but Hashem forbade it". For the Rambam every misvah has a reason, and a hok is only one whose reason is harder to find. Hazal's statement includes the arayot, which shows they are counted as hukim.

In the Moreh Nevuchim the Rambam gives a practical reason: these women are always available to us, so if they were permitted there would be endless immorality. Since the Torah forbade it, it became second nature. The disgust is not innate but developed. The Hinukh points to the problems it would cause within the family. The Rabbi concluded that it is not clear and can be argued both ways.

Torah arayot and rabbinic ones

The punishments differ (most skilah, some karet or srefah, some only a lav). More important for us: Hazal added secondary prohibitions (sheniyot) as a fence, so that we do not come to a Torah prohibition. There are two kinds:

  • An extra generation. The Torah forbids your mother, not your grandmother, and Hazal added her. Likewise Hazal added your grandfather's wife to your father's wife.
  • To avoid confusion between the male and female lines. The Torah forbids your son's wife, and Hazal added your son's son's wife as an extra generation. But they also forbade your daughter's son's wife (kalat bito). That is not another generation but a new idea: since the son's wife is forbidden, they forbade the equivalent through a daughter. When learning each case, ask which of the two kinds it is.

Seif 1: is there kiddushin in the rabbinic arayot?

The Shulhan Arukh opens: "These are not subject to kiddushin..." That seems to say that even the rabbinic arayot have no kiddushin and need no get. But in practice, with the rabbinic arayot (the sheniyot), the kiddushin does take effect and a get is needed, as the Siman itself says. The Beit Yosef says the wording is imprecise.

The Darkei Moshe suggested that this kiddushin is not complete, so that if another man did kiddushin with her, he too would need to give a get (out of concern that people may think the first did not work). He himself raised the difficulty of a Kohen who marries a divorcee: he transgresses a lav, yet the kiddushin works, and nowhere do we find that a second man needs a get. The Aharonim reject the idea. The Rabbi presented it as interesting, not as a ruling.

Seifim 2-4: mothers

Your mother is forbidden by the Torah, and your grandmother is rabbinic, an extra generation. A great-grandmother is forbidden too. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam, who learns that the decree continues only as long as the line is unbroken, em em em. If a man comes in between, there is a hefsek and the line stops.

The source is the Yerushalmi. Rashi disagrees: when Hazal said a mother has no hefsek, he understands it in all directions, paternal and maternal, so for Rashi the further mothers are forbidden forever. He said the cases are confusing and he would gladly go over them again.

Seifim 5-7: the wife of your father

By the Torah, the wife of your father is forbidden. This applies even if your father died or divorced her. It needs some marriage connection: a casual one-off encounter does not make the woman forbidden to the son. You may marry the mother of your stepmother. The Rabbi said it may be disrespectful if your father is still alive, but it is not an ervah problem.

Hazal added the wife of your grandfather, and through the father's line all the way back, up to the wife of Yaakov Avinu. If Rahel or Leah were alive today, a descendant of Yaakov through his fathers could not marry her, unless he is a convert.

But if the grandfather is your mother's father, Hazal forbade only one generation. The wife of your mother's father is forbidden rabbinically, and the wife of his father is permitted. The Rabbi explained that this time it is the mother who creates the hefsek, because the prohibition in the Torah was on the wife of your father. The Rema brings Rashi's view, which is the same mahloket as before: for Rashi it continues without end whenever Hazal said there is no hefsek. The Rambam's view is that a person of the opposite sex breaks the line, and only one generation is forbidden.

Seifim 8-9: aunts

Seifim 8 and 9 are the aunts, meaning the wife of an uncle. There are four kinds:

  • The wife of your father's brother, where they share a father. Forbidden by the Torah, even after he divorced her or died.
  • The wife of your father's brother, where they share only a mother. Rabbinic, because of confusion.
  • The wife of your mother's brother, where they share a father. Rabbinic.
  • The wife of your mother's brother, where they share only a mother. Rabbinic, and in a sense doubly removed from the Torah (an aunt on the mother's side, and a sibling through the mother).

Only the first is from the Torah. The other three are sheniyot to avoid confusion, not extra generations. Asked whether the doubly removed case is less severe, the Rabbi said Hazal made no distinction and it is the same rabbinic prohibition. He would have understood had Hazal permitted that case, but they did not, "so we accept it".

Seif 10: sisters (the beginning)

A sister is always forbidden, whether she is your mother's daughter or only your father's daughter, and whether she was born in the home or outside it (moledet bayit, moledet hus). She is forbidden even if your father fathered her in a forbidden union, so that she is a mamzeret. The Rabbi began with a half-sister born to a shifhah. The Gemara says a shifhah is not halachically your sister, and the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh permit marrying her. A half-sister born to a gentile mother who then converted is a question of Hilchot Geirut, not of arayot.

This troubled the Geonim already, from the case of the Reish Galuta Dostai and his son by a shifhah, who does not carry his father's lineage. Some said that if a man has continuous relations with his own shifhah, there is a hezkah that he freed her (meshahroha), so she is no longer a shifhah, and her daughter is truly his daughter. The Rambam rejects this, saying he applies such a hezkah only in two places in Hilchot Gittin (an ex-wife, or a failed kiddushin), and "people have relations all the time" so it cannot be a general rule. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam. On the Geonic view a one-off with a random shifhah is permitted, but a child of your father's own maidservant is a problem. The Rema brings a further novel idea, which the Rabbi said is not really accepted. The stepsister raised in your home was left for next week.

Practical summary

  • The arayot come in three kinds: blood, connected through a blood relative, and by combination. Ask each time whether the isur is Torah, or rabbinic (an extra generation, or against confusion).
  • Even a rabbinic erva needs a get if there was kiddushin. The Darkei Moshe's idea that the kiddushin is incomplete is not accepted.
  • A line of mothers without a man in between is forbidden without end. A man in between stops it after one generation. Rashi disagrees and holds it continues in all directions.
  • The wife of your father is forbidden, and of your paternal grandfathers back to Yaakov Avinu. For your mother's father only one generation.
  • Of four kinds of aunt, only one is from the Torah. The other three are rabbinic and all equally forbidden.
  • A half-sister is forbidden, except for the question of a shifhah. A one-off with a shifhah is permitted, but continuous relations with one's own maidservant raise a problem.
