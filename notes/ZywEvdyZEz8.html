Topic: Prophecy, Ruah HaKodesh and heavenly assistance in halachic decision-making Shiur: Part 4 of a four-part series given by Dayan Daniel Kada in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 6 June 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording. The recording ends early, when the Rabbi's battery ran out.

The series so far

The Rabbi summarised the first three classes. The Sanhedrin began with Moshe and the seventy elders and continued into exile. Rashi and the Ramban hold that one must follow it even when it seems to say right is left; the Rambam disagrees for a talmid hakham who knows it erred. According to the Rambam, rabbis today have only local authority, by communal acceptance; according to the Sefer HaHinukh, we must still listen to the great hakhamim of each generation. Last week defined the talmid hakham, the dayan and the Gadol, and explained Emunat Hakhamim as trust that leads to questioning, not blind acceptance.

Tonight's question: is there a role for prophecy or Ruah HaKodesh when rabbis decide halacha? And what is the difference between the two?

Two approaches at one table

He opened with a story. The Noda BiYehudah, Rav Yehezkel Landau of Prague, one of the greatest Ashkenazi poskim of the eighteenth century, was dining with a Hasidic rebbe. Just as they were served meat, the host rushed in: he was not sure the salting had been done properly, so perhaps the meat still contained blood. The Noda BiYehudah took him aside, asked about every detail, and concluded that the meat was kosher. When he sat back down, the rebbe said there had been no need. Hashem would not have let him come so close to eating something forbidden. That itself was the ruling.

These are two very different ways of approaching halacha: as a careful legal process, or as something spiritual and metaphysical.

The Rambam: a prophet has no say in halacha

The Rambam, in his introduction to the Mishnah, is clear. A prophet is distinguished from other people in the messages he brings from Hashem. In reasoning about the misvot, he is like any other sage. If a prophet claims that Hashem told him his reasoning is correct, do not listen to him. "If there are a thousand prophets, all like Eliyahu and Elisha, following one reasoning, and a thousand and one sages following the opposite, we follow the majority."

You do not ask Eliyahu HaNavi what to do because some milk fell into your meat. He probably knows the answer, since a prophet must be wise, but that is not his role. "There's the educator and there's the motivator. Two different personalities. Don't mix the two."

The Kuzari: the court has divine help

Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi, in the Kuzari, takes a different view. We must listen to the judges of each generation because divine assistance cleaves to them, and so they give clear and certain answers. On this reading the Sanhedrin did not use legal reasoning alone. Heavenly help was a key way they reached their rulings.

Responsa from Heaven

The Rabbi showed a book from the shelf, Sheelot UTeshuvot Min HaShamayim, by a thirteenth-century rabbi in France who posed questions before going to sleep and woke with the answers. One question was whether women who voluntarily perform time-bound misvot, such as hearing the shofar, taking the lulav or sitting in the sukkah, should say the berakhah. The answer he received was yes.

The Shulhan Arukh rules, like the Rambam, that they do not, and Rav Ovadia Yosef follows this. The Torah is not in heaven. A dream, however holy, is not how we decide law. The Kuzari, by contrast, would likely have been more open to such a book.

Did the revelation stop?

At Sinai the Torah says Hashem spoke "kol gadol velo yasaf" (Devarim 5). Rashi brings both readings. "Yasaf" can mean "add": a great voice that was never heard again. Or it can mean "gather in, stop": a great voice that never stopped.

The Rabbi suggested this captures the two views. For the Rambam, the revelation happened once, and from then on the Hakhamim use the legal tools they were given. For the Kuzari, the revelation never paused, and divine help continues to accompany the sages.

Sources on both sides

Pointing towards the Kuzari: - The first Mishnah in Avot lists the prophets as links in the chain of the Torah's transmission, suggesting they had a part in passing on its laws. - The Tosefta says that after the last prophets died, Ruah HaKodesh departed, but the sages still received guidance through a bat kol, as in the story of the sages gathered in an upper room in Yeriho.

Pointing towards the Rambam: - The Gemara in Bava Batra (12a): from the day the Temple was destroyed, prophecy was taken from the prophets and given to the sages, and "a sage is greater than a prophet". Someone asked how the verse proves this. The Rabbi explained that the prophet is described as having wisdom within him, which makes wisdom the greater quality.

Most remarkable of all is the Raavad, who wrote critical glosses on the Mishneh Torah. Usually he argues with clear reasons. But in two places he cites Ruah HaKodesh. On a hadas whose tip is cut, which the Rambam allows, he writes that Ruah HaKodesh has appeared in their study hall for some years and taught that it is invalid. And on whether the sanctity of the Temple site is permanent, he ends: this is what was revealed to me from the secret of Hashem to those who fear Him. The Raavad clearly belongs with the Kuzari.

Defining the terms

The Rabbi then argued that the two sides may not truly disagree, once we understand the terms. He used the Ramhal in Derech Hashem, part 3:

  • Nevuah, prophecy. Not primarily delivering messages. The Gemara says there were thousands of prophets; most of their prophecies were never written down because they had no message for the people. Prophecy is a bond with Hashem so strong that truths are revealed to the prophet. It is unmistakable: the body cannot contain the light and shuts down. It took years of training, with music and meditation, in schools of prophets. And a prophet is not a prophet all the time.
  • Ruah HaKodesh. No trance. An idea falls into your mind from heaven, what Yiddish calls an "einfal".
  • A touch of Ruah HaKodesh. Something even ordinary people experience, what we would call intuition.
  • Dreams. These can contain truth, but mixed with falsehood.

Prophecy has ended. Even a prophet knew only what he was shown during the prophecy itself.

Bridging the two views

Does the Rambam deny that a hakham has divine help? He never says so. A posek may well be guided towards an insight while learning. The Rabbi described his own experience: you are asked a question about something you just learned, or you open a book and the answer is right there. "That's not Ruah HaKodesh. That's a touch of Ruah HaKodesh." When you are doing the will of Hashem, He gives you guidance.

The Rambam's point is that you may not base a legal ruling on a claim of Ruah HaKodesh. Help in reaching your analysis is another matter. So the gap is smaller than it looks, though the Rambam would never accept "responsa from heaven".

Summary

  • The Rambam: a prophet has no special authority in halacha; a thousand prophets do not outweigh a thousand and one sages.
  • The Kuzari and the Raavad: the sages enjoy divine assistance, and it can play a part in their rulings.
  • The Ramhal distinguishes prophecy, Ruah HaKodesh, intuition and dreams; prophecy has ended.
  • No rabbi can answer every question by Ruah HaKodesh, but a hakham may be helped to see the answer while learning.
  • The Rambam forbids basing a ruling on such claims, so the two views may be closer than they seem.
