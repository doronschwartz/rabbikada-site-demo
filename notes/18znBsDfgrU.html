From the Sefer Torah to Purim

After more than six months on the laws of the Sefer Torah, the class turned to Purim, to be followed by Pesah. Siman 685 deals with the order of the four parashiyot. The Mishnah Berurah gives a good introduction: besides the weekly readings, Hazal instituted four special readings around this time of year.

Why Shekalim?

The Torah calls Rosh Hodesh Nisan "the offering of each month for the months of the year". The Gemara learns that from Nisan the communal offerings had to be bought from new donations, so the call to give went out from Rosh Hodesh Adar, a month ahead. Leftover funds could go to the general Temple upkeep, since money given to the Temple can be used for anything. Even when Rosh Hodesh Adar falls on Shabbat, the special haftarah of Shekalim is read rather than that of Rosh Hodesh, because it is the rarer and special reading of the day.

Working out the dates

If Rosh Hodesh Adar falls on a weekday, even Friday, Shekalim is read the Shabbat before, so that the announcement comes before Adar begins. That year Rosh Hodesh fell on Tuesday and Wednesday, so Shekalim was read on 27 Shevat. Rosh Hodesh Adar can fall only on Shabbat, Monday, Wednesday or Friday. The Rabbi explained the traditional memory aid, which gives the breaks (Shabbatot with no special reading) for each case:

  • Shabbat: one break, on the 15th (Shushan Purim). Zakhor on the 8th, Parah on the 22nd, HaHodesh on the 29th.
  • Monday: one break, on the 6th.
  • Wednesday: one break, on the 4th.
  • Friday: two breaks, on the 2nd and the 16th; HaHodesh then falls on Rosh Hodesh Nisan itself.

Parashat Zakhor

The Shulhan Arukh says: "Some say that Parashat Zakhor and Parashat Parah are obligatory from the Torah, so those living in villages without a minyan must come to a place with a minyan to hear them." The Rabbi pointed out how to read the Shulhan Arukh's language with the Beit Yosef in mind: "some say" (yesh omrim) means he follows this view but hints that others disagree; "there is one who says" usually means he found it in a single source.

He also noted that there is no halacha requiring a person to live where there is a minyan. If you live near one you must go; but the Shulhan Arukh assumes some people live in places without one, and asks them to make the effort for Zakhor and for Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur.

What is the Torah obligation? According to those who hold it is from the Torah, it is to say aloud, from a book, once a year, the remembrance of Amalek. It is not tied to that particular Shabbat. "A memory lasts twelve months", as the Gemara says about mourning. So:

  • If Zakhor was missed, read it the next week.
  • In a leap year more than twelve months pass, so when reading Ki Tesse in the summer before, one should have in mind the passage about Amalek. Since the coming year was a leap year, the Rabbi asked people to have this in mind that summer.
  • Someone who cannot hear Zakhor should have in mind the reading of Vayavo Amalek on Purim morning. The Mishnah Berurah discusses someone who can make only one trip: if the choice is between Zakhor and the night Megillah, Zakhor comes first.
  • Where reading from a Sefer Torah is impossible, the Rema says at least read it from a Humash.

The reader of Zakhor must read properly and must be a verified adult, not a new bar misvah whose maturity is not yet evident.

Women. Most Rishonim say Zakhor does not apply to women, since the misvah of eliminating Amalek is for those who go out to war. The Rabbi noted that since Sanheriv mixed up the nations, we no longer know who Amalek is. Women who come to hear Zakhor do something lovely. Rav Ovadia Yosef did not favour taking out the Sefer Torah again specially for women. "Why are you making a fuss out of it?" A woman who wishes can read it from a Humash at home.

The Shulhan Arukh adds Parashat Parah as being from the Torah too, but the Rabbi said this view comes from one Rishon and in practice is treated as rabbinic.

The Fast of Esther (Siman 686)

Fasting and eulogies are permitted on the days before and after Purim and Hanukkah, since the old restrictions of Megillat Taanit have lapsed. On Purim itself there are no fasts and no eulogies, except for a hakham whose body is present.

We fast on the 13th of Adar, and when Purim falls on Sunday the fast is brought forward to Thursday. Yet we normally never bring sad things forward. The answer is that this fast is not sad. According to the Rambam, it recalls the day of the battle, the 13th of Adar, when the Jews gathered to fight and fasted and prayed for mercy, as Moshe did in the war against Amalek. It is not a commemoration of Esther's three-day fast, which was much earlier, around Pesah. "So it's not sad. There's nothing sad to this."

The Shulhan Arukh also mentions a custom of fasting three days, Monday, Thursday and Monday, after Purim to recall Esther's fast. This is only a description of what some did.

A lenient fast

Because it is a commemoration, the Rema says this fast is not an obligation in the same way, and anyone with a real need can be lenient:

  • Sefaradim do not require pregnant or nursing women to fast on any of the minor fasts; Ashkenazim in theory do, though in practice they are lenient.
  • Someone with eye pain need not fast; the Aharonim say a healthy person should not separate himself from the community.
  • Those involved in a brit milah that day, the father, the sandak and the mohel, need not fast. Ashkenazim also include those who carry the baby in and out.

He compared the levels of fasts: Yom Kippur and Tisha BeAv, then the three sad fasts (Gedaliah, the 10th of Tevet and the 17th of Tammuz), which are postponed rather than brought forward, and then this fast. Before the Megillah one may drink and eat a little; Rav Ovadia Yosef is more lenient still.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that Adar is a month of joy: if you have a court case with a non-Jew, try to have it heard in Adar.

Reading the Megillah (Siman 687)

Everyone must read the Megillah at night and again by day. The night reading may be done the whole night, and the day reading the whole day, from sunrise, or from dawn after the fact. The original institution of Mordekhai is the day reading, so "if you've got a choice between night and day, you always choose the day."

Why the whole night? Most night-time misvot should be done by midnight, in case one forgets. The Megillah is so beloved (haviv) that there is no fear of forgetting. Someone asked why Hanukkah lights are different: they need passers-by to see them, and people do not walk the streets in the middle of the night.

The Rabbi stressed knowing the levels of an obligation: what is required, what works after the fact, and what is the most beautiful way (misvah min hamuvhar). With Shema at night, any time until midnight is fully correct, and saying it early is a bonus. Doing it first is beautiful; delaying within the proper time is not wrong.

Starting just before sunset

Every year he is asked about people who wake late on Purim afternoon and start the Megillah a few minutes before sunset. Can they say the berakhah if they will finish after sunset? He compared the question of starting the Amidah a minute before its latest time. The Gemara says Bilaam knew the brief moment each day when Hashem is angry; Tosafot asks what he could say in such a short time, and one answer is that once he began in that moment he could continue. From this some poskim learn that a misvah begun in time may be completed afterwards. Combined with the doubt about the period after sunset, the Rabbi ruled that if one starts before sunset, one says the berakhah and continues after.

Practical summary

  • Shekalim, Zakhor, Parah and HaHodesh recall the half-shekel, Amalek, the red heifer and the new month; when Rosh Hodesh Adar falls midweek, Shekalim is read the Shabbat before.
  • Make every effort to hear Zakhor from a Sefer Torah read by a proper adult reader. If missed, read it later; in a leap year have it in mind at Ki Tesse.
  • Women are not obligated in Zakhor; if they wish, they can read it from a Humash at home.
  • The Fast of Esther recalls the fast on the day of battle, so it is brought forward to Thursday and is lenient for anyone with a real need.
  • The night Megillah may be read all night and the day Megillah all day; the day reading is the more important.
  • If you begin the day reading before sunset, say the berakhah and finish after.
