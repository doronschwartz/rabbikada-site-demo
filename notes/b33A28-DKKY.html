Topic: How to pronounce the letters, when a sheva is sounded, and when a kamats is read "o" Shiur: Part 2 of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on the rules of Lashon HaKodesh, given on Zoom, Tuesday 15 February 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why precision matters

The Rabbi recalled the first class. Placing the stress on the wrong syllable, or ignoring a dagesh, can change the meaning of a word. A talmid hakham is recognised by how he speaks, and the Shulhan Arukh rules that someone who cannot articulate the words properly may not serve as hazzan or even say Birkat Kohanim. Some Ashkenazi poskim wrote that these rules are no longer applied, but for Sephardim, he said, they remain essential.

Tonight had three parts: the letters, the sheva, and the kamats.

The letters

Six letters, בגד כפת, must sound different with and without a dagesh. He described the traditional Sephardi sounds while stressing that consistency in your own tradition matters most. "I'm not saying we should start changing the way we speak." - ב / בּ. In the Syrian and Spanish and Portuguese tradition, ב without a dagesh is a soft "b", not a "v". - ג / גּ. Without a dagesh, ג is "gh", the sound Israelis now give to ר. - ד / דּ. Without a dagesh, ד is "dh", like "the" in English. This is why the halacha says to lengthen the ד of אֶחָד in the Shema. You can draw out "dh", but not "d", which is why many lengthen the ח instead. - ו. The Iraqi tradition says "w". Semitic languages have no "v", so this may be the original sound. - ט / ת. ט comes from deeper in the mouth. Two letters cannot sound the same. - ס / שׂ. He knows of no community that clearly distinguishes them; he had read that שׂ is a more refined "s". - ע / ח. Guttural, from the throat. - צ. Not "tz". It is a deep "s", like the Arabic sad. "It's not Tzitzit. It's Sisit." This is why he writes it with an "s" in English. - ק / כּ. ק comes from the throat. - ר. Either roll it or use an English "r", but not the Israeli guttural, which belongs to ג. - ת / תּ. Without a dagesh, ת is "th" as in "Thursday".

Two kinds of sheva

The Shulhan Arukh (Orah Hayim 61), on reading the Shema, warns: do not "relax the moving" or "move the relaxed". A sheva na, from "to move", is sounded and has its own syllable. A sheva nah, from "to rest", is silent. Is the first word שְׁמַע read "Shema" or "Shma"? The rules decide. Modern siddurim mark the sounded sheva with larger dots, which is a good way to start, but it is important to know the rules. There are five.

Rule 1. A sheva at the beginning of a word is sounded. שְׁמַע is "Shema". The books are בְּרֵאשִׁית, שְׁמוֹת and דְּבָרִים, "Bereshit", "Shemot", "Devarim", whatever your accent, but never "Shmot" or "Dvarim". The same applies to פְּרִי and פְּנֵי.

Rule 2. A sheva under a letter with a dagesh is sounded. - הַדְּבָרִים is "haddevarim", four syllables. - מְצַוְּךָ is "mesavvekha". - הַמְּאֹרֹת is "hammeorot". - In the Amidah, וְקַבְּצֵנוּ is "vekabbesenu", not "vekabsenu".

Rule 3. Two shevas together: the first is silent, the second sounded. נַפְשְׁךָ is "naf-she-kha". Others are וּבְשָׁכְבְּךָ and יִשְׁרְצוּ. Few people get this wrong, because it is hard to say otherwise.

Rule 4. Twin letters: if the first has a sheva, it is sounded. נִשְׁעָנְנוּ, in the Amidah, is "nishanenu", four syllables.

Rule 5. A sheva after a vowel marked with a gaya (meteg), or with a cantillation note, is sounded. This is the rule people do not know, and where most mistakes are made. The little vertical line lifts the vowel before, so the next sheva is sounded. - לְבָֽבְךָ is "levavekha", not "levavkha". - שָֽׁרְצוּ is "sharesu", not "sharsu". - The grandson of Aharon, פִּֽינְחָס, is "Pinehas". "There is no such person called Pinhas." - In the Amidah: סוֹמֵךְ נֽוֹפְלִים is "nofelim"; וְקָֽרְבֵנוּ is "vekarevenu"; in summer, בָּֽרְכֵנוּ is "barekhenu".

Someone asked what to do with a text without the marks. "If you have a siddur without taamim, you've got the wrong siddur." He noted that there are some exceptions to rule 5, such as לֵכְנָה שֹּׁבְנָה in Ruth, with reasons beyond an introduction.

He also warned about silent shevas moved to the wrong letter. The king of Egypt, פַּרְעֹה, is "Par-oh": the sheva under the ר is silent and closes the first syllable. It is not "Pa-roh".

Patah and kamats

Patah comes from "to open": the mouth opens, "ah". Kamats comes from "to close", as the kohen closed his hand for the kemisah; the mouth is more closed. Ashkenazim say "ah" and "o"; most Sephardim say "ah" for both, though some communities, such as the Syrians, keep a slight difference: אֱלֹהַי with patah is open, מִרְמָה with kamats is more closed. In five cases, even Sephardim read the kamats as a clear "o", the kamats katan.

Rule 1. A hataf kamats is "o". צָהֳרַיִם is "sohorayim". He remembered correcting his modern Hebrew teacher at school, who said "saharayim" because that is how everyone speaks. In the Kaddish, קֳדָם is "kodam", and קֹדֶשׁ הַקֳּדָשִׁים is "Kodesh HaKodashim".

Rule 2. A kamats followed by a dagesh is "o". In the Shirah, עָזִּי is "ozzi". In the Amidah, חָנֵּנוּ is "honnenu". At the end of Tehillim 72, כָּלּוּ תְפִלּוֹת דָּוִד is "kollu".

Rule 3. A kamats in the last syllable before a makaf is "o". This is why בְּכָל־לְבָבְךָ is "bekhol". In Tehillim 145, וּגְדָל־חָסֶד is "ugdol hased". Without a makaf, it stays "kal": in Nishmat, כָּל עַצְמוֹתַי. For the same reason, he noted, the siddur has no makaf in כָּל נִדְרֵי, so it is "Kal Nidre".

Rule 4. A kamats before a silent sheva, without a gaya, is "o". חָכְמָה is "hokhmah", and קָרְבָּן is "korban". Compare בָּֽרְכֵנוּ, where the gaya makes it "barekhenu".

Rule 5. The rarest one he left for the end, for those who stayed. In short words beginning with ו that have two kamatses and the stress on the first of them, the second is "o": וַתָּקָם is "vattakom", and in the Shirah, וַיָּשָׁב הַיָּם is "vayyashov hayam".

A reading exercise

The class then read Ashrei together. The Rabbi stopped the reader at each rule. In וַאֲבָרְכָה, the text on screen was missing a gaya. "This is sloppy editing," he said. With the gaya it is "vaavarekhah", and without it the reader would have had to say "vaavorkhah". In וּגְדוּלָּתְךָ אֲסַפְּרֶנָּה, he corrected the stress and pointed to the dagesh in the פ. And in אֶת אָכְלָם בְּעִתּוֹ, the kamats before a silent sheva makes it "okhlam".

At the end someone admitted feeling back at square one. The Rabbi said the purpose was never to discourage anyone from reading, only to make our reading more precise. Hashem's love extends even to those who miss letters; the main thing is the intention. His advice was to buy a siddur that marks the sounded shevas and the kamats katan, follow it, and the rules will start to become second nature.

Summary

  • The six letters בגד כפת must sound different with and without a dagesh; know your tradition and keep it consistently.
  • A sheva is sounded at the start of a word, under a dagesh, as the second of two shevas, under the first of twin letters, and after a gaya.
  • A kamats is "o" with a hataf kamats, before a dagesh, before a makaf, before a silent sheva without a gaya, and in a rare fifth case.
  • Begin with a siddur that marks these rules, and they will become natural.
