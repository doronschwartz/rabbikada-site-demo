Topic: The history behind the Megillah, the misvot of Purim and their reasons, and the hidden hand of HaShem Source: Megillat Esther and the laws of Purim in the Shulhan Arukh Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in the week of Purim, March 2015 (Taanit Esther, Purim and Shushan Purim fell on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The recording begins during informal conversation before the shiur.

The story in its setting

To appreciate Purim, the Rabbi said, we need its history, not just the story. The first Beit HaMikdash stood for 410 years and was destroyed for the three cardinal sins. The ten northern tribes had already been exiled and lost by Sanheriv; the two remaining tribes went to Bavel, a short journey away. Yirmiyahu prophesied a return after seventy years. Persia then conquered Babylonia and ruled the region, and its king was Ahashverosh, whom he described as deeply insecure, a man who had risen from humble beginnings.

Ahashverosh miscalculated the seventy years. Believing the prophecy had failed and the Jews would never return, he threw a lavish banquet and used the vessels of the Beit HaMikdash, as if to say: it is over. Mordekhai urged the Jews not to attend, but most could not resist, and the Gemara says that this is why Haman's decree came upon them. Purim, he noted, comes long before Hanukkah: it is Persian, between the two Temples; Hanukkah is Greek, in the Second Temple.

Taanit Esther and Al HaNissim

We fast on Taanit Esther in memory of the three days Israel fasted when Esther went to the king, and to remember that sincere prayer in distress is answered.

Purim has seven features in all: the four well-known misvot and three more. The first is Al HaNissim. Hodaah means both to thank and to acknowledge: first we acknowledge that HaShem performed the miracle, then we thank Him. If you forget Al HaNissim and have already finished the berakhah, you do not go back; instead you add at the end "HaRahaman, may He perform miracles for us as He did for our fathers."

But we are normally not allowed to ask for miracles. How can we ask on Purim and Hanukkah? Because our festivals do not only commemorate; we relive them. On Shavuot we receive the Torah again; on Pesah each person leaves his own Egypt. On Purim and Hanukkah we relive the miracles, so it is an auspicious time to pray, even for a miracle.

Reading the Megillah

We read at night and by day, but the main obligation, written in the Megillah itself, is by day; the night reading was added later. If you can hear only one, choose the day.

Women are obligated equally, even though it is time-bound, because they too were part of the miracle. Haman, like Hitler, wanted to destroy every Jew, men, women and children. Some also say because of Esther's central role. On a woman reading for herself or for other women, the Rabbi said she fulfils her obligation if she reads properly from a kosher Megillah, but she does not make the berakhah, because the Rishonim disagree about whether the berakhah is "to read" or "to hear." For the same reason, a man who has already heard and reads again only for women does not make the berakhah.

In the morning we read the Torah portion about Amalek, since Haman was descended from Amalek.

Mishloah Manot

Why are Purim's misvot so physical, food, wine, gifts, when Hanukkah has no misvah of a meal? Because on Hanukkah the Greeks threatened our faith, so we celebrate spiritually, with lights, prayer and Torah. On Purim our bodies were threatened, so we celebrate with a feast.

There are two reasons given by the Rishonim for Mishloah Manot: - To increase friendship, answering Haman's charge that the Jews are "scattered," a disunited people. Ideally one sends to someone with whom there was friction during the year. - So that everyone has plenty for the seudah.

The two reasons lead to different results. If the person refuses your gift, you have increased friendship but given him no food. If you give anonymously, he has food but no friendship has grown. So the halacha is to satisfy both: at least one person, over bar or bat misvah, must receive from you two different items of food, knowing it is from you. The rest may be anonymous. Clothes or raw meat do not count. Children do it for hinukh. The poskim write that a woman should not send directly to a man, or a man to a woman, though from family to family is fine.

Matanot LaEvyonim

Giving to the poor also answers Haman: we care for one another. One gives two gifts to two poor people, each a substantial gift. The Rabbi suggested at the time that £5 to £7 per gift was certainly enough, and that someone who found that hard could rely on a smaller amount, around £2. It must reach them on Purim day itself. Best of all is to give someone you know is struggling directly, even anonymously through the letterbox, in cash or vouchers they can use that day. Otherwise give through a reliable rabbi or organisation that distributes on the day. Schools that collect beforehand hold the money in trust until Purim. Inviting two poor people to your seudah is a wonderful way to fulfil it.

A poor person nowadays means anyone in financial hardship. And to anyone who stretches out his hand on Purim, we give, even a small coin, unless he will misuse it. Someone asked about collectors stopping cars and spraying windows. Where it is dangerous, he said, one should not encourage it. A Hillul HaShem applies 365 days a year, and in a mixed area "we are on show."

The seudah and the wine

The seudah must be by day; according to Kabbalah, ideally in the morning. Learn a little Torah before it. The meal is also the most auspicious time to pray, because the whole salvation came through banquets: Vashti's fall and Esther's request.

Why wine? Because the miracles came through mishteh hayayin, the banquets of wine, so whisky does not fulfil it. The Shulhan Arukh writes to drink until one cannot tell "cursed is Haman" from "blessed is Mordekhai"; the Rema says drink a little more than usual and go to sleep. All the poskim agree that if drinking will lead you to miss benching or Arvit, to insult someone or to be a danger, it is wrong. Levasumei means to get a taste of it, to leave your usual boundaries while staying in control. If you suspect you will not stay in control, drink one cup and lie down for a while.

God in a costume

The Megillah must be read in order and fully unrolled. God's name never appears in it, though "the king" appears many times. HaShem was there throughout the story, but hidden, in a costume. An atheist could read the Megillah as a chain of coincidences: Vashti's death, Esther chosen, Mordekhai overhearing the plot, a sleepless king. Purim strengthens our recognition of HaShem within nature. In Hebrew, nature is teva, and to sink is litboa: we sink into nature until we forget who stands behind it.

He ended with a thought on why HaShem made children grow used to the world before they can think about it: had we been born with a mature mind, the Creator would be so obvious that there would be no free choice. The role of a Jew from thirteen is to look again and ask who is behind it all, as Avraham did.

Practical summary

  • The main reading of the Megillah is by day; women are equally obligated.
  • Mishloah Manot: at least two different foods to one adult, with your name on it.
  • Matanot LaEvyonim: a substantial gift to each of two poor people, reaching them on Purim day.
  • The seudah is by day; drink wine to leave your boundaries, never to lose control.
  • Purim teaches us to see HaShem behind the costume of nature.
