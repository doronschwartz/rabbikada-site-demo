Topic: A convert child's right to object on reaching maturity, the problem of retroactive non-Jewish status, and whether a convert may hold positions of authority Source: Gemara Ketubot 11a and Yevamot 45b; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268 Shiur: Seventh class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, on Zoom, Wednesday 7 July 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

When can a convert child object?

Ketubot 11a says that a child converted by the Beit Din or by a parent can object once he or she grows up, and returns to being a non-Jew. The Gemara also says that someone who has grown up and "was silent" for a certain time can no longer object. The Rabbi noted the apparent contradiction.

The Rosh, whom the Shulhan Arukh follows, answers simply. An objection before bar or bat misvah has no force, as the child is not of full mind. After reaching maturity the child may object, but only until he or she has acted as a Jew. Any act that shows interest in continuing, such as netilat yadayim in the morning, a Jewish custom, or putting on tefillin (or saying Shema at night if he became bar misvah in the evening), confirms the status. At conversion the convert also accepts "to act in accordance with all Jewish custom".

Why the objection works backwards

The conversion of a child works through zekhiyah, conferring a benefit on someone who is not there. If the grown child says that this was never a benefit, he or she is "megaleh lemafrea", showing that it was never a benefit, and so was never Jewish. The Rabbi stressed this: it is not that the child was Jewish and then became a non-Jew. The child is retroactively a non-Jew, "and this is going to pose a real issue".

What if he never knew?

The Maharshal (Yam Shel Shlomo) says the rule that practice confirms status applies only if the child knew he had a choice. If he was never told, he may object at any age, even sixty, however many years he lived as a Jew. This is not a rare scenario, as many parents do not tell their children.

The Hatam Sofer's problem

Take a nine year old boy who was converted, and who learns shehitah and slaughters for the community. He drinks wine with the family and touches their wine. At thirteen he is told he has a choice and objects. Retroactively he was always a non-Jew. The meat he slaughtered is non-kosher, the wine he touched is forbidden, and if he married a Jewish girl young, she is now a zonah, disqualified from marrying a Kohen.

The Hatam Sofer (cited in the Pitchei Teshuvah) answers that we do not need to worry, because it is so unlikely: "anan sahadei", we assume that a child brought up in a Jewish home will not object.

Rav Moshe Feinstein: tell them before they marry

Rav Moshe accepts the Hatam Sofer's assumption for a twelve or thirteen year old who is still under his parents' influence. But if the child was never told, he is in doubt whether we can say the same about an eighteen year old, who has desires and a world in front of him. A girl converted as a child may marry, have children, and find out twenty-five years later when her father dies. She may then object, and her children would be non-Jewish. Rav Moshe therefore says it is wrong to allow a boy or girl to marry without having been told of the conversion. Keeping the secret is not realistic, since it will come out eventually.

The Rabbi's conclusion: converted children should be told as early as possible, before twelve or thirteen. Answering a student, he said one wants a positive opt-in but cannot force it. Saying nothing is worse, because the ramifications are huge: think of a sofer who has written thousands of mezuzot and at fifty learns that he is not Jewish.

Asked whether this matters for the laws of wine, the Rabbi said that stam yeinam applies even to a minor non-Jew, whatever his beliefs.

A fetus converted with its mother

The Pitchei Teshuvah links this to an earlier class. A child who converted in the womb with its mother cannot object, if we take the view that the fetus is part of its mother. Anyone immersed after birth can object until bar or bat misvah.

Can a convert hold a position of authority?

The Torah says "you shall place a king over you from among your brothers" (Devarim 17:15), and the Gemara in Yevamot (45b) learns from it that every appointment of authority (serarah) must be from born Jews.

  • Someone whose mother is Jewish and whose father is not may be a dayan for everything. The only exceptions are halitzah, which needs a full Jewish lineage, and being king.
  • A convert (mother not Jewish) may judge fellow converts but not Israel. The Rosh holds that even financial cases are forbidden to him, and the Shulhan Arukh follows the Rosh.
  • The poskim add that this applies only when the Beit Din imposes itself. If both parties choose him, he may adjudicate, as anyone may. The same is said of Devorah: some Rishonim say she was not a judge but was accepted.

The Gemara tells of Rav Mari bar Rachel, whose father was not Jewish and whose mother was, and who was appointed a parnas. He is called after his mother, and from this we learn that in a get the mother's name is written where the father is not Jewish. The Mishnah in Sotah tells of King Agrippas, whose mother was Jewish and father not. When he read the verse about not appointing a foreign king, the Sages told him "you are our brother", and the Mishnah says they were liable for flattering him. Tosafot explain that although his mother was Jewish, the king is a special case, since the verse repeats the word "brothers" and requires both parents to be Jewish.

Modern questions

  • A convert as a member of the Israeli Knesset. Rav Herzog ruled that he may. His reasons: the voters accepted him, an MP is a representative who can be removed at any time, and the prohibition may cover only a lifelong appointment.
  • A convert as a Rosh Yeshiva. Rav Moshe Feinstein permits it. The Rabbi explained that the head of a yeshiva is like the head of a company, not a position of true authority. Rav Moshe adds that the commandment to love the convert calls us to be lenient in these matters.
  • A mashgiach kashrut. Rav Moshe held that a woman cannot be a mashgiach, since a mashgiach acts against the wishes of the owner, which is serarah, and by that logic neither could a convert. The Rabbi doubted how much weight this has today.
  • A rabbi of a congregation. The Rabbi said a rabbi today is under the congregation's authority, so it is not serarah, and he doubted that anyone would object to a convert as rabbi.
  • A police officer. A student asked whether a convert could serve in Israel. The Rabbi was unsure, but could hear an argument that an officer merely carries out the law and can be dismissed, so it is not serarah.

The Sephardi approach

The Rabbi finished with Rav Uziel, who says we are commanded to bring people close. When a Jewish spouse is involved, we should encourage the conversion so that the children are Jewish. If the couple are not yet married and can be discouraged from marriage, we should try. If they cannot, Rav Uziel says he is ready to act as a "hedyot", to convert them even lishem ishut, to save them from intermarriage, though he recognises this is not how the Gemara would have it. He also cites the idea of zera Yisrael: a child of a Jewish father and non-Jewish mother carries the seed of Judaism, so there is a misvah to convert him.

Practical summary

  • A convert child may object after bar or bat misvah until he or she has acted as a Jew; Shulhan Arukh follows the Rosh.
  • A valid objection works retroactively: the child was never Jewish.
  • A child who never knew of the right may object at any age (Maharshal).
  • The Hatam Sofer relies on the assumption that children will not object; Rav Moshe says a child must be told, earlier is better, and must not marry without knowing.
  • A convert whose mother was not Jewish cannot sit as a dayan or impose himself on Israel, but may be a Rosh Yeshiva and seemingly a rabbi.
  • A person with a Jewish mother and a non-Jewish father may hold any position except halitzah judge and king.
  • On Rav Moshe's logic a convert seemingly cannot be a mashgiach kashrut, though the Rabbi doubted this today.
