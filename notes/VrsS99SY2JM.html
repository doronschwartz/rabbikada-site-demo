Topic: Sukkot as the time to recharge our happiness: journey, gathering and death Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Israeli Embassy, London, on the eve of Sukkot, Friday 25 September 2026

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording. Parts of the recording are hard to follow, and only what is clear is reported here.

The happiest time of the year

Standing beside a symbolic sukkah a few hours before the festival, the Rabbi asked the room which is the happiest time of the year. His answer was Sukkot, 100 percent. In our prayers the three festivals have their own titles: Pesah is the time of our freedom, Shavuot the time of the giving of our Torah, and Sukkot Zeman Simhatenu, the time of our happiness. In the Torah itself, he said, Sukkot is clearly the happiest festival. The Rambam, he noted, describes Sukkot as a time to recharge our happiness. On Pesah we relive the idea of freedom rather than just commemorate it, and over these eight days we do the same with happiness. Happiness, then, is not binary, a matter of being either a happy or an unhappy person. It is something to work on, and the festival gives us eight days a year to do so. He promised five ideas on how Sukkot can teach us about happiness, and developed the following in detail.

Idea one: happiness is a journey

Sukkot is the only festival that is not tied to a fixed place. Pesah and Purim are about exile, Shavuot about Mount Sinai, Hanukkah about the Temple. Sukkot commemorates forty years of travelling in the desert, living in temporary huts. If Sukkot is about happiness and about a journey, then happiness is a journey. We often think "when I reach X, or get Y, or get this promotion, I will be happy." The Torah teaches that the journey itself can be the happiest part. He linked this to the psychology of flow: an activity that is too easy bores us and one that is too hard gives stress and anxiety, but a person who is developing, in work, relationships, sport or health, is engaged. A person who is static in life is the one who becomes depressed. The destination is not the climax, and we should enjoy the present and the journey. He added that retirement makes little sense from a Torah perspective: you cannot retire an identity, and until the day we die every day is part of a journey.

Idea two: gathering and death

Sukkot has another name in the Torah, Hag HaAsif, the festival of gathering, because the harvest is gathered in at this time of year. He asked where the root of the word "asaf" first appears in the Torah. The answer is in the expression for dying: the patriarchs, when they died, "were gathered to their people." At its root, the word means to die and join your ancestors, and only later does it come to mean gathering produce. So at its core, he said, Sukkot has something to do with death. He asked the room to imagine living 5,000 years, rather than the 80 or so we may expect. Would we approach each day differently? With endless time we would not appreciate any single moment, because there would always be millions more. Limited time gives us structure and a framework and makes us value the moment. Every moment that passes will never return, and recognising that should not depress us but push us to live now, instead of waiting for what will happen in five or ten years. Every aspect of Sukkot is linked with temporariness. The Halacha requires the sukkah roof to be made of something that grows from the ground and can wither, not something permanent. The willow branches we wave are also a plant that wilts. He compared this with the Grim Reaper and his sickle, an image that joins harvest and death.

Why we read Kohelet

He then asked why we read Kohelet on Sukkot, the book he described as the most depressing in the Tanakh, with its refrain that everything is vain. One reason he gave is that Sukkot is so happy a time that we are brought down a little so that we do not get carried away. His own point was that contemplating mortality properly does not make us morbid: it gives us a deep appreciation of every moment.

The final point

In the last section, which is difficult to follow on the recording, he turned to the Torah's command to rejoice on the festival, which lists our son and daughter, our servants, and the stranger, suggesting that happiness is not meant to be a private matter.

Recap and blessing

He recapped: first, the journey, and the flow of life and of our identity; second, death and temporariness, which make every moment fresh. He blessed the room that over the eight days we think about happiness and work on it. He noted that the American Constitution speaks of "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness," not "happiness" itself, because happiness is something we pursue, work on and recharge, and then use as a springboard for the whole year. He tied this to the spirit of Israel, which despite tragedy and existential threats continues to persevere and make the best of the world.

Summary

  • Sukkot is the festival of happiness, and the Rambam describes it as a time to recharge it.
  • Happiness is not binary; it is a skill and a pursuit to work on.
  • Sukkot is the festival of the journey, so we should enjoy the journey and not wait for a destination.
  • Hag HaAsif is rooted in the word for death, and Sukkot's temporary structures remind us that every moment is limited and valuable.
  • Awareness of mortality, as in Kohelet, deepens our joy.
