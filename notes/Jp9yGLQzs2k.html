These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Back to basics

The series was the community's own idea, and the Rabbi thanked them for it. "Sometimes you have to get the basics," he said. We pray throughout our lives, some daily, some weekly, some less often, yet we do not always understand what we are doing, why, or how. The plan was to work through the Shabbat morning service, the one most people attend, to see which parts matter most and where a person should focus. But first, as with anything, comes the history.

Prayer in the Torah

Nowhere does the Torah command us to pray. Yet the Avot and others pray throughout Tanakh: Avraham prays for Sedom, Yaakov prays to be saved from Esav, Moshe prays for Miriam with the words "El na refa na lah", still used today when we pray for the sick, and Hannah prays for a child.

The Rabbi compared this with belief in God. There is no verse that says "believe in God". "I am the Lord your God who took you out of Egypt" is about a God involved in our history. Some things are so basic that no verse is needed.

The source is in the Shema: "to love the Lord your God and to serve Him with all your heart". You can serve God with sacrifices, with money or with your body. What is service of the heart? Hazal answer: that is tefillah. So prayer, at its core, is in the heart.

Better less, with meaning

Imagine a choice: say every word without understanding, or think every word in your mind without saying it. Prayer is "service of the heart", so it has to start with the heart. A Hasidic teacher compared prayer without the heart to a body without a soul. So it is better to pray less and understand it than to pray more and not understand.

At its core, the obligatory part of the morning service is the Shema with its blessings and the Amidah, perhaps 10 to 15 minutes. Over the centuries more was added at the beginning and the end, which is lovely, but people then see a three-hour service and lose the core. That does not mean skipping parts. One should work towards understanding, because ultimately it starts in the heart.

1,500 years without a Siddur

The Rambam (Hilchot Tefillah 1) writes that the misvah is to pray every day: to praise God, then ask for one's needs, then give thanks, each person according to his ability, in his own words, as often as he wished. This was the practice from Moshe until Ezra, roughly 1,500 years, with no prayer book and no fixed text.

Why did that change? Someone suggested that people needed structure and guidance. The Rabbi agreed and added the Temple. While it stood, worship centred on it: you came to the Temple at the festivals and brought offerings, and that is where you turned in need.

The Rambam continues: in the exile of Babylon the people mixed with other nations, and their language became a mixture. People could no longer express themselves properly. So Ezra and his court established the eighteen blessings, three of praise, three of thanks and the requests in between, so that everyone could learn them and the prayers of those who struggled would be as complete as those of the eloquent. "Essentially the fixed service we have is a concession. It wasn't meant to be like this."

Women, the Amidah and the minyan

Women are exempt from time-bound positive misvot, such as the sukkah, the lulav or the shofar, which the Rabbi explained by the traditional reason that historically women were responsible for raising children. But the original misvah of prayer has no fixed time, so women are obliged to pray. The set times came later with Ezra; a woman fulfils the original obligation by praying once a day, at any time.

When we speak of tefillah, the core is the Amidah, also called Shemoneh Esreh. It is the only part of the service where one must stand, which is why it is said quietly and without interruption. "If you only have five minutes to pray, just pray Amidah." For a boy or man putting on tefillin in the morning, the minimum is the Shema and the Amidah.

Someone asked whether the fixed text was a good idea. One person felt it keeps everyone together across the exile. Another felt prayer in one's own words might be more meaningful. The Rabbi's answer was balance: left open-ended, many people would simply never pray, but reciting a text by rote is also not prayer. He suggested sometimes reading the optional parts slowly in one's mind to think about their meaning, while the obligatory parts must be said with the lips. And the Amidah keeps a place for one's own words: before finishing, and within the relevant blessing, one may add a personal request, for a meeting, an exam or someone's operation.

The minyan, too, was an addition, not part of the original prayer. One can pray alone. Kaddish, however, sanctifies God's name and needs ten men.

Someone asked whether the Amidah may be said in English. The Rabbi said yes, one can, though it is better in Hebrew; learning it in English for a while and then returning to Hebrew is a good path. On Shabbat we make no personal requests, because Shabbat is about living in the present.

Four kinds of prayer

The Rabbi listed four kinds of prayer: requests, thanks, acknowledgement and praise of God, and confession and repentance. Praise is not because God needs it but because we need to recognise Him. Thanks teaches gratitude. Confession is part of who we are. Kabbalat Shabbat, where we greet Shabbat as a bride, is song rather than prayer.

Why ask God for anything?

The hardest kind is requests. We believe God is entirely good. If someone is ill, that must be for his good. So why pray for it to change? The handout puts the classic question: either God has decided that a person should receive a benefit, so prayer is unnecessary, or He has not, so how can prayer change His will?

The answer is that prayer changes the person, not God. If we take it seriously and pray from the heart, we introspect and become different people, and a different person may receive a different decree. The Rabbi gave an analogy. A doctor tells a patient who has neglected his health that he has a month to live. The patient changes his diet and exercises; the doctor now says the leg must be amputated. He keeps improving, and finally the doctor says he is fine. The doctor did not change his mind; each diagnosis fit the patient as he was. So too, prayer does not change God's mind. It changes us, and the "diagnosis" changes with us.

He left a question for the next class: how does asking for our own needs lead to introspection?

Summary

  • The Torah never commands prayer explicitly, but "serving God with the heart" is understood as tefillah, so prayer starts in the heart.
  • For about 1,500 years people prayed in their own words, at any time; Ezra fixed the eighteen blessings as a concession to exile.
  • The core of the service is the Shema with its blessings and the Amidah; better to pray less with understanding than more without it.
  • Women are obliged to pray, since the original misvah has no fixed time.
  • Prayer does not change God; it changes the one who prays.
