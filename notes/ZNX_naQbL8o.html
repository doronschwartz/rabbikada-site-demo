Where we are

The feedback from the first shiur was that people wanted more time on the principles of deciding Hilchot Shabbat. "Once you know all the principles, three quarters of the questions of Hilchot Shabbat you can answer yourself." The core principle is melekhet mahshevet: the Torah forbids thoughtful, purposeful work. Its conditions include intention, usefulness, permanence, and doing it in the normal way.

The first shiur covered intention: pesik reisha (an inevitable consequence), and the Sefaradi leniency where the inevitable result is unwanted and only rabbinic, which Ashkenazim are strict about. Tonight: finishing intention, then melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah.

Pesik reisha through a non-Jew

Asking a non-Jew to do melakhah, amirah lenokhri, is rabbinically forbidden. The Rishonim give reasons: the Hakhamim treated him as your agent for Shabbat, and the verse in Yeshayahu teaches that even our speech on Shabbat must be different.

Yet the Rema rules, from the Terumat HaDeshen, that where it is very cold, which halacha treats as a risk of illness, one may ask a non-Jew to light the fire, and he may first put the pot on the stove. We ask him to light it for warmth; the food heating up is an inevitable side effect. That is pesik reisha through a non-Jew, and it is permitted. Asking him to light it in order to heat the food would be forbidden.

The fridge. Even with an old filament bulb, where opening the fridge yourself would be a problem, you may ask a non-Jew to open the fridge so you can take out food. The light coming on is an inevitable consequence of something he may be asked to do. The Mishnah Berurah says this clearly. With today's LED fridge lights, the first shiur had already found reason to be lenient.

Gerama: indirect action

The source. The Mishnah allows placing jars of water in the path of a fire so that the fire is put out when it reaches them; Rabbi Yose forbids. The Gemara concludes that both agree the Torah forbids only doing a melakhah, not causing it indirectly. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh (334:22) rule that one may make a barrier of vessels full of water, because it is gerama.

What is gerama? The simple understanding, followed by Hazon Ovadia and most poskim, is that the result does not happen immediately; it takes time. Example: a sukkah roof full of rain over grass. Opening it so the water falls onto the grass is watering, a melakhah. If the water lands on concrete and then trickles into the grass, that is gerama.

Is gerama permitted outright? Here the Rishonim disagree. The Mordekhai quotes Rabbenu Yoel that gerama is rabbinically forbidden, and was permitted only to prevent loss. The Rema rules this way, so for Ashkenazim gerama is only permitted for loss, a misvah or illness. The Taz disagrees and permits gerama outright, and the Vilna Gaon seems to agree. A simple reading of the Shulhan Arukh suggests he permits it outright, but some Aharonim argue he was only discussing fire.

The Shabbat timer. Hazon Ovadia rules that you may extend a timer, so that the lights stay on longer, and Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach agrees; the timer is not mukseh. But you may not bring it forward so that the lights go off earlier, even though that only happens later, unless there is real need such as illness. The same applies to an ice maker: letting it keep going is fine; causing a new cycle to start is not.

The Rabbi concluded that the consensus of both Ashkenazi and Sefaradi poskim is not to rely on gerama alone, though some, such as the Zera Emet, permit it outright and can be joined to other reasons. On Yom Tov, even Ashkenazim permit gerama, for example placing a candle where the wind will later blow it out.

Pesik reisha with gerama

Now the two come together. Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach (Minhat Shlomo) and Hazon Ovadia argue: if pesik reisha through a non-Jew is permitted, though asking a non-Jew is itself rabbinic, then surely pesik reisha through gerama is permitted. So a hut used during the year, with no misvah involved, may be opened even though the rain will fall on concrete and trickle into the grass. You open it to sit there; the watering is inevitable and indirect.

Mixed motives. Someone asked: what if the person is also glad the grass gets watered? The Rabbi answered that the question is what you are doing it for. If you open the roof to sit in the hut and are happy about the grass, that is pesik reisha. If you really want to water the grass and the hut is an excuse, it is no different from using a hose. If both intentions are equal, it is forbidden.

When the process is the melakhah. The Gemara in Bava Kamma asks why someone who fans a fire together with the wind is exempt from damages, yet winnowing, which also uses the wind, is a melakhah. The Rosh explains: winnowing is always done with the wind, so for that melakhah the indirect process is the normal way. Likewise trapping by closing a door, or cooking, which takes hours, are not gerama. "Gerama isn't just indirect, straight out it's fine. If it's part of the process, then it's not mutar."

Asking a child. Someone asked about telling a child to do it. The Rabbi called this a common misconception: in almost every case asking a child is as bad as doing it yourself. The difference is only in the severity of the punishment.

Melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah

The Mishnah in Shabbat: someone who digs a hole because he needs the earth, not the hole, is exempt. This follows Rabbi Shimon, who holds that a melakhah not needed for its own purpose is not forbidden by the Torah.

Two definitions. Rashi, and the Rambam with him, say it is a melakhah done to get rid of something, such as carrying out a dead body. Tosafot say it is a melakhah done for a different purpose than in the Mishkan. Trapping a mosquito: according to Rashi, you want to be rid of it; according to Tosafot, unlike the Mishkan, you do not want its hide. Expressing milk to relieve an animal's pain is another example.

The ruling. Almost all the Rishonim follow Rabbi Shimon. The Rambam stands out against them and holds it is forbidden by the Torah, "which makes Shabbat much more complicated for the Rambam than it does for us." The Shulhan Arukh brings the main view in two places (316 on trapping, 334:27 on a dangerous coal) but also quotes the Rambam. The Mishnah Berurah concludes that the halacha follows the first view.

Plucking hairs. In 340 the Shulhan Arukh says one who plucks two hairs is liable, though this looks like a melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah. The Magen Avraham says he must be using the hairs; the Vilna Gaon says here he followed the Rambam. The Rabbi preferred the answer brought in the Biur Halacha: in the Mishkan the hides were smoothed by removing hair, so removing hair to make something look better is the Mishkan's purpose.

The most severe rabbinic prohibition. Asked why, a participant answered correctly: with every other deficiency the action itself is different. Here the action is identical; only the thought makes it rabbinic. Next time: how this differs from pesik reisha, and shinui.

Practical summary

  • Pesik reisha through a non-Jew is permitted: you may ask him to do something permitted even if a melakhah inevitably follows.
  • Gerama is not forbidden by the Torah, but the poskim, Ashkenazi and Sefaradi, generally permit it only for loss, a misvah or illness. On Yom Tov it is permitted.
  • You may extend a Shabbat timer but not bring it forward without real need.
  • Pesik reisha that happens through gerama is permitted even without a special need.
  • Where the melakhah is normally done through a process, such as cooking or winnowing, there is no leniency of gerama.
  • Melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah is rabbinic, but it is the most severe rabbinic prohibition. Asking a child to do a melakhah is almost always as bad as doing it yourself.
