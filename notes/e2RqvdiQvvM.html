Where We Are

The previous classes reached the Seder night: reclining, the four cups and the obligation of women and children (Simanim 471-472). This class continues with Siman 473. Seif 1 deals mainly with Kiddush when the Seder falls on Shabbat, so the Rabbi skips it and begins at seif 2.

Part 1: The Cups, and Eating During Maggid (Seifim 2-3)

Seif 2. The second cup is drunk reclining, with all the halachot already learnt, and no after-berakhah follows it. Sepharadim hold that the berakhah over the first cup covers all the cups. We only say a new berakhah on the third cup because Birkat HaMazon has come in between. Without Birkat HaMazon, one berakhah would cover all four. The berakhah on the third cup then covers the fourth.

Seif 3. One may drink more than four cups, but should not drink between the first and second cups unless there is great need, so as not to become unable to finish the Haggadah. The Mishnah Berurah and the poskim say that the concern is only with intoxicating drinks. Water and other drinks are fine.

Food is not a problem either, so one may eat during the Haggadah if there is a need. It is still better not to, for two reasons:

  • To keep an appetite for the massah. "Even if I'm absolutely starving... it's still better not to eat a large amount."
  • If one eats a kezayit, one must say an after-berakhah. That ends the Borei Peri HaAdamah said on the Karpas. Since it is unclear whether a new berakhah would then be said on the Maror, the berakhah question is best avoided. If someone had to eat, he does not make a second HaAdamah on the Maror.

Why is it unclear? A vegetable such as lettuce can be eaten with bread, which suggests that the meal's berakhah covers it. But the Maror is eaten specifically as a misvah, and before the meal.

Part 2: The Seder Plate (Seif 4)

The Shulhan Arukh: one brings to the table a ke'arah (plate) with three massot, Maror, Haroset, Karpas (or any other vegetable; celery is typical), vinegar or salt water, and two cooked dishes, "one in memory of the Korban Pesah and one in memory of the Korban Hagigah": meat and an egg.

The massot belong on the plate. That is the plain reading, and it works with smaller hand massot. According to the Arizal, the three massot sit above the seven other items, corresponding to Keter, Hokhmah and Binah over the lower sephirot.

The Seder plate is not ancient. "The whole concept of a Seder plate is in itself not later than 700 years old." Before then, what was needed was simply on the table. The Shulhan Arukh just says "a plate" and sets no order. The order comes from the Rema, and the familiar arrangement from the Arizal's Kabbalah. "Every year you go to people's houses and they start to argue... really it's not worth arguing about."

The Rema's order is practical: what is used first is placed nearest, so that one never passes over a misvah. The Karpas and salt water go on top, then the massot, then the Maror, and the zeroa and the egg at the end.

Why the two dishes? Our Seder is incomplete without the real Korban Pesah. Erev Pesah in the Temple was "probably one of the happiest days" of the Jewish year: every group gathered, roasting its lamb while singing Hallel. So the Gemara requires a reminder of the Korban Pesah, and adds one for the Korban Hagigah, which was brought on every festival.

  • Meat resembles the meat of the sacrifice.
  • An egg is round, like mourning, and recalls our sadness that there is no sacrifice.
  • The shank bone is roasted and the egg is cooked, because the Korban Pesah was roasted while the Hagigah was cooked.

Part 3: Roasting, Cooking and Baking (a digression for Shabbat)

Halacha has three terms:

Term

Meaning

Seliyah

Roasting over an open flame, as on a barbecue

Bishul

Cooking with water (stewing, braising)

Afiyah

Baking in a closed space, as in an oven

On Shabbat, there is no cooking after cooking (ein bishul ahar bishul): a fully cooked dish may be reheated, under the usual conditions. Nor is there baking after baking. But is there cooking after roasting or baking?

The Torah on the Korban Pesah: "Do not eat of it raw, nor cooked in water, but roasted over fire." The Gemara in Pesahim learns from the double expression that even a lamb that was roasted and then cooked is forbidden. Tosafot proves from here that cooking can add a new dimension to food that is already fully roasted, so on Shabbat cooking a baked or roasted food would be Torah-level bishul. The answer to the proof: the Torah needed a special verse here precisely because everywhere else, "once something is cooked, it's cooked". That is the view the Rambam holds.

The Shulhan Arukh brings both views as "yesh... veyesh" (some say, others say). The rule that the halacha follows the second opinion is relatively weak, so Sepharadim have room to be strict. Some rely on the lenient view. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul does not want to rely on it where a Torah prohibition may be at stake. The Biur Halacha (Siman 318) adds that the whole question applies only between a wet and a dry method. Roasting after baking, or the reverse, is no problem for anyone.

Practically for the Seder: the shank bone should be roasted. The ones sold by kosher butchers usually come ready roasted. If you buy it raw, roast it rather than baking it in the oven.

Part 4: Maror and Hazeret (Seif 5)

The Mishnah in Pesahim lists the species for Maror: hazeret, ulshin (probably chicory), tamkha, harhavina and maror. Translations vary, but all are bitter herbs. The leaves and the stems count; the hard bottom of the lettuce does not count towards the kezayit.

"We Sepharadim don't really do the misvah of Maror properly." The Mishnah and the halacha clearly treat Maror and Hazeret as two different things, yet most of us use the same vegetable for both. Some Sepharadim put something different for the Hazeret to show the difference.

What is Hazeret? Lekhathilah the misvah is done with Hazeret, the bitterest herb and the closest to the original. It is unlikely to be our sweet modern lettuce. Lettuce became the custom because that is what our countries had. Ashkenazim translated it as horseradish, probably also from local produce, and horseradish may not grow in the Middle East at all. The Rabbi recalls that research points to chicory.

A participant's question: if chicory is the best Hazeret, shouldn't it be used for the first eating, with the berakhah, and lettuce for the Korekh sandwich? The Rabbi: "This has bothered me for years... The honest answer is I don't know." The basic rule is simple: any bitter herb fulfils Maror. Hazeret is only the best way to do it (misvah min hamuvhar), "which is why we don't go crazy" about identifying it. The open question is whether our lettuce counts as bitter at all.

Part 5: The Second Cup and the Questions (Seif 6)

The second cup is poured before Maggid so that the Haggadah is said over a cup, the same cup over which Hallel is said at the end. (This is another criticism of the custom of pouring wine out at the ten plagues: the head of the household ends up saying the rest of the Haggadah over an almost empty cup.)

If the child does not ask, the father must still teach him. If there is no child, a man asks his wife. If he is alone, he asks himself. This is straight from the Mishnah, and it applies to women as much as to men. "This happened a lot during COVID."

Why ask even when alone? Leaving Egypt was intellectual freedom as well as physical freedom. When Moshe asked Pharaoh to lighten the load, Pharaoh's answer was to increase the work so that the people would have no time to think. Asking a question challenges the status quo, so the story must be told through question and answer, even to oneself.

A guest at someone else's Seder fulfils the misvah by listening, as long as he understands. The Gemara has one person reading for everyone. This probably works through the Torah's command to tell and discuss, not through shome'a ke'oneh, since we never find that the leader must intend to fulfil it for others, as we do with the shofar. Still, a misvah is better done oneself than through an agent (misvah bo yoter mibeshelucho), so pause at the key points and explain them to your children. "If you're just reading the Haggadah in Hebrew, which most people don't understand, you haven't done the misvah."

Leaning at one's father's Seder needs his permission, although we presume that a father allows it.

Even sages ask each other, as the five rabbis did in Bnei Brak. The Maharil: if the child has already asked his own questions (why pour now? why lean?), Ma Nishtanah is not needed. Avadim Hayinu is the answer to the questions, and Ma Nishtanah is "a springboard" to reach it.

Part 6: Massot Uncovered, and the Wine at the Plagues (Seif 7 and the Rema)

The plate is removed for Ma Nishtanah to prompt the questions, then returned. The Haggadah is said with the massot uncovered because massah is "bread over which many things are answered". This is not essential: without a plate or massot, the Haggadah is still said.

  • At "Massah zo", lift up the massah (check your Haggadah for which one), and at "Maror zeh", the Maror.
  • The zeroa is not lifted, because it is only a reminder, not the real thing. Ashkenazim point to it.
  • At Hallel, each person holds his own cup until the end of the berakhah.

Spilling wine at the plagues. The Rema brings this custom (sixteen times in all) from the Maharil. It was a German custom, unknown 800 years ago; the Arizal grew up among Ashkenazim, added Kabbalistic meaning to it, and it spread "like wildfire".

His approach: "If this is your minhag, do it. But try to think of rational reasons." His reason: even while celebrating, we do not rejoice when God's creatures suffer ("My creatures are drowning in the sea and you sing?"), which is why only half Hallel is said on the seventh day. Wine was precious, so pouring some away expresses that sadness. The wine itself is harmless, and since it has a purpose this is not bal tashhit.

Summary

  • Cups: no after-berakhah after the second cup; only intoxicating drinks are a concern between the cups; eating during Maggid is allowed if needed, but better avoided.
  • Plate: three massot, Maror, Haroset, Karpas, salt water, a roasted bone and a cooked egg. The arrangement comes from the Rema and the Arizal, so it is not worth arguing about.
  • Cooking after roasting: a dispute between Tosafot and the Rambam; the Shulhan Arukh brings both views. Roast the shank bone.
  • Maror: any bitter herb fulfils it; Hazeret is the best, probably chicory rather than modern lettuce.
  • Questions: asked even when alone, as an expression of intellectual freedom; explain to your family even at someone else's Seder.
