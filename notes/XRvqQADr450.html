Topic: Holding the Sefer Torah without a cloth, touching books, and the misvah of Gelilah, and why it is hard to understand for communities that keep the Torah in a case Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 147:1-6 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 19 January 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two kinds of Sefer Torah

After a short review of the previous class, the Rabbi introduced Siman 147, the laws of Gelilah. He deliberately did not translate the word at first, "and you'll see why." He said this would be one of the last two classes on the Sefer Torah before turning to Purim and Pesah.

He opened with a frank admission. Most of the time the Sefaradim are more faithful to the simple reading of the Gemara and to ancient practice. "This chapter is a proof this isn't always the case." Here, the Ashkenazi way of handling the Sefer Torah is the more precise one.

Two background points:

  1. The case (tik). The Eastern Sefaradim keep the Sefer Torah in a rigid case. The Western Sefaradim, meaning the Spanish and Portuguese, the Italians, many Moroccans and the Bukharian Jews, use a soft mantle like the Ashkenazim. There is no evidence for the case before about 800 or 900 years ago, and the Rambam never mentions it.
  2. Hagbahah. As learned earlier in the series, Hagbahah was originally done before the reading, as Sefaradim do. Ashkenazim moved it to the end because people would see the scroll lifted and leave.

The Gemara speaks of Gelilah as a great misvah: the one who does Gelilah receives the reward of all who read. Gelilah means rolling. For Ashkenazim, that is clear: after the reading the scroll is rolled, bound and dressed. For a scroll in a case, the Rabbi asked, where is there any rolling? "All there is is the closing of the box."

Holding the Sefer Torah "naked" (147:1)

The Shulhan Arukh rules that it is forbidden to hold a Sefer Torah arom, without a cloth. The Gemara says that whoever does so "is buried naked." The Mishnah Berurah explains this as losing the reward of the misvah he was doing, such as the reading, Hagbahah or Gelilah. The prohibition applies even to touching the parchment, even with washed hands, unless there is a real need such as sewing or repairs.

So when the columns become uneven while rolling, one straightens them through a cloth, a mitpahat, not with the hand. That is the Ashkenazi practice, and the Rabbi said it should be the halacha for us as well, since it is what the Shulhan Arukh says.

The Arizal's leniency

Sefaradim are often less careful, rolling a scroll with bare hands when it is open at the wrong place. This is based on the Arizal, who held that the Gemara's prohibition applies only during the reading. The Ben Ish Hai brings this, the Kaf HaHayim as well, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph rules this way, especially where tirha deSibura is involved, since rolling by the edges alone is slow.

The Rabbi called this "a rare instance where the Kabbalah comes and makes it lenient," against the Shulhan Arukh.

  • During the reading: never touch the parchment. If the last page sticks, use a cloth. Here there is no leniency.
  • Not during the reading, for example rolling to the right place: there is what to rely on to use one's hands, but it is still better to use a mitpahat.

The Rambam understood the Gemara differently. "Holding it naked" means holding it while the person himself is not properly dressed, and "buried naked" is meant literally. According to him there is no problem touching the parchment at all. The Shulhan Arukh did not follow this.

Someone asked whether this is connected to the rabbinic decree that touching scrolls makes the hands impure, said to have arisen because terumah was stored near the scrolls and mice were drawn to them. The Rabbi said that is a separate law. This one is about honour. "Your hands touch everywhere."

He described a beautiful Spanish and Portuguese custom: a silk cloth wound inside the scroll across several columns, so that the reader's hands touch silk rather than parchment. It has to be moved along every few parashiyot.

Touching other books

The Rema mentions some who apply this to other holy books, but says the custom is not to be strict. The Mishnah Berurah explains that it would only apply to books written like a Sefer Torah, with its script and ink. Our printed books, including a printed Haftarah book, carry no such sanctity and may be touched freely.

But that is the default case. If you know your hands touched something unclean, such as your shoes or a normally covered part of the body, you must wash before touching holy books or learning. The Rabbi said this is why the Hazon Ish disliked short sleeves, since one is more likely to touch the upper arm. He added that the arm on which one puts tefillin is not counted as a covered part.

Who should do Gelilah (147:1)

The Shulhan Arukh quotes the Gemara: the greatest of those called up does the Gelilah. But, the Rabbi said, "as often happens, important things get hijacked by the wealthy." The Shulhan Arukh records that the custom became to sell it for large sums.

Who is the greatest of those called up? Customs differ. In Eastern Sefaradi communities, following the Kabbalah, Shishi corresponds to Yesod, so the rabbi is called for Shishi. In the Spanish and Portuguese community Shishi carries no such significance.

The Mishnah Berurah explains that what the Gemara called Gelilah is what Ashkenazim today call Hagbahah, now done at the end, so the greatest reward goes to the one who lifts. The Hazon Ish and Rav Hayim Kanievsky disagreed, holding that Gelilah means rolling. In Ashkenazi and Spanish and Portuguese communities Gelilah is given to children, to educate them. The best explanation he had seen: children are the pure ones.

So what is Gelilah for Sefaradim?

The Rabbi argued it cannot be Hagbahah, since Sefaradim lift before the reading, when no one has yet been called. The Shulhan Arukh speaks in the past tense, of "those who were called." And there is no rolling. What is left is closing the case and making sure it rests correctly. Some explain that this is why Shevi'i became so prominent: the last one closes the scroll. He found it difficult. "Although most of the time we're right, we're not always right."

Someone asked why Gelilah is so highly valued. For a rolled scroll, the Rabbi answered, the next paragraphs show it is a kind of farewell: rolling it carefully so it rests safely all week, ending at a seam so that any tear will be at the stitching and not across a column of text.

Dressing the scroll (147:2)

If you were given Gelilah, does that include dressing the scroll? No. Another person may be honoured with handing the mantle to the one rolling, and the roller cannot object, even if he bought the Gelilah. This shows that the Shulhan Arukh's scroll was rolled and dressed like an Ashkenazi one.

The Rema adds that where taking the scroll out and returning it are sold, the hazan cannot object. Each honour is separate, and whoever buys one gets only that. The Rabbi said he once thought selling the putting on of the rimonim was "making money out of nothing," but this source supports it. It is still best for the one who took the scroll out to return it, since one who begins a misvah should complete it.

He closed with the Spanish and Portuguese rule that one may only perform the levantar, the Hagbahah, after at least three practice sessions and an examination before three elders, on three Sifrei Torah, including one heavy on one side, like Bereshit or VeZot HaBerakhah.

Practical summary

  • Do not touch the parchment of a Sefer Torah with bare hands; use a mitpahat or the rollers.
  • During the reading there is no leniency. When rolling to the right place outside the reading, Sefaradim may rely on the Arizal, Ben Ish Hai and Rav Ovadia Yoseph to use their hands, though a cloth is better.
  • Printed books may be touched freely, but wash your hands first if they touched something unclean.
  • Gelilah was meant for the greatest of those called up; today it is often sold or given to children.
  • Each honour around the Sefer Torah is separate, and whoever buys one receives only that one.
