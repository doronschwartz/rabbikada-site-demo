Topic: Who must light, guests and households, where the candles must be, lighting when you have electric light, and acceptance of Shabbat by the community Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263:8-14 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 5 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (early 2014, estimated).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two obligations: the person and the place

The class began with a distinction from the previous week. There is a personal obligation to light (chiyuv gavra) and an obligation that the place be lit (chiyuv makom). A man who stays alone in a hotel room while his wife lights at home has no personal obligation, since his wife exempts him, but the room needs light, so he must light. A man at home with his wife who lights has neither obligation. A guest with no room of his own and no wife lighting for him should contribute a little toward the host's candles and take part in them.

A guest who is part of the household (ben bayit) does not light separately. The Peri Hadash says that to count as part of the household you need to be there for seven days, and a Shabbat alone is not enough. It does not need to be a full week already past: if he arrives on Friday intending to stay until the next Friday, he is already a member of the household.

Several households at one table (263:8)

If two or three households eat at one table, Maran says only one should say the berakhah, because of the doubt about berakhot. The Rema says that Ashkenazim let each woman light and bless. The Kaf HaHayim reports, in the name of earlier authorities, that some Sefaradi communities followed the Rema, and the Rabbi said that from his wording this was the Kaf HaHayim's own custom.

Candles must be where you eat (263:9)

If you light in the dining room and then eat outside, for example on a balcony in the summer, the berakhah is in vain unless the candles are long enough to last until you come back and use them. The Rema's reasoning: if you eat with the candles there, the pleasure of the meal is enough, even though it is still daytime. If you eat elsewhere, the only purpose is that people do not stumble, which only applies when it gets dark.

The Rabbi separated the two purposes. Oneg Shabbat (the pleasure of the meal) only applies while you eat, even if the candles do not last until nightfall. Shalom bayit (not stumbling) applies for as long as people might be walking around. A family with young children who wake in the night needs some light in the house, even only in the hallway. If the candles help you find the plates, or you read, that is enough benefit, but you should still try to make them last until nightfall.

Electric light: what is the candle adding?

The Rabbi came back to a point from the previous week, the argument that modern electricity is not a "fire" because the supply is constantly renewed, like adding oil to a flame. He said that this is not how it works: even if the power station failed there would be energy for a while, and power cuts are very rare. So a normal bulb with a filament can count. Fluorescent lamps he left for later.

The harder question is what the candle adds when the house is already lit. The Rema says every extra candle adds festivity, so Ashkenazim can bless even with electricity on. Some Ashkenazi poskim say that with electricity the candle is worth nothing, so one should switch the lights off first. For Sefaradim, the Kaf HaHayim reports a custom like the Rema, and the Rabbi said that if your custom is that way you are fine. Otherwise the Ben Ish Hai suggests that, in Baghdad, when they ate outside in the moonlight, the first person to light is fulfilling a real obligation even if the light is not needed. A second person cannot make a berakhah on a second candle.

A practical solution is to switch the lights off before you light, then switch on and light the candles as one act. The Sefaradi order: switch off, make the berakhah, switch on, then light. The Ashkenazi order: switch off, switch on, light, then make the berakhah. In the summer, when the sun is still bright and the candles add nothing at 7.30, the candles must last until nightfall, or you can draw the curtains first. The best way is for the lady to have both the candles and the electric light in mind.

Different situations

  • A yeshiva student away from home. In the same time zone, he is covered by his parents' lighting. If a lamp post shines in, no one needs to light. In Israel, where the time is different, each boy in the room has a personal obligation. One of them should light for the others, taking turns.
  • A couple staying with another family. The best place is the bedroom, if the host agrees, and the candles must still be alight when they return. If the host does not allow it, a Sefaradi who follows the Kaf HaHayim may light at the table with a berakhah. Otherwise, they can hear the berakhah from the hostess.
  • Going out to eat after lighting. Make sure the candles last until you return, or stay home until a little after nightfall. If neither works, you may light where you eat, with a berakhah, if you follow the Rema, and the light must be used. Lighting must be after Plag HaMincha.
  • Hotels. If you cannot light in the room and the hotel has a candle area away from the meal, do not make a berakhah, because it adds nothing to the meal, according to either opinion. Someone suggested switching a bathroom light on with a berakhah; the Rabbi agreed this is possible, and said it is also good to hear a berakhah from an Ashkenazi lady lighting there.

Accepting Shabbat by prayer, and the community (263:10-14)

Someone who has prayed the evening prayer of Shabbat after Plag HaMincha has accepted Shabbat and may do no more melakhah. The Rema's rule about the majority is relevant today. If a town has one synagogue and most of the town has accepted Shabbat there, the minority follows. If the town has several synagogues, the smaller ones are not pulled along by a big early one. A home minyan has no fixed status, so those who attend follow the majority.

If you are a member of an early shul but stay home, you can say that you could have prayed at a later shul, so you may continue melakhah until that shul accepts. The Shulhan Arukh HaRav, the Ba'al HaTanya and the Kaf HaHayim bring this. The Shulhan Arukh leaves a doubt if you are Sefaradi and the later shul is Ashkenazi. The Rabbi found this hard to understand, since the earlier timing of Sefaradi shuls in London is a matter of custom. Someone who is unsure should ask his rabbi. In a place like London, where most people do not come to shul, he said that only those who normally attend are counted in the majority.

Rav Moshe Feinstein rules that if a husband accepts Shabbat early for halachic reasons, his wife follows. If he does so only for convenience, such as eating earlier with the children, she is not bound, though she should not do melakhot for him, such as cooking or sewing that he needs. A traveller who arrives in town and finds it has accepted Shabbat must put down what he is carrying.

Practical summary

  • Light where you will eat; candles must be there for the meal, and last until nightfall if you will not be eating with them.
  • With electricity on, the Rema allows the berakhah; Sefaradim who do not follow that custom switch lights off, make the berakhah, switch on, then light.
  • Guests who stay for Shabbat alone do not become part of the household and should join in the host's candles.
  • A hotel candle area away from where you eat does not give a berakhah.
  • If you are a latecomer and the congregation has accepted Shabbat, pray Mincha outside the hall, unless you have yourself accepted.
  • Wives are not automatically bound by an early acceptance by their husbands, if it was only for convenience.
