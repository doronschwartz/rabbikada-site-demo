These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Introduction: what is forbidden, and how seriously?

Siman 16 is about relations between a Jew and a non-Jew, and with a shifhah (a non-Jewish maidservant). The key seifim are Seif 1 and Seif 2. The Rabbi's central point is that there are three different levels here, and the halachic result depends on which level we are in.

He recalled Siman 4, where we learned that the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish, and set that aside. The question here is the prohibition itself.

The Gemara: why did Hazal need a decree?

The Torah forbids marriage to the seven nations (Lo titHaten bam). The Gemara asks why Hazal needed to forbid non-Jewish women at all, as part of the eighteen decrees. The answers:

  • The Torah's prohibition applies to the seven nations only, so Hazal added the other nations.
  • Rabbi Shimon bar Yohai holds that the Torah verse applies to all nations.
  • The verse speaks of a marriage. Hazal then dealt with a Jewish man and a non-Jewish woman, which the Torah did not cover.

From this the Rabbi drew out three levels:

Level

Status

Derekh ishut (a real partnership: civil marriage, living together as husband and wife)

A Torah prohibition. The Tanna Kamma says only the seven nations, Rabbi Shimon says all nations

Regular relations (a mistress, ongoing)

Dating from the Hasmonean court: the decree of niddah, shifhah, goyah and zonah

A one-off

A rabbinic prohibition, also with yihud

The big question of the Rishonim

The Gemara in Yevamot discusses a non-Jew who has not yet converted. If the Torah's Lo titHaten bam only applies after conversion, how can it be the source for a prohibition on marrying a non-Jew? The Rabbi reminded the class of the Siman 4 discussion of the netinim, where one view holds that this prohibition is rabbinic (and does not come from Lo titHaten bam) and the other that it is from the Torah but only after conversion.

  • The Rambam treats the prohibition as being derekh ishut and takes the verse to speak of marriage. This fits a prohibition that the Rambam holds to be rabbinic for the netinim.
  • Tosafot hold that the prohibition applies only after conversion and ask how the verse can then be the source for a non-Jew who has not converted.
  • The Ramban answers that the verse applies in both situations, before and after conversion.

An important point: according to the Rambam and the Ramban, Lo titHaten bam means derekh hatnut. According to Tosafot, the Torah prohibition might reach even something less than a marriage.

The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh (Seif 1)

The Rambam (Isurei Biah, chapter 12) rules like Rabbi Shimon: the Torah prohibition applies to all nations when it is derekh ishut. The Shulhan Arukh copies this almost word for word. The practical levels:

  1. A one-off. The Rambam rules it is rabbinic (mideRabbanan) and carries makkat mardut, lashes by rabbinic authority. For a Kohen the Rambam is stricter, and treats the woman as a zonah.
  2. Regular relations. More serious, still rabbinic, and with lashes.
  3. Marriage or a long-term partnership (derekh ishut). A Torah prohibition with malkot.

Then the Shulhan Arukh adds that "the zealots strike him" (kanaim pogim bo). The Rabbi explained, as in the Gemara and the Rambam, that this is only for a public act, in front of ten Jews, at the moment itself, and with an idol-worshipping woman, and that otherwise it is forbidden. He said it is remarkable that anyone may act without a Sanhedrin, and that it does not apply outside these conditions. He raised a doubt whether a Christian woman today would count as an idol worshipper, since Tosafot say Christians are not truly engaged in idol worship but continue their tradition, and the Rabbi was unsure.

He also noted that even where Beit Din does not punish, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say there is a heavenly punishment (karet), and that this is "not like other sins".

Yesh Holkin and the Beit Shemuel (Seif 1-2)

The Rema writes Yesh Holkin, and the commentators explain it. The Rabbi showed that there are two practical differences (nafka minot) if one learns like Tosafot, that the Torah prohibition is learned from Lo titHaten bam and applies to the seven nations:

  • A Torah prohibition applies only to the seven nations.
  • With the seven nations it applies even without derekh ishut, even for a single act.

Since the seven nations can no longer be identified, he added, nowadays the Torah-level prohibition does not apply in practice.

In Seif 2 the Shulhan Arukh does not repeat the law of the zealots. A commentator asks why, and the Rabbi left it open.

Summary of the siman for today

The Rabbi summed up what a Jewish man who is not a Kohen incurs today:

  • A one-off: a rabbinic prohibition (some say a Kohen becomes forbidden as with a zonah; some disagree).
  • Regular relations with the same woman: lashes by rabbinic authority.
  • A civil marriage: according to the Rambam a Torah prohibition, but according to others not, since it is not one of the seven nations.

"In all circumstances, even a one-off, you should recognize that it is not like other sins."

Seifim 3 and onward: the shifhah

The remaining seifim were covered quickly.

  • A shifhah is forbidden. The Rambam says the penalty is makkat mardut since in his view Lo yihyeh kedeshah refers to a different case, namely a woman not set aside, which makes it rabbinic. Onkelos reads that verse as speaking of the shifhah, which would make it a Torah prohibition.
  • Near the end he raised the question of a sofek ger (a person of doubtful Jewish status): must he keep the misvot? He said there is a strong argument that he must, since otherwise he could not marry anyone, but that this is a larger question, and he left it there.

Questions from the class

  • A student asked what "an idol-worshipping woman" means, whether actively engaged in idol worship or only belonging to a religion. The Rabbi said he was not sure of the answer and would look at it in the Mishneh Torah.
  • He asked the class to remember the Siman 4 mahloket (before and after conversion), since it explains the whole siman.

Practical summary

  • Three levels: a real partnership, regular relations, and a one-off, each treated differently.
  • A one-off is rabbinic according to all views (some say for a Kohen it is more serious).
  • Regular relations carry lashes by rabbinic authority.
  • Nowadays the Torah prohibition on marrying non-Jews probably does not apply in practice because the seven nations are no longer identifiable, but the Rambam holds a Torah prohibition for a real partnership.
  • Even where Beit Din does not punish, there is a serious heavenly punishment according to the Rambam.
