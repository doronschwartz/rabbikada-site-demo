Where we are

After the break for Pesah, the Rabbi opened Siman 5. He said it is not the most pleasant siman and is quite technical, but parts of it matter a great deal, for people and for animals (it touches on castration, which vets meet). The siman has two parts: a man who has been injured or castrated (who may he marry?), and the prohibition of castrating a person or an animal. Today was Seif 1, the main seif, with a start on Seif 2.

The basic definitions

The Torah (Devarim 23:2) excludes from Kehal Hashem a pesuah daka (crushed testicles) and a kerut shofkhah (a severed organ). The Rabbi explained: - Pesuah daka concerns the testicles, and kerut shofkhah concerns the organ itself. - Rashi adds that the person cannot have children. The Rambam makes the same point in several places: the whole matter is about fertility. Someone with an injury who can still have children is not a pesuah daka. - The Rabbi noted that Rav Moshe Feinstein builds on this. In a teshuvah in Even HaEzer, doctors wanted to remove part of a man's testicle in order to locate sperm for IVF. Rav Moshe permitted it, because the purpose is to make him more fertile, and he does not become a pesuah daka. The Yerushalmi has a small difference: the testicles, unlike the organ, can heal. - It is a medical question: can he have children? The ruling can also go the other way. Some people cannot have children but are not disqualified, as we will see regarding bidei shamayim (injury caused by Heaven), but to be disqualified you need a real injury of the proper kind. - The disqualification here is about men only. Women are discussed later.

The reason (Sefer HaHinukh). In ancient times kings castrated servants who guarded their wives, as we see in Megillat Esther. The Torah wanted to wipe out that practice, so whoever does this to himself cannot marry into the community. A second reason given: a woman who cannot have children with her husband will not be satisfied and may turn to another man, which increases adultery.

The Gemara: who may such a man marry?

The Mishnah is unanimous that a pesuah daka may marry a female convert (giyoret). Then the Gemara raises two further questions: may a Kohen who is a pesuah daka marry a giyoret, and may an Israelite pesuah daka marry a mamzeret?

The Gemara brings a beraita that a pesuah daka Israelite is permitted to marry a Netinah. A normal Israelite may not marry a Netinah, because of lo titkhaten bam. So once he becomes a pesuah daka he loses that status. Then Rava has two versions (lishnot) of how to read this:

  1. First version. The prohibition on marrying Netinim only applies after conversion by a rabbinic decree (derabbanan). The verse speaks of before conversion, so the Netinim are only a rabbinic prohibition. Then there is no proof that a Kohen pesuah daka may marry a giyoret.
  2. Second version. The verse lo titkhaten bam only makes sense after conversion (Gentiles do not marry in the Jewish sense), so the Netinim are forbidden by Torah law. Since a pesuah daka Israelite may nevertheless marry a Netinah, he has lost his holiness (kedushat Yisrael), so there is proof that a pesuah daka Kohen may marry a giyoret.

The Rabbi noted that the two questions are naturally linked: if you hold the second version, both the Kohen and the giyoret and the Israelite and the mamzeret should follow. He asked the class why a mamzeret is different, and noted that the Mishnah and the beraita do not mention a mamzeret at all.

  • The Raavad is consistent: he rules like the second version, so he loses kedushah and both are permitted.
  • The Baal HaMaor is consistent the other way, following the first version. The Netinim are only rabbinic, so nothing is learned from them.
  • The Rambam seems to be in the middle. He rules that Netinim are rabbinic (like the first version) but still permits the Kohen with a giyoret (like the second). The Maggid Mishneh explains: although the Rambam rules like the first version, there is also a statement of Rav Sheshet, and he is willing to take it for the Kohen. As for the mamzeret, the Rambam gives two reasons for the difference. First, a mamzeret is forbidden explicitly in the Torah while a giyoret for a Kohen is only learned out (from zonah). Second, a mamzeret is not like all other cases, since a prohibition that applies to everyone is different from one that applies only to a Kohen (lav shaveh bekol).

The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam: a pesuah daka may marry a giyoret and may not marry a mamzeret. The Rema says "there are those who permit even a mamzeret" (like the Raavad), but he does not rule that way.

The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel

The Rabbi asked: if a Kohen pesuah daka may marry a giyoret, may he also marry a gerushah, a zonah or a halalah? The Helkat Mehokek says yes: the same logic applies to all, which would mean a Kohen pesuah daka could marry a divorcee. The Beit Shemuel disagrees and is strict. The Helkat Mehokek also says that such a man loses his Kehunah. The Rabbi said we do not follow that: he stays a Kohen in everything outside marriage.

He made a practical point: in Siman 6 we will see the serious treatment of a Kohen who marries a woman forbidden to him. At the least he is not called up as a Kohen, and the Rabbi said that is what is done today. He added that Hazal did not set up a public marker here (marit ayin), and he left the reason open.

He did not go into details here, and noted that the Helkat Mehokek's view, if taken, would widen the options considerably, while the Beit Shemuel argues against it.

Doubtful cases (safek)

Why may a pesuah daka marry a safek case, such as a safek Netinah or, for the Raavad, a safek mamzeret? The Rabbi's answer: the rule that a vadai mamzer is forbidden is about lineage (yihus), but the pesuah daka disqualification is anatomical. The Rambam therefore permits even the doubtful cases, because the extra stringency of lineage (maalat yihus) applies only to a definite mamzer and not to a doubtful one.

Summary of Seif 1

  1. A pesuah daka is forbidden to marry a Jewish woman.
  2. He may marry a giyoret.
  3. He may marry a Netinah.
  4. A Kohen pesuah daka may marry a giyoret (Rambam and Raavad, against the Baal HaMaor).
  5. An Israelite pesuah daka may not marry a mamzeret (Rambam and Shulhan Arukh), though a safek mamzeret is permitted.

Seif 2: which organs count?

The Rabbi began Seif 2 with two questions to cover over the next classes: which organs make a man a pesuah daka, and whether it matters if the injury came from human hands (bidei adam) or from Heaven (bidei shamayim).

Beyond the testicles (pesuah daka) and the organ (kerut shofkhah), the Gemara gives a third: the hutei beisim (threads of the testicles). Rashi understands this as the vas deferens, the pipe inside the sac. The Rambam places it more internally, as the passages (shevilin) where the semen is prepared and from which it emerges. Rashi is more external and the Rambam more internal.

Why this matters: prostate operations. The Sis Eliezer, the Hazon Ish and others discussed the case of an older man whose enlarged prostate is operated on. After the operation he can no longer produce semen, though he can still have marital relations. Does the operation cut the hutei beisim? The Rabbi said we would need a urologist to be precise. His preliminary conclusion: according to Rashi it is fine. According to the Rambam, the structures being operated on seem to be a different, more internal one from those the Rambam described. Rav Moshe says the same. He noted the Gemara thought there were two separate channels for semen and urine, and we now know it is one. The details are for the next class.

Practical summary

  • The disqualification of a pesuah daka or kerut shofkhah depends on real infertility from an injury of the proper kind.
  • A medical procedure that aims to help fertility (such as IVF extraction) does not create the disqualification, according to Rav Moshe.
  • Such a man may marry a giyoret or a Netinah, but may not marry a Jewish woman.
  • A Kohen in this position may marry a giyoret (Rambam, Raavad); we do not follow the Helkat Mehokek that he may marry a gerushah or zonah.
  • An Israelite in this position may not marry a mamzeret according to the Shulhan Arukh, though doubtful cases (safek) are permitted.
  • For prostate procedures, the question is which structure is affected; the Rabbi left the details for the next class.
