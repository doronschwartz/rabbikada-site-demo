Topic: Self-defence, the rodef, collective punishment and civilian casualties in war, from the Gemara, the Rambam and the narratives of Tanakh Sources: Midrash Tanhuma, Parashat Sav; Mishnah and Gemara, Sanhedrin 73a; the Rambam, Hilchot Rose'ah 1 and Hilchot Genevah 9; Rashi and the Ramban on Bereshit 32 and 34; the Rambam, Hilchot Melakhim 9; the Maharal; the Radak; the Nesiv; Yehezkel 33 Shiur: The second class of the Torat Am Yisrael series, given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Tiferet Eyal, in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 14 November 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Peace first

Last week's class dealt with the hostages. Tonight's subject is one heard constantly in the media since 7 October: collective punishment, proportionality and the accusation of genocide. The Rabbi set out to ask what the Torah says about it.

He opened with a midrash on Parashat Sav. Moshe was commanded to besiege a city, yet he did not want to destroy the guilty and the innocent alike, so he first sent messengers with words of peace, and Hashem accepted this and wrote it into the Torah: "Ki tikrav el ir lehilahem aleha vekarata eleha leshalom", when you approach a city to fight it, first call out to it for peace. This is why Mishlei says that all the Torah's paths are peace.

Any killing a Jew does, even when justified, must come from the perspective of shalom. The goal at the beginning and the end of any war is peace. The Rabbi noted that Israel withdrew from Gaza in 2005 as a gesture of peace, and that gesture was abused. In his view the efforts for peace with Gaza have been exhausted, so the stage after peace, milhamah, has been reached.

Where do we find the laws of war?

The difficulty is that there is no chapter in the Shulhan Arukh on the laws of war. In medieval times the Jewish people had no sovereign state, in Spain or in Eastern Europe, so the codes never needed one. The Rabbi therefore drew on three kinds of source: - the halachot of individual self-defence - the Rambam's laws of kings and wars, limited as they are - the stories of Tanakh and their commentaries

When asked whether we may learn halacha from aggadah, he answered that the rule against it applies where halachic sources exist. Where there are none, as here, one has to learn from the narratives.

The rodef

The Mishnah in Sanhedrin lists those one may stop by killing them: someone pursuing another to kill him, or to commit rape. One may not kill someone to stop him desecrating Shabbat or serving avodah zarah; that goes through warning, witnesses and a court.

The Rambam codifies this and adds a condition. If the pursuer can be stopped by injuring him, by striking a limb with an arrow, a stone or a sword, that must be done. Only when he cannot be stopped any other way may he be killed, even though he has not yet killed. Someone who could have stopped him by wounding him and killed him instead is called a murderer, liable to death at the hands of Heaven though not by a human court. And anyone who could save a life and does not do so transgresses "lo ta'amod al dam re'akha", do not stand idly by your brother's blood.

The Rabbi applied this directly. Hamas fire missiles and have stated, in their charter and in their media, that they would repeat the massacres of 7 October if they could. A rodef does not have to be physically running after you; it is someone engaged in trying to kill you. In his view Hamas clearly have the status of rodef, and every Jew has the status of the one pursued. A participant observed that this would make a pre-emptive strike permitted even before 7 October, and the Rabbi agreed.

The condition still applies. In his example, if a member of Hamas came seeking refuge in your house and you could detain him safely and call the police, that would be your duty. Only if you feared he would discover you were Jewish and kill you first would you be permitted to kill him. For the IDF, the question in every operation is whether there is an alternative, and at what cost, including the cost in soldiers' lives.

The tunnel

The Torah permits killing a thief who breaks in through a tunnel, "haba bamahteret", even though he has come only for money. The Rambam explains that the householder will resist and the thief will presume he may have to kill him, so he is treated as a pursuer. If it is clear that he will not kill, as with a father breaking into his son's house, he may not be killed.

The lesson the Rabbi drew is that one may act when it is not certain the attacker will kill: unless it is clear he wants only money or to injure, one may defend oneself. He wondered whether that presumption still holds of burglars in England today. With Hamas there is no such doubt, he said, and "coming in a tunnel" is very literal.

Are the civilians innocent?

The harder question is the rest of the population. Are they innocent if they elected Hamas? What if they did not, or did not realise, and now fear to object because Hamas kill Palestinians who resist? What of those who handed out sweets on 7 October?

Yaakov's fear. Yaakov was "very afraid and distressed" before meeting Esav. Rashi explains he was afraid of being killed and distressed that he might kill. But Esav's four hundred men were pursuers, so why be distressed? The Maharal (Gur Aryeh) suggests some of them may have been coerced. The Mizrahi suggests Yaakov feared killing innocent bystanders in the fighting, which the Rabbi felt fits the Rambam better.

Shekhem. After Shekhem abducted and violated Dinah, Shimon and Levi killed all the men of the city. Yaakov rebuked them, and the brothers answered "shall our sister be treated like a harlot?" The Rambam (Hilchot Melakhim 9) holds that the people of Shekhem were liable: the Noahide laws require courts, and they saw what Shekhem did and did nothing. On this view Yaakov objected only to the consequences.

The Rabbi took the Rambam's logic to its conclusion for Gaza, stressing that he was not saying this should be done, and then questioned it. Did the people of Shekhem, or of Gaza, really have the ability to stop their rulers? He said he did not think the men of Gaza have the ability to stop Hamas, and if so the logic does not extend to them. He preferred the Ramban, who asks exactly this question and holds that Shimon and Levi did wrong.

Both agree that someone who could have stopped the crime and did not is complicit; they differ only on whether the people could have. He added two cautions: it is highly arguable that ordinary Gazans can resist Hamas without being killed, and such liability applies only under Jewish sovereignty, itself a separate debate.

War between nations. The Maharal answers differently: war between two nations is not like punishing an individual. When a nation acts against Israel, the war is with the nation as a whole, as with the war against Midian. The Rabbi compared a responsum of the Sis Eliezer, that in war a soldier must risk his life to save a comrade, though outside war he need not, because in war the unit is seen as one. He noted that this view of the collective may sit uneasily with Western ideas, and that international law is not the Torah's measure.

The cost of war

Treaties and reputation. Yehoshua kept his treaty with the Givonim even after discovering it was obtained by deceit. The Malbim explains he feared what the nations would say. International relations are a real consideration, the Rabbi said, though how much weight Israel must give them nobody can say.

David. David was told he could not build the Beit HaMikdash because he had shed much blood. The Radak explains that in his wars he may have killed people who were not fighting him, even righteous people. He was not punished, because his aim was to destroy the wicked, yet the Temple, which stands for peace, was not given to him. Killing civilians in war can be justified and still not be the ideal.

The Nesiv reads "mi'yad ish ahiv" in Parashat Noah to mean that bloodshed is punished in a time of brotherhood, but in war there is no such penalty: that is the reality of the world.

Yehezkel's watchman. When the watchman blows the shofar and someone hears and does not take shelter, his blood is on his own head. The Rabbi applied this to the repeated warnings to the residents of northern Gaza to move south. Someone who could leave and did not bears responsibility. The sick, the infirm and children who cannot leave are different, and greater care must be taken for them.

Rav Shaul Yisraeli, founder of Eres Hemdah, where Dayan Kada trained as a dayan, wrote that civilians may not simply be killed, but when terrorists embed themselves among civilians it is justified and proportionate to attack them.

Questions

  • Mocking terrorists. The Radak shows we do not celebrate killing. Even if the mastermind of 7 October is killed, we celebrate that Jews are protected, not the death. Mocking captured terrorists is wrong, and so is talk of revenge, including by some Israeli ministers. The war is to be seen through rodef and self-defence, not revenge.
  • Carpet bombing. Asked whether warnings would allow carpet bombing northern Gaza, the Rabbi said no. Women, children and the bedridden who cannot leave are not responsible. One must go in and fight the terrorists, even if civilians are sometimes killed, but one must choose the means that kills fewest. Mass killing of women and children is unacceptable halachically.
  • Jewish and non-Jewish life. The question of saving a non-Jewish life on Shabbat is a separate discussion; he recommended Gil Student's long article on it.

Summary

  • War is approached from shalom: peace is offered first, and its end goal is peace.
  • Hamas have the status of rodef and of "haba bamahteret"; they may be killed to prevent killing, but only where they cannot be stopped another way.
  • Collective liability depends on the ability to prevent the crime. The Rambam and the Ramban agree in principle but disagree whether ordinary people could have; the Rabbi doubted the civilians of Gaza can stop Hamas.
  • In war, civilian deaths may be justified, yet, as with David, they are never the ideal. Warnings shift responsibility only for those able to leave, and carpet bombing is not permitted.
