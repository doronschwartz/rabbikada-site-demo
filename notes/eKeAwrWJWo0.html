Knowing how to blow

The class continued the laws of Rosh Hashanah with Siman 590, "seder haraui litkiot," the correct way to blow the shofar. It has nine paragraphs. The first two explain how we arrive at thirty blasts; from the third onwards they explain how to blow. During the class the Rabbi demonstrated the sounds and timed them with the class.

"Many people think they know how to blow the shofar, but they actually don't. You're going to see there are many details people simply do not know."

From the Torah: nine blasts (590:1)

The word teruah appears three times in the Torah: twice for Rosh Hashanah, "zikhron teruah" in Emor and "yom teruah" in Pinehas, and once for the Yovel, "veha'avarta shofar teruah" on Yom Kippur of the fiftieth year. A gezerah shavah, through "the seventh month," links the Yovel to Rosh Hashanah, so we have three teruot.

Every teruah has a plain blast, a tekiah, before and after it. This is learned from the verse itself: veha'avarta, a passing, long sound, comes before the teruah, and ta'aviru comes after it. Three teruot, each with a tekiah on either side, make nine blasts. "That's the Torah. Full stop."

The doubt: what is a teruah? (590:2)

The Gemara is unsure what the Torah's teruah is. Three options:

  1. Yelalah, a wail: short, sharp, intense cries. This is what we call teruah.
  2. Genihah, a sigh: longer, groaning sounds, like a sick person crying out. This is shevarim.
  3. Both together: the sigh and then the wail, shevarim-teruah.

He described the teruah as "a very sharp cry, very intense, very sudden," and the shevarim as "a more reflective cry," and mentioned the professional mourners of the Gemara, whose job was to evoke weeping.

Since we do not know which is right, we do all three, each three times and each surrounded by tekiot:

Someone asked: why not just blow tashrat three times, which already contains both shevarim and teruah? Because the teruah must be enveloped by a tekiah before and after. If the true teruah is only shevarim, or only teruah, then in tashrat the other sound is an interruption, and the teruah no longer has a tekiah directly next to it on both sides. So we need all three sets.

How long is a teruah?

The Gemara says clearly, and everyone agrees:

  • a tekiah is the length of a teruah;
  • a teruah is three yevavot.

The question is what a yevavah is.

  • Rashi (not the halacha, but worth knowing): a yevavah is one short sound. A whole teruah is just three quick notes, so a tekiah is very short, and the shevarim must be very short too, or they would become tekiot. "It's quite hard."
  • The second opinion (590:3), which we follow: a yevavah is three short breaths, kohot. So a teruah is nine kohot, and so is a tekiah. This gives more room in the shevarim, "but the humra is that your tekiah is going to have to be long." Each view has its leniency and its stringency.

The lengths every blower must know

The Shulhan Arukh rules that the tekiot of tashrat must be longer than those of tashat, and those of tashat longer than those of tarat. Since a tekiah equals the teruah it surrounds, and in tashrat the "teruah" may be shevarim-teruah together, that tekiah must be the longest.

The Rabbi took the class to Mishnah Berurah 590:15 for the practical instructions:

  • The tekiot of tashrat must be at least 18 kohot: 9 for the shevarim and 9 for the teruah. He timed it at about five seconds.
  • The tekiot of tashat and tarat must be at least 9 kohot.
  • No single shever may reach 9 kohot, about two and a half seconds by his estimate, because then it is already the length of a tekiah.

There is no maximum. One may make every tekiah long, as long as each reaches the minimum. "You could get away with a tekiah like that in the tarat, but you can't get away with that in tashrat. If you're in the first set and you hear a tekiah like that, you should go and stop them."

He said the shevarim are always the hardest. They must be long enough not to be a teruah, but not so long that they become a tekiah. The teruah gedolah at the end is easy, since you breathe between each part. He noted that there is no halachic blast called tekiah gedolah or teruah gedolah: "It's just a nice thing we do."

This is why, traditionally, the rabbi blew. People think that if they can sound a shofar, they can blow for the congregation. "Leaving aside the kavanot, just knowing how to do it correctly."

Questions from the class

What if the blower runs out of breath in the middle of a set? Go back to the beginning of that set. If he stopped in the middle of the shevarim of the second tashrat, he starts the second tashrat again with its tekiah.

Should someone call out the sounds? There was a discussion. The Rabbi said both Ashkenazim and the Spanish and Portuguese use a caller. The argument for it is that the blower is guided; the argument against is that it is an interruption. Personally he finds the caller more distracting, and once a caller's mistaken call of "shevarim" completely threw him.

Who blew in Temple times? The Kohanim. Even in the Gemara's time not everyone knew how to blow. One of the reasons given for not blowing on Shabbat is that a person might carry the shofar to an expert to learn. In the Mikdash they blew on Shabbat, because there they knew how.

Shevarim-teruah in one breath (590:4)

When blowing tashrat, the Rabbi said the shevarim-teruah should be done in one breath, since together they may be the one teruah. He demonstrated it. And if a mistake is made in the middle, the safest course is to go back to the start of the set: "Don't let him just repeat the teruah, go back to the beginning, to the tekiah."

He ended: "When you hear the shofar, pay attention." See whether the blower knows these lengths.

Practical summary

  • The Torah requires nine blasts; because of the doubt about the teruah we blow tashrat, tashat and tarat, three times each, making thirty.
  • We follow the view that a teruah is nine short breaths, so a tekiah must be at least that long.
  • The tekiot of tashrat must be at least 18 kohot (about five seconds), and those of tashat and tarat at least 9 kohot; longer is always fine.
  • A single shever must not reach the length of a tekiah.
  • In tashrat the shevarim-teruah is blown in one breath; if the blower stops or errs in the middle of a set, he restarts that set.
  • The blower should be someone who knows these laws, not just someone who can make a sound.
