What is a honey trap?

After classes on the hostages, self-defence and civilian casualties, Dayan Kada turned to a very different question. A honey trap is an operation in which an agent, usually a woman, poses as the lover of an enemy figure, often sleeping with him, in order to extract information.

This is not theory. In 1966 an Israeli agent persuaded an Iraqi pilot to defect. In the case of Mordechai Vanunu, a Mossad agent persuaded him to leave England for Italy, where he was seized and brought back to Israel. Men have done the same kind of work: Eli Cohen lived as a spy in Damascus, where Dayan Kada's parents were living when he was hanged. And the enemy uses the method too: in 2020 Hamas tried to get IDF soldiers to download spyware with the promise of illicit photos.

Hazal knew the power of this. The Gemara says that a woman always "carries her weapon with her", which Rashi explains as her ability to stop a captor from harming her. The Sefer Hasidim notes that Shimshon, the strongest, David, the most pious, and Shlomo, the wisest, all fell through women, to show how strong the pull is: "how much more so must we be careful". People throw away careers and everything else for a few moments of false pleasure.

No practical teshuvah on honey traps is known, he said, since such operations are secret and any rav consulted would be sworn to silence. But the sources allow a fairly clear picture.

The starting point: yehareg veal yaavor

The first reaction should be: absolutely not. To save a life one may break any prohibition except three: idolatry, murder and forbidden relations. The Shulhan Arukh rules that for these one must give up one's life.

The Rema adds an important qualification: this applies only to an active act. A woman who is entirely passive, karka olam, "like the ground", is not required to give her life. A woman threatened with rape or death who does not resist is not committing the sin. This already points to a difference between men and women in such operations.

Esther

When Ahashverosh took Esther into the palace she was passive, and she did not need to give up her life. But Hazal read the verse "Mordekhai took her as a daughter (le-vat)" as "as a house (le-bayit)", meaning as a wife. Esther was a married woman.

Later Mordekhai urges her to go to the king, and she says, "If I perish, I perish." The Gemara reads the double language as: just as I am lost to my father's house, I will be lost to you. Until now she had been taken by force, and a woman who is raped may remain with her husband (unless he is a kohen). Now she was going in willingly, and a married woman who willingly has relations with another man is forbidden to her husband forever. How could she commit one of the three cardinal sins, even to save the Jewish people?

Someone asked whether an agent could remain technically passive in the act itself. In theory yes, he said, but in practice no such operation could work, and the Rema treats intimacy short of relations as part of the prohibition too. Another asked why Mordekhai did not simply give her a get. One answer given is that a get is public and would have exposed the plan.

Yael

Yael is described explicitly in the verses as the wife of Hever the Kenite. She went out to greet Sisera, gave him drink and killed him. In her song the verse repeats "between her feet he knelt, he fell, he lay" seven times, and the Gemara learns that Sisera had relations with her seven times. Nazir 23b calls it "a great sin done for the sake of Heaven", and the Gemara adds that she had no pleasure from it. Nowhere is there a hint that he forced her.

Dayan Kada asked the class to consider a difference. Esther was saving almost all the Jews, since most lived in Ahashverosh's empire. Whom was Yael saving? The battle had already been won and Sisera was in flight.

Saving the many: two Aharonim

The Meiri explains that Yael acted to save the many, hasalat rabbim, which is different.

The Shevut Yaakov (Prague, seventeenth to eighteenth century) dealt with a group of Jewish travellers seized by bandits. One woman, with her husband's consent, agreed to sleep with the bandits if they spared everyone, and they did. He ruled that she had acted correctly, since she saved many; but having gone in willingly, she was now forbidden to her husband. By the same logic Esther could not return to Mordekhai, nor Yael to Hever. The uncertainty of success does not matter: like Esther and Yael, one acts on a real chance of saving life, just as with breaking Shabbat for pikuah nefesh.

The Noda BiYehudah disagreed. Esther and Yael may have acted by a temporary instruction from Heaven, as Eliyahu offered sacrifices on Mount Carmel; Dayan Kada called that a weak argument. The stronger one is that they saved all of Israel. Even Yael, by killing a powerful general who could regroup, was saving the whole nation, as Tosafot say. A group of travellers is not comparable. He agreed the woman was forbidden to her husband, but held that, though not punishable after the fact, she should not have done it.

Does a modern operation qualify?

Dayan Kada argued that such operations are permitted even according to the Noda BiYehudah. The Rambam, in the laws of a mistaken ruling of the Sanhedrin, counts the majority of the kahal by those living in Eres Yisrael only. An operation protecting Israel's population therefore counts as saving all Israel. Asked whether preventing an attack like 7 October would qualify, he said yes: although aimed at the south, such an enemy threatens everyone. Only something like a personal vendetta against one person would not.

A Jewish target, a married agent

Rabbenu Tam held that relations with a non-Jew are not in the category of yehareg veal yaavor, and allowed a woman whose husband had died to marry the non-Jew with whom she had had an affair, after he converted. But the Rema and the Shakh reject this. So no distinction can be drawn from Esther and Yael's partners being non-Jews: the same would apply to a Jewish enemy, such as a collaborator.

For an unmarried agent, he said, this is most likely not one of the cardinal sins at all. A married agent, however, would be forbidden to her husband afterwards. It would therefore be preferable to use an unmarried woman, or for a married agent to receive a get beforehand. The prohibition on remarrying one's former wife applies only if she married someone else in between, so the couple could remarry after the operation. A woman who had relations with a non-Jew could never marry a kohen; casual relations with a Jewish man would not have that effect.

He closed with one more point from the Gemara: even in such an act one must try not to have pleasure, reminding oneself throughout that it is wrong, while doing it as a misvah.

Practical summary

  • In principle forbidden relations fall under yehareg veal yaavor; the Rema exempts a woman who is entirely passive, which is not realistic in an operation.
  • Esther and Yael, both married, acted willingly; the Meiri explains that saving the many is different.
  • The Shevut Yaakov permits it to save a group; the Noda BiYehudah only to save all Israel. Dayan Kada argues that protecting the population of Eres Yisrael meets even that standard.
  • His conclusion: such operations seem permitted, arguably even a misvah, with the right framework and intention, and even where the target is Jewish.
  • A married agent becomes forbidden to her husband, so an unmarried agent, or a get beforehand and remarriage afterwards, is preferable.
