Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 261, seifim 3-4, and 262, seifim 1-3 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 29 June 2023

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seif 3: no clock? Look at the trees

If you do not know when sunset is, light when the sun is at the top of the trees. The same measure is used for Neilah on Yom Kippur, which should start a little before sunset. If you are somewhere without a clock or phone, look at the sun on the tops of the trees or the houses: Shabbat is a few minutes away, so light then.

Seif 4: accepting Shabbat early

Once the community has said "Barkhu" at Arvit, Shabbat is accepted for you, even though it is still day.

The Rabbi explained the history. Until about 500 years ago there was no Kabbalat Shabbat service: one prayed Minhah, said "come, O bride" or something similar, and started Arvit. Then "Mizmor Shir LeYom HaShabbat" was introduced, and Rabbi Shlomo Alkabes in Safed composed Lekha Dodi. The Shulhan Arukh says Mizmor Shir is like Barkhu; it does not mention Lekha Dodi, because Rabbi Yoseph Karo and Rabbi Alkabes were contemporaries and the song had not yet spread. The Mishnah Berurah says that today, saying "Boi Kallah" in Lekha Dodi is like saying Mizmor Shir. The Rabbi marvelled that no prayer composed after the Talmud has spread like Lekha Dodi; even the Yemenites and the Spanish and Portuguese say it.

Once you have accepted Shabbat early, the leniencies of twilight we learned two weeks ago no longer apply to you. An early acceptance is stricter than twilight without acceptance.

Is the wife bound by her husband's acceptance?

A very common summer question: the husband accepts Shabbat at shul at 7.45, and his wife at home is still preparing and lights later.

The Rabbi said there are those who rule that she may continue. A wife is not bound by her husband's acceptance. The exception is a place with one community that accepts Shabbat at one time; then she must follow the community, not her husband. In a place like Hendon, where people accept Shabbat at many different times, there is no communal acceptance. It is good for the house to be ready when the husband comes home, but if it is not, it is permitted for her to finish.

Answering Barkhu without accepting Shabbat

The Rabbi told of a Friday night when his minyan was one short. A man who lived nearby did not want to pray, because he planned to accept Shabbat later and had things to do. Can one answer Barkhu with explicit intention not to accept Shabbat?

This is a dispute. The Kaf HaHayim says your words override your thoughts. Rav Ovadia Yoseph proves that if you have explicit intention not to accept Shabbat, you have not accepted it, and you may still pray Minhah and do Melakhah. The Rabbi said this seems to be the halacha. The man came, and they had ten.

So if you arrive late on Friday night and the minyan is at Barkhu, answer Barkhu with the intention not to accept Shabbat, then pray Minhah. (Best not to get into that situation.) The same applies at the Kotel, where you may hear another minyan's Barkhu on the way to your own.

What about lighting candles with intention not to accept Shabbat, for example before driving to a meal? Sefaradim do not hold that lighting accepts Shabbat at all. Even for those who do, the Shulhan Arukh says that a condition works there, since that acceptance is itself not clear. Barkhu is stronger, as it begins the evening prayer. One caution: if you light very early in summer, make sure the candles will still be burning when you eat.

Siman 262: a set table and a tidy house

Seif 1: one should set the table, make the beds and tidy the house so that everything is ready when you come home from shul. This is a law of the Gemara, codified by the Rambam, not just a nice idea. Do not plan to clean and lay the table after shul, especially in winter.

The Rema adds that the table should be covered with a cloth all Shabbat, even outside mealtimes. The Mishnah Berurah mentions a custom of two tablecloths, so that one remains when the other is shaken out. The Rabbi said this is nice, but "it's not the end of the world" if the table is bare for a moment.

The Mishnah Berurah brings the Gemara: two angels accompany a person home from shul on Friday night. If they find the candle lit, the table set and the beds made, the good angel says "May it be so next week too" and the bad angel must answer Amen.

Shalom Aleikhem

A question came up about "Seitkhem LeShalom", asking the angels to leave. Someone in the class had been told to stop saying it. The Rabbi brought the Hida: a great person erased this line, but the Hida says this is incorrect; it means "when you leave, leave in peace", and one should not remove a custom of the Jewish people. "Don't tamper with things just because you don't understand them."

He said the bigger concern is "Barkhuni LeShalom", "bless me". Asking angels themselves to bless us would contradict the Rambam's fifth principle, not to serve intermediaries. It must be understood as asking them to intercede, not as the angels blessing. He mentioned that Rav Moshe Feinstein stopped saying it because people misunderstood it.

Seifim 2-3: Shabbat clothes and joy

Seif 2: special clothes. The pasuk does not mean you must wear clothes never worn in the week. It means your Shabbat clothes should be a different level from your weekday clothes. A suit you wear in the week for special meetings or weddings may be worn on Shabbat, as long as it is your best. The Mishnah Berurah brings the Arizal's custom not to wear on Shabbat anything worn in the week; the Rabbi said it is a nice custom but not halacha, and hardly anyone keeps it fully.

The point is a visible distinction. If you only have weekday clothes, for example your luggage was lost, do something to mark Shabbat: buy a tie for Shabbat, something different. In their day, one would let one's cloak hang lower.

Seif 3: rejoice at Shabbat's arrival. There is no misvah of simhah on Shabbat as there is on Yom Tov, but there is a misvah to rejoice as Shabbat comes in, like going out to greet a king or a bride. Rabbi Hanina would wrap himself and say "come, let us go out to greet Shabbat the Queen", and Rabbi Yannai said "Boi Kallah, Boi Kallah". That is the source of Lekha Dodi.

We turn around at "Boi Beshalom" not because of the west but to show we are going out to greet. If you were praying facing north for some reason, you would still turn round. Some communities used to step out of the shul. Some, such as the Spanish and Portuguese, sit through Kabbalat Shabbat and stand only for "Boi Beshalom", the moment of acceptance, which makes the greeting more apparent.

Practical summary

  • If you have no clock, light when the sun is on the treetops.
  • After Barkhu (or "Boi Kallah") you have accepted Shabbat, even in daylight.
  • A wife is not bound by her husband's early acceptance in a place without a single communal time.
  • If you must answer Barkhu before praying Minhah, have explicit intention not to accept Shabbat.
  • Have the table set, beds made and house tidy before Shabbat.
  • Wear clothes that clearly mark Shabbat as different, and greet Shabbat with joy.
