Why we read Ruth on Shavuot

This was the fourth class of the month, after the misvah of living in Israel, halacha and minhag, and the count of 613 misvot, with Shavuot to follow. The Rabbi used the story of Ruth as a springboard for a modern question: can a conversion be nullified, and on what grounds?

Several reasons are given for reading Ruth on Shavuot. The story takes place at the wheat harvest, the season of Shavuot. Ruth was the ancestor of David HaMelekh, who died on Shavuot. Most likely, though, it is because on Shavuot we became a nation of Torah, in a sense converting as a people, and Ruth later did the same as an individual. She is the quintessential convert. The numerical value of her name, רות, is 606; add the seven Noahide laws a non-Jew already keeps and you reach 613. Many of the laws of conversion are learned from her story.

When did Ruth convert?

All the sources agree Ruth converted. The question is when.

Only later, on the road with Naomi. After Naomi's husband and sons die, she sets out for Yehudah and tries to send Ruth and Orpah home. Ruth answers, "Wherever you go I will go... your people is my people and your God my God" (1:16), and when Naomi sees how determined she is, she stops trying (1:18). The Gemara in Yevamot (47b) learns from this how to treat a candidate: we do not press too hard. It also records a dialogue in which Naomi warns Ruth about the Shabbat boundary, yihud and idolatry, and Ruth answers each one. If this is how we learn to receive a convert, Ruth must not yet have been Jewish. Rashi says the same. Naomi tells the girls that even if she had another son, they would be too old by the time he grew up. That implies they could otherwise marry him. Yet the widow of a brother who died before the second was born is forbidden to him, so there cannot have been a valid Jewish marriage. Ruth and Orpah were not Jewish.

From the start, before marrying Mahlon and Kilyon. The Gemara in Bava Batra says in the name of Rabbi Shimon bar Yohai that Elimelekh, Mahlon and Kilyon were the great men of their generation, punished only for leaving the Land of Israel for Moav. Could the Gemara call them great if they had married non-Jewish women, and blame their deaths on leaving Israel rather than on that? More seriously, chapter 4 falls apart. Boaz redeems Ruth to perpetuate the name of the dead, a kind of yibum, and the verse calls her "the wife of the dead man." Neither is possible without a halachic marriage.

So each answer leaves a problem. The Rabbi parked the question and turned to the halacha.

Informing versus accepting

Beyond milah and mikveh, the spiritual preparation raises an apparent contradiction. The Gemara, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say the Beit Din informs the candidate of some lighter and some weightier misvot, and if it did not, the conversion is still valid. Yet they also say that without kabbalat misvot, acceptance of the misvot, there is no conversion.

The answer is the distinction between notification and acceptance. The Beit Din does not have to inform the candidate of every misvah, and if it informed him of very little, the conversion stands bediavad. Acceptance is different: the candidate must sincerely accept every misvah of the Torah and of the Hakhamim, even ones he does not yet know. The proof is Sinai itself, where the people said naaseh venishma before knowing what the misvot would be.

The Ahiezer (Rav Hayim Ozer Grodzinski) was asked about a woman converting for marriage who said she could not cover her hair. As someone in the class suggested, the key is her motive. If she accepts the misvah and intends to keep it one day but is not yet able, she has accepted it. "Every one of us has shortcomings." But a woman who says she will not cover her hair because it is sexist or outdated has rejected a misvah, and according to all opinions that is not a valid conversion. This is one of two reasons a Reform conversion is not a conversion halachically: an ideological lack of acceptance, and three officiants who are very often not fit to serve.

The Rambam: converts whose motives are doubtful

In Hilchot Issurei Biah (Chapter 13) the Rambam writes that we must not think Shimshon or Shlomo married non-Jewish women. When a candidate comes, the Beit Din checks for ulterior motives: wealth, status, fear, or a Jewish partner. If there are none, the candidate is told how heavy the yoke is, and if he still accepts, he is converted. In the days of David and Shlomo the Beit Din did not accept converts, who might be coming out of fear or for the nation's prominence. Some converted before three ordinary people, and the Beit Din HaGadol neither rejected them nor drew them close until it saw how they turned out.

The rule that follows: a person converted with a doubtful motive is no longer a non-Jew, but is under suspicion until his conduct proves him. Conversely, a person who was checked and was sincere at the time is a full Jew forever. If twenty years later he leaves everything, he is a Jew who stopped practising, nothing more.

Ruth and Orpah resolved

Now the story falls into place. Ruth and Orpah converted when they married, but for the sake of their husbands, so their sincerity was in doubt. When the husbands died, Naomi put them to the test: the reason you converted is gone, so go home. Orpah went back, proving retroactively that hers was never a sincere conversion. Ruth withstood the test, proving that she had been sincere and Jewish from the start. That is why the Gemara can learn how to treat converts from Naomi's words, and why Boaz could redeem her as the wife of a Jewish husband.

Someone asked about the status of Orpah's husband and children. The husband did not know, the Rabbi answered, and the children's status is exactly what the modern case raises. Another suggested Orpah was simply sparing Naomi the burden of supporting her; the Rabbi heard the point but said some such resolution is needed to reconcile the sources.

A modern case

He then described a case that reached the Beit Din HaGadol, the highest rabbinical court in Israel, where marriage, divorce and conversion are governed by the Chief Rabbinate by law. A Danish woman arrived in Israel with a Jewish partner and converted, after a short process, in a certified rabbinate Beit Din. They married and had three children. Years later they divorced in Ashdod. The Ashdod Beit Din then learned that she was only traditional in practice, declared the conversion invalid and the get unnecessary, and placed the children on the list of those who may not marry.

On appeal, as the Rabbi told it, two of the three dayanim overturned the Ashdod ruling:

  • Kabbalat misvot is essential, but it is judged at the moment of immersion.
  • That judgement belongs to the Beit Din that converted her, not to a later one. The Beit Yoseph writes that the whole matter of conversion depends on what the Beit Din sees before its eyes.
  • Unspoken thoughts do not count (citing Rav Kook and Rav Moshe Feinstein), and human weakness must be allowed for, as the Ahiezer said.

The third dayan went back to the file. The process had taken under six months, four months later she was using electricity on Shabbat and admitted it was too hard, she was pregnant at the time, and her husband was secular. He held that one Beit Din may review another and upheld the annulment. He then tried to force a fresh hearing by declaring that he could not decide. The others answered that a dayan cannot do that after writing his ruling, the Chief Rabbi agreed, and the majority decision stood.

Even the majority wrote that clear evidence of no intention to keep the misvot at the time would void a conversion. Examples were given: a convert who already had a flight booked to leave Israel on the following Shabbat, and a man who went home from the mikveh to live with a non-Jewish woman.

Questions after the class

Asked about converts who change after their conversion, the Rabbi said a convert is not on probation forever: once established, he or she is simply a Jew. Naomi, he added, was not overturning a conversion, only judging for herself whether Ruth was sincere.

Summary

  • Ruth and Orpah converted when they married, but their sincerity was proved only afterwards; Ruth's held, Orpah's did not.
  • A Beit Din need not inform a convert of every misvah, but the convert must accept them all.
  • A sincere convert remains Jewish even if he later stops keeping the misvot.
  • A conversion can be annulled only on strong evidence that at the time of immersion there was no real acceptance.
