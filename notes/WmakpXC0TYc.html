Topic: The structure of Shaharit from Barekhu, the three blessings of the Shema, and the meaning of the Shema itself Text: The Shabbat morning service in the Siddur Shiur: The second class of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on prayer at Holland Park, Wednesday 19 June 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where the real prayer begins

The first class, just before Shavuot, dealt with why we pray and where prayer came from. Now the series turned to the text of the Shabbat morning service, so that people can understand what is happening in shul.

The service opens with the morning blessings, three pages thanking Hashem for sight, for the ability to walk, for clothing and more. These, Dayan Kada stressed, must be said every day, whenever one arrives. Then comes a long section of songs and praises, Pesukei DeZimrah. These are like footballers stretching before the match: a warm-up. They contain only praise and thanks, with no requests, and need no minyan. If one comes late, this is the part to skip. "If we needed a minyan for this, we'd be waiting every Shabbat."

Shaharit proper begins with Barekhu. The hazan calls "Bless Hashem", and everyone answers: the referee's whistle that starts the game. Barekhu alone requires a minyan, since it is a public call to pray together. Why is it repeated near the end of the service? For latecomers who missed it. "Even in the Rabbis' times, two thousand years ago, they had latecomers."

The core of prayer, as Hazal instituted it, is short: the morning blessings, the Shema with its blessings, and the Amidah. Everything else was added later. That is why this part deserves the most time and focus, and why it is the part to be in shul for. Women too should say the Shema and its blessings, in shul or at home.

Three blessings, one story

Around the Shema stand three blessings: two before and one after. Their themes are creation, revelation and redemption, the same order as the Torah itself: Bereshit, then Sinai, then the redemption we await. The Shema sits between revelation and redemption.

The first blessing: creation

The blessing opens "Blessed are You... who forms light and creates darkness, makes peace and creates everything." Two points stand out.

The present tense. Hashem did not create the world long ago and leave it to run by itself. He creates it at every moment. Dayan Kada gave his favourite analogy: close your eyes and dream up your ideal world, palm trees, beaches, perfect weather, no traffic, kind people. Then the phone rings with bad news, and the whole world vanishes. So too, the universe exists only because Hashem wills it at every moment.

Light and darkness from one source. When these blessings were composed, dualism, the belief in a god of light and a god of darkness in constant struggle, was widespread; it is associated with Mani of Persia. The blessing declares that the same God creates both. This is more than a statement about day and night. People who see the world in black and white want a source for the good and a separate source for the bad. Judaism asks us to live with the harder truth that everything comes from Hashem. "If we all lived with this in our minds, we would be the happiest people in the world."

The rest of the blessing develops the theme:

  • A poem built on the word hakol, "all": all will praise You, because Hashem created everything.
  • An alphabetical poem, four words a line, from aleph to tav. Someone added that the orderly poem reflects an orderly world, which he liked.
  • On Shabbat, a paragraph about the seventh day, the culmination of creation.
  • Spiritual creation: the angels, whose words "Kadosh, kadosh, kadosh" we repeat.
  • The close: "in His goodness He renews each day, constantly, the work of creation".

This is why the ideal time for Shaharit is sunrise, when you can see creation happening and the heart opens with thanks. In an English June, he noted, sunrise is around four in the morning.

The second blessing: revelation

Revelation means Hashem communicating with humanity, first and publicly at Sinai. Asked whether revelation is mainly rules, he said it is more a covenant, a relationship: "You are partners with Me in this world."

The blessing asks Hashem, who revealed Himself to our fathers, to reveal Himself to us, not through prophecy but through understanding: "Put in our hearts the capacity to understand, to learn, to listen, to teach, to observe and to fulfil with love all the words of Your Torah." It ends "who chooses His people Israel with love", which is why it is sung with such a beloved tune. Someone noted that Israel was chosen at the revelation; he agreed that at Sinai the family became a nation, and added that our first covenant, with Avraham, was over the Land.

A side question about Sinai: the people heard the first two of the Ten Commandments directly, asked Moshe to hear the rest for them, and Moshe then received the written Torah over forty days. The Shema is part of that written Torah.

The Shema

The word shema means far more than "hear". As in English, "do you hear me" and "do you listen to me" are different. Shema means listen, understand, let it penetrate, integrate it.

The verse uses two names of God. The four-letter name signifies Hashem's mercy, when we can see His goodness clearly. Elokim signifies judgment, the hard parts of life. "Hashem Elokeinu" says that the good and the bad come from one source. And judgment is itself a form of love: a parent who never judges a child does not love the child. "Hashem Ehad" repeats it: this is a far more refined monotheism than "one God, not two". Everything that happens comes from the One.

That is why we say it morning and night, at a brit, on a deathbed, and why it is in the mezuzah: leaving home each morning and returning at night, we remind ourselves that everything that happens is from Hashem.

Emunah and bitahon. Emunah is faith; bitahon, from betah, secure, is trust. Believing in Hashem is one thing; truly trusting Him is a higher level and the work of a lifetime, with ups and downs, like the moon waxing and waning.

Covering the eyes. The simple reason is concentration. The deeper reason: we say that the good and the bad are one, but we cannot yet see it. We close our eyes and believe what we cannot see. That is also why "Barukh shem kevod malkhuto" is said quietly, since His glory is not yet revealed to all, except on Yom Kippur, when we are on a level to feel it and say it aloud.

The three paragraphs: accepting Hashem's sovereignty, accepting the misvot, and sisit as the reminder of the misvot. Why only men wear sisit drew a lively exchange, which he promised to take up another time.

The third blessing: redemption

The final blessing begins with fifteen words all affirming that what we have said is true, echoing the fifteen steps of the Temple and the fifteen Songs of Ascents. It then recalls past redemptions, Egypt and the Red Sea, to usher in the future one, and ends "who redeemed Israel", leading straight into the Amidah.

He reminded the class of the phonetic siddurim recently donated to the shul, for those still learning to read the Hebrew. On women and the Shema at night: men must say it morning and evening; for women it is good to say, and even the first verse is better than nothing.

Summary

  • Until Barekhu the service is preparation (except the morning blessings, said every day); the core is Barekhu, the Shema and its blessings, and the Amidah.
  • The three blessings follow the Torah's own story: creation, revelation and redemption.
  • Creation is constant, and light and darkness come from the same God.
  • The Shema teaches that mercy and judgment, the good and the hard, all come from the One, a truth we cover our eyes to affirm until it is revealed.
