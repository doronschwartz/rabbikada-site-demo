Chapter 8: the plan of the book

Chapter 8 is the last and shortest chapter of the Introduction. Before starting the book itself, Rav Saadiah tells the reader what it contains and how it is built. He lists ten essays:

  1. The world has a beginning: creation from nothing.
  2. The unity of Hashem.
  3. Commandments and prohibitions: revelation at Sinai and the reasons for the misvot.
  4. Obedience and disobedience: free will, and our duty to serve Hashem.
  5. Merits and demerits: what counts, in halachic and moral terms, as a good or bad deed.
  6. The soul, and what happens at death.
  7. The resurrection of the dead.
  8. Redemption.
  9. Reward and punishment.
  10. How a person should live a good life.

The Rabbi noted that the real book is the first nine essays. The tenth is a kind of ethical appendix.

One structure for every essay

Rav Saadiah then gives the order he will follow in each essay, and he keeps to it all the way through the book:

  • First he states the belief, what the argument is.
  • Then he brings the proofs from the Tanakh, what Hashem told us through the Prophets.
  • Then he supports it with reason and philosophy.
  • Then he discusses those who disagree, and explains why their arguments fail.
  • Finally he closes with one more proof from the Prophets, and asks Hashem for guidance for himself and for everyone who studies the book.

The Rabbi explained the reason for the last step. Rav Saadiah told us in chapter 7 that people go wrong because they hear wrong ideas. So he wants to end each essay with the right proof still ringing in your ears.

The Rabbi gave Essay 1 as an example. It will state that the world was created from nothing. It will not be as simple as it sounds, because others held that Hashem and matter were both eternal, or that the world itself is eternal. Rav Saadiah will bring four proofs of his own, then go through twelve opposing views and answer them one by one, and then conclude. This fits the man: Rav Saadiah is very structured, loves numbers and loves order, and he numbers his proofs, his opponents and their arguments.

The order also reflects the principle of the Introduction. The Prophets give us the foundation, and we then have a duty to verify it with our own intellect. That is why each essay begins with the Tanakh and only then turns to philosophy.

Review of the whole Introduction

Chapter 1 is a thanksgiving to Hashem for being able to write the book. Rav Saadiah lived in tenth-century Iraq, where many beliefs competed, Islam was still fairly new, and people were confused.

Chapter 2 explains why people make mistakes. Some do not know what they are looking for, and with the wrong destination, no effort will get you there. Others know the goal but lack the courage and energy to go the whole way. Often it is both. There are four kinds of people: those who found the truth and are confident; those who found it but doubt it; those who hold false beliefs and think they are right; and those who constantly flip-flop. The last group is why he wrote the book. He also humbly asks readers to tell him if they find a mistake.

Chapter 3 answers "why does Hashem not simply give us the right beliefs?" Everything created takes time and effort. Asking to be handed wisdom is like asking to be like Hashem. Wise people are patient and see the process through.

Chapter 4 defines emunah: something you have thought through, believe to be correct, and then store within you. Wise people shape their opinions around the evidence. Foolish people start with an opinion and bend reality to fit. Beliefs can be true or false; the problem is starting with the false one.

Chapter 5 is the foundation we will return to often. All knowledge comes from sense perception, from the self-evident truths of reason, or from inference, which is where most of our knowledge comes from: from smoke we infer fire, and from observation of the moon whole sciences such as geometry and astronomy are built. Alongside these he places a fourth source, tradition, but always a verified tradition. Not every tradition is reliable, and he assumes that our Torah is a verified one. He also warned that not every inference is sound: he listed seven mistakes people make when inferring, and an inference must be one that cannot be rebutted.

Chapter 6 takes apart the view that we should not study belief and should just have simple faith. Rav Saadiah likens that view to the folk belief that a dragon swallows the moon at an eclipse. Using the intellect is a religious duty. The Sages' warning about philosophy applied to philosophy on its own. With the Tanakh as the foundation, philosophy is a means of verifying it, and then it is an obligation. If everything can be verified by reason, why were miracles needed? Because most people cannot be relied on to reach the truth by thought, and they needed signs. For us it is not enough: we should understand it with our minds, and not build our beliefs on miracles alone.

Chapter 7 gives the eight reasons people deny Torah: it is inconvenient to accept the truth; people do not think; people are too busy; thinking is too tiring; arrogance; influence of surroundings; a shallow presentation of Torah; and the bad behaviour of religious people. Most of these are as relevant today as they were then.

Chapter 8, which we have just seen, is the plan of the ten essays and the structure of each one.

Looking ahead

The Rabbi asked if there were any questions on the summary, and there were none. He reminded the class that the Introduction is a foundation, not just an introduction. Throughout the book Rav Saadiah will refer back to it, particularly to chapter 5 and the sources of knowledge. The second part of the evening began Treatise 1.

Summary

  • Chapter 8 lists the ten essays; the first nine are the book proper, and the tenth is an ethical appendix.
  • Every essay follows the same pattern: the belief, proof from the Tanakh, proof from reason, the opposing views and their refutation, and a closing proof.
  • The Tanakh gives the foundation and philosophy verifies it, which is why the Tanakh always comes first.
  • The Introduction as a whole: why people err, why the process takes patience, what emunah is, how we know things, why thinking about faith is a duty, and why people reject it.
