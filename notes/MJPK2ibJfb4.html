Topic: Who the Mashiah will be and what the messianic age will be like, in the Rambam's eschatology Sources: The Rambam, Hilchot Melakhim 11 and 12; Mishnah Makkot 3:16 with the Rambam's commentary; Avodah Zarah 17a Shiur: The fifth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on the soul at Jackie Benjamin House, Monday 3 March 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Putting it all together

The class opened with a song for the month of Adar and thanks to the host. At the group's request, Dayan Kada first gave a three-minute summary of the series so far, all according to the Rambam:

  • A person lives in this world, and on death immediately begins Olam HaBa, which is for the soul alone and is in proportion to the perfection of a person's intellect and character. "Reward" is the wrong word: it is the consequence of who we have become.
  • The worst consequence is karet, ceasing to exist, for those too corrupt to connect with God. It applies to very few: heretics and those who commit one of the sins the Torah punishes with karet without repenting.
  • At some future time there will be Tehiyat HaMetim, a limited period in which souls rejoin bodies, after which souls continue in Olam HaBa for eternity.
  • There is no reincarnation in the Rambam's thought. "You've got 120 years in this life."

One misvah done properly

Asked whether karet can be undone, he said every sin can be repented, even on one's deathbed, though one cannot plan to sin and repent at the end. A whole life of debauchery and teshuvah on the last day gives a portion, but not like a life of Torah and misvot. He told the story of Elazar ben Durdaya (Avodah Zarah 17a), who travelled the world in pursuit of promiscuity until a woman taunted him that his sins could never be forgiven; he wept until his soul left him, and was granted a share in the World to Come.

Someone said she would rather do one thing with true intention than a thousand on autopilot. He agreed, citing the Mishnah "Hashem wished to give Israel merit, lezakot, so He gave them much Torah and many misvot". The Rambam explains that whoever performs even one misvah properly, purely for its own sake, earns a share in the World to Come, and over a lifetime of misvot, surely one is done properly. Leshem shamayim means with no ulterior motive, not even reward in the next world.

Who will Mashiah be?

The Rambam (Hilchot Melakhim 11:1) writes that the King Mashiah will restore the kingdom of David, build the Temple and gather in the exiles, and all the laws of the Torah, sacrifices and shemitah, will return. One who does not believe in him or does not await his coming denies the Torah.

Then (11:3): do not think that he must perform miracles or revive the dead. Rabbi Akiva, the greatest sage of his generation, carried the weapons of Bar Kokhba and believed he was the Mashiah, as did the sages of his time, until he was killed. No one asked him for a miracle.

So who is he? The criteria in 11:4:

Stage

What is required

Presumed Mashiah

A king from the house of David, who studies Torah and keeps the misvot, and fights the wars of Hashem

Certain Mashiah

He succeeds, builds the Temple and gathers in the exiles

If such a person dies before the messianic age is established, he was not the Mashiah. Asked whether that means he is immortal, Dayan Kada said no: once the messianic age is established, he is a human being and will die one day. In every generation, he explained, there is a potential Mashiah, if the generation is worthy. He recalled a former Chief Rabbi of Israel, under whom he had learned, who loved the Baba Sali and said that in his lifetime he may well have been the Mashiah, but the generation did not merit it. Asked how that fits with fighting wars, he said some interpret "the wars of Hashem" as spiritual battles, though he does not think that is what the Rambam means.

A process, not a trumpet blast

The idea many of us grew up with, that one day the Mashiah will blow a shofar and everyone will gather overnight, is not the Rambam's view. "Mashiah is a process, a long process," and it may take hundreds of years. The State of Israel is already part of it; Rav Kook spoke of the beginning of the redemption, and Dayan Kada said he believes the process has begun. Which other nation has been in exile for two thousand years, kept its identity and returned to its land?

This, he explained, is central to religious Zionist thought, which is why aliyah matters so much there. Asked whether any current leader could fit, he said the criteria have not been met: Israel is not yet truly sovereign in the Rambam's sense, in full control of its borders and security.

Rabbi Akiva in Bnei Brak

With Pesah five weeks away, he digressed. Why did the five sages of the Haggadah gather in Bnei Brak? Because Rabbi Akiva lived there, and they came to him. It was, he explained, the bleak time just after the fall of Bar Kokhba, when the whole movement seemed finished, and on Pesah, when the loss of the Temple and of sovereignty hurt most. Rabbi Akiva was the one who could always find the good, as in the story of the foxes on the Temple Mount and his saying "everything Hashem does is for the good".

What will the messianic age be like?

The Rambam (Hilchot Melakhim 12:1): "Do not think that in the days of the Mashiah anything of the natural order will change." The world continues as usual. Yeshayahu's famous "the wolf shall dwell with the lamb" is a metaphor: Israel will live securely among the nations that once sought to destroy it.

The only difference between now and then, he said, is that Israel will be sovereign and secure, and no one will seek to destroy it. Some war will come first; the Rambam says we cannot know the order of events from the Midrashim, and one should not waste time calculating them.

After that there will be no more war, jealousy or competition, because good things will be plentiful. When the Gemara says clothes will grow on trees, the Rambam reads it as a metaphor: everything will be readily available.

And life goes on. If the Mashiah came in our lifetimes, we would keep our bodies, faces and relationships, and we would still have free will, but on a higher level, with a new heart: not a choice between good and terrible sins, but how much good to do and how well. We would still die, and our place in Olam HaBa would still follow our choices.

Why long for Mashiah, then?

Someone pointed out that this seems a downgrade: Mashiah is only a stage in this world, not the ultimate. Dayan Kada agreed that in the grand scheme it is minor, and has no direct connection to Olam HaBa; it is a prelude to the resurrection. But it is the best we can hope for while alive. To long for Olam HaBa instead would be to long for death, and Judaism does not do that. A world of Jewish sovereignty, peace and plenty is far better than the world we live in now.

Summary

  • For the Rambam, the Mashiah is a human king from the house of David, learned and observant, who wins the wars of Hashem, rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles; he need not perform miracles.
  • If a candidate dies before succeeding, he was not the Mashiah; every generation has a potential one.
  • The messianic age changes nothing in nature: its essence is Jewish sovereignty and peace, and the prophetic images are metaphors.
  • It is a gradual process, which Dayan Kada believes has begun, and it remains a world of free choice.
  • In the larger picture Mashiah is minor compared with Olam HaBa, but it is the best this world can offer.
