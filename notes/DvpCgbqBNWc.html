Tonight's hakham

The previous week was Rav Uziel, and there is a small connection: the two corresponded. As before, the class had three parts: his life, his works, and some interesting passages from his book. The series would continue with Rav Yoseph Messas and end with Rav Haim David HaLevi.

A family of rabbis and kabbalists

Rav Shem Tov Gaguine was born in Jerusalem in 1884. His family had been expelled from Spain and went to Morocco in 1492. He came from a long line of rabbis, which explains much about his works and his outlook:

  • His great-grandfather, Rav Hayim Avraham Gaguine, was the first Hakham Bashi when the Ottoman Empire created the title in the mid-1800s. He hosted Sir Moses Montefiore for several weeks on one of his visits to the Land of Israel.
  • That great-grandfather was a grandson of Rav Shalom Sharabi, the Rashash, perhaps the greatest kabbalist after the Arizal and Rav Hayim Vital. Many kabbalistic kavanot used today come from him.
  • His grandfather and his father were both dayanim in Jerusalem.

So he grew up with Kabbalah, yet he would spend his life serving the Spanish and Portuguese, who historically have little affinity for it. That tension runs through his work.

Education

He went to Doresh Sion, a school that also taught secular studies, which was controversial in Jerusalem in the late 1800s. There he learned many languages, "his passport for the rest of his life", allowing him to travel and learn the customs of many communities. He studied at the Tiferet Yerushalayim yeshiva, and also learned at Beit El, the centre of Kabbalah in Jerusalem, where the Rashash had been Rosh Yeshiva. In 1906, aged 22, he received semikhah from Rav Kook, before Rav Kook became Chief Rabbi, and also from Rav Yaakov Meir, later the Sepharadi Chief Rabbi. Rav Kook would later write the main haskamah to his great book.

Cairo, Manchester, London and Ramsgate

  • Cairo (1910-1919). When the Ottomans introduced compulsory military service in 1910, he took a position in Cairo. In 1911 he became a dayan on the Sepharadi Beit Din there, and in 1912 also on the Ashkenazi Beit Din, thanks to his knowledge of Ashkenazi halacha and minhagim. His wife came from the Ashkenazi community of Cairo.
  • Manchester (1919-1927). He was called to the Syrian community in Manchester, founded by immigrants from Aleppo. A year later, in 1920, he was appointed Hakham of the Spanish and Portuguese community in London, while still living in Manchester. In 1921 he was also appointed to the Ashkenazi Beit Din of Manchester. As Hakham he answered the halachic questions of the London community, which was served day to day by ministers.
  • Ramsgate (1927-1949). He became Rosh Yeshiva of the Judith Montefiore College in Ramsgate, which Sir Moses founded in memory of his wife, wishing that Torah should always be studied there. A century after his great-grandfather hosted Montefiore, he headed Montefiore's yeshiva. Free of congregational duties, he had time to write.
  • The war. Ramsgate, on the south coast, was the front line, and in 1939 he was evacuated to Tunbridge Wells in Kent. In Keter Shem Tov he laments being cut off from his library and the letters he had received.

He retired in 1949, returned to Manchester, and is buried there. The Rabbi noted that the Montefiore endowment still supports Torah study today, and that some elderly members of the London community grew up in Ramsgate and remember Hakham Gaguine.

His works

His main work is Keter Shem Tov, seven volumes. The name plays on his own and on Pirkei Avot: there are three crowns, but the crown of a good name rises above them. Volumes 1 and 2 appeared in 1932, volume 3 in 1948, and volumes 4 to 7 were published after his death by his son, a rabbi in Manchester. All seven were printed together in 1998.

He also wrote notes on the Shulhan Arukh, still in manuscript, correcting its text from early manuscripts and the first Venice printing. He was among the first to compare manuscripts in this way. He corresponded with Rav Uziel about a manuscript in Rav Uziel's library.

What makes Keter Shem Tov unique

No other community has all its customs recorded, with their sources, as the Spanish and Portuguese do in Keter Shem Tov. For each custom he records the practice in London (including the differences between Bevis Marks and Lauderdale Road), Manchester and Amsterdam, and also in the Land of Israel, Syria and Egypt, France, Spain, Portugal and beyond. He travelled to Morocco, France, Gibraltar, Vienna and elsewhere to see communities' customs for himself.

The book carries haskamot from Rav Yaakov Meir, the Rishon LeSion, a long one from Rav Kook, and Rav Raphael Ankawa, Chief Rabbi of Morocco. The 1998 edition, however, carries a publisher's note saying it was reprinted only for its record of customs. Given the haskamot and the scholarship, the Rabbi felt this was a shame, and hoped a new, better edition would appear.

Kabbalah and the Spanish and Portuguese

The Rabbi gave examples of the tension between Kabbalah and the customs he served.

Kaparot. In the manuscript of the Shulhan Arukh, the heading of the siman on kaparot calls it a foolish custom. Later printers, publishing it with the Rema, who encourages the custom, softened the heading. The custom has no Talmudic source; a form of it appears in the time of the Geonim, and the Arizal made it important, so most Eastern Sepharadim do it, with a chicken or with money. The Rashba objected to it as darkei haEmori, and the Spanish and Portuguese do not practise it at all. Hakham Gaguine records a story from his Manchester years: a man who had lost two sons asked for kaparot to protect his third, then took the chicken to bury it by his sons' graves. The caretaker saw blood and called the police, and the community feared a blood libel until the chicken was found.

Modeh Ani. The very first entry in his book notes that Modeh Ani is absent from the Amsterdam and De Sola prayer books. It is a late text. The Spanish and Portuguese day begins with the morning blessings, and the festival mahzorim still do not include it.

Selihot at night. In London and Amsterdam the custom was to say an abridged Selihot after Arvit, the night before, perhaps because it was too cold or people were busy in the early morning. The kabbalists strongly oppose Selihot before midnight, as night is a time of din. Rav Moshe Feinstein permitted it, which is why many Ashkenazi communities begin Selihot late on Mosei Shabbat. Hakham Gaguine, though he knew the kabbalistic objection, wrote that there was no reason to stop it: let each community keep its own custom. The Rabbi added that after a leading Sepharadi hakham visited London some decades ago, the community stopped the practice.

A lesson for today

He was respected by Sepharadim and Ashkenazim alike, and his scholarship is extraordinary; he quotes books the Rabbi had never heard of. It is a shame he is not better known. As a child the Rabbi thought Taamei HaMinhagim was the source of every custom, but it covers Ashkenazi minhagim only. Nothing else does for the Sepharadi communities what Keter Shem Tov does.

Above all, he defends minhagim tirelessly. He rarely says a custom is wrong; he will go to great lengths to find the value in customs that seem strange. That is a lesson for us. Too often we are losing the customs of our grandparents' countries, and all Sepharadim are being blended into one. We should make the effort to understand the minhag of our parents, grandparents and great-grandparents.

Summary

  • Rav Shem Tov Gaguine (1884-1953), from a Jerusalem family of rabbis and kabbalists, served as dayan in Cairo, rabbi in Manchester, Hakham of the Spanish and Portuguese, and Rosh Yeshiva in Ramsgate.
  • His Keter Shem Tov records the customs of the Spanish and Portuguese and many other communities, with their sources.
  • He lived with a tension between his kabbalistic upbringing and a community that does not follow Kabbalah, seen in kaparot, Modeh Ani and Selihot at night.
  • His great lesson is to value and preserve each community's minhagim.
