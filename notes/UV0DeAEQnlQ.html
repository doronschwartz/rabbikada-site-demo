Not just "one God"

"Hashem Ehad": Jews have died with these words for centuries. Why a whole class to explain them? Ask what this principle should say, and most people answer: there is one God and no other.

The Rambam says something else. God's oneness "is not like the oneness of a pair, nor like the oneness of a species, nor like a man, whose oneness may be divided into many parts, nor like a simple body, one in number but divisible without end. Rather, He is one with a oneness that has no parallel." He does not even bother to say there are not two gods. The focus is the uniqueness of God's unity.

Before reaching that, the Rabbi went through five beliefs that "Hashem Ehad" rules out, from the oldest to the most subtle.

1. Polytheism

The belief in many gods was the great challenge of the time of the prophets, who constantly castigate the people for serving the Baalim. When Moshe told Pharaoh in God's name to let the people go, Pharaoh answered, in effect, that this God was not on his list. "You shall have no other gods" is the second of the Ten Commandments. Today, the Rabbi noted, this is no longer anyone's real test.

2. Dualism, open and subtle

In the time of Hazal the challenge was more sophisticated: two powers, a god of light and a god of darkness. Our prayers answer it: - Shaharit opens with "Who forms light and creates darkness", just as night gives way to day. - Arvit opens with "Who rolls away light before darkness and darkness before light". It is the same God. - The Mishnah says that someone who says "Modim, modim" twice is silenced, as if thanking two powers.

The Talmud tells of a Zoroastrian who said to Amemar that the upper half of his body belonged to one god and the lower to another. Then why, answered Amemar, does the one let water pass through to the other? Rabbi Yehoshua Leib Diskin, as a boy, laughed when his father warned him not to believe in two gods: "We say every morning that His glory fills the world. How could there be two?" Once we accept, from the first principle, that God is all-powerful, two gods is a contradiction in terms. That is why the Rambam did not need to say it.

But the Rabbi drew out a subtler dualism we all fall into. The Ramhal makes it a central idea of Derech Hashem: whenever we feel there is a force in the world outside God's control, we are in a sense dualists. Evil, suffering and temptation can seem to be outside God's rule. The purpose of creation and of the misvot is to reveal God's oneness, by recognising that nothing is outside Him and turning away from what pulls us. The serpent's promise to Havah was exactly that: you will have your own power.

He told the story of a Lubavitcher Rebbe interrogated in a Soviet prison. A guard put a gun to his head: "This toy has made many people talk." The Rebbe answered: "That works for people with one world and two gods. I have one God and two worlds." Someone who believes in only one world must explain the evil in it with rival powers. Someone who believes in one God knows that injustice here is put right in the next world.

He also warned against treating segulot and amulets as independent powers. The Rambam writes that those who write names of angels inside a mezuzah lose their share in the World to Come, because they turn a misvah of God's unity into a charm "for their own benefit".

3. The Trinity

Christians do not say they believe in three gods, but in one God with three parts. For us, pure unity allows no parts at all, so this is avodah zarah. He cited the Rambam's ruling during the Almohad persecution, against a scholar outside Morocco who said every Jew forced into Islam was an apostate. The Rambam replied that Islam is not idolatry, since Muslims believe in the same single God; Christianity is different. "What that means today is a discussion for another time," the Rabbi added. He also described the Ramban's public disputation with Pablo Christiani in Barcelona in 1263, in which the Trinity was a central issue.

4. Deism

A more sophisticated view says God created the world, perhaps the Big Bang, and then left it to run by itself. That is one God, but still a denial of His unity, because nothing can exist without Him. He returned to last week's image: a world imagined in the mind vanishes when the mind turns away. A carpenter can leave a table, because the wood and the laws of nature exist without him. God created something from nothing, so the world needs Him at every moment. And why would an all-powerful Creator make such a world and walk away?

5. Pantheism, and Hakham Nieto

Pantheism says God is nature and nature is God, and nothing more. The Rabbi said he never understood how this differs from atheism. Its famous proponent was Baruch Spinoza, put under herem by the Mahamad of Amsterdam.

About fifty years later came a famous episode in London. Hakham David Nieto of Bevis Marks, a doctor, mathematician and great scholar, said in a Shabbat sermon in 1703 something like "God and nature are one". Some members, led by a Joshua Sarfati, accused him of being a new Spinoza. He stood his ground and published his view. Hakham Sevi Ashkenazi of Amsterdam was asked to rule, and fully vindicated him in his responsa: Nieto meant that God is behind all of nature and runs it, the opposite of Spinoza.

The difference is small in words but huge in meaning. The Rabbi cited a gematria: hateva, nature, equals Elohim, 86. And teva shares its root with "sinking". We sink into the routine of sunrise and sunset and forget who runs it. That, he said, is the message of Hanukkah, coming soon.

The core: a unity we cannot picture

Now to the heart of the principle. The Mishneh Torah says God is not one like a category or a body with parts. And "He knows, but not with a knowledge external to Him as we do... He, His knowledge and His life are one." A person called Reuven is clever, kind and handsome: separate qualities in one person. God has no parts and no separate qualities.

We cannot really picture this. The Rambam, in a debate with an Arab philosopher, argued that we can only imagine combinations of things we know, like someone cycling across the sky, or a dragon. Something wholly outside our experience we cannot imagine. Absolute unity is such a thing; we can only believe it.

Then what about "Rahum veHanun", the 13 attributes we say on Yom Kippur? The Rambam (Moreh Nevuchim I:58) answers that God has no positive attributes. "Merciful" means He is not lacking in mercy, "mighty" that He is not weak. "The negative attributes of God are the true attributes." We can never grasp God positively. What we can know is that there is no other power.

In the questions, someone suggested that God simply contains all these qualities. The Rabbi answered that God created mercy; to call Him "mercy" puts Him beneath His own creation. Another offered the analogy of a programmer outside a computer world of ones and zeros, and the Rabbi agreed it is much the same idea.

Why then speak of God loving our prayers or wanting our misvot? Because we are human and must relate to Him in human terms; otherwise "we would close our books and leave". These are borrowed words. Every so often we must remind ourselves what they really mean, as one goes back to the meaning of a familiar equation.

He ended with Rav Moshe Feinstein, asked what one must have in mind when saying Shema: the simple meaning, that God is one, is enough. But now and then we should refresh our understanding of how unique that oneness is.

Summary

  • The second principle is not only that there is one God, but that His oneness is unlike any other.
  • "Hashem Ehad" rules out polytheism, dualism, the Trinity, deism and pantheism.
  • Subtle dualism, feeling that anything is outside God's control, is the real everyday test.
  • Hakham David Nieto was vindicated: God is behind nature, not identical with it.
  • God has no parts or positive attributes; our descriptions of Him are borrowed words we use in order to relate to Him.
