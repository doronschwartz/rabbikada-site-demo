The halacha

  • For the Rambam, with three hours a day one learns an hour of Tanakh, an hour of the Oral Torah, and an hour of its underlying logic, applying older laws to new situations.
  • The Rambam writes that the second category is satisfied by learning the final halacha (halakhah pesukah), which is why he wrote the Mishneh Torah, condensing the Mishnah, both Talmuds, the Tosefta, Sifra and Sifrei. Learning it, or any body of halacha, fulfils that part.
  • Today most Yeshivot spend most of the day on Talmud Bavli. Rabbenu Tam, grandson of Rashi, already addressed this: Bavel means mixture (from the Tower of Bavel), so the Talmud Bavli mixes all three categories and learning it all day suffices.
  • The Rambam clearly rejects this. The Shulhan Arukh codifies the Rambam; the Rema, for Ashkenazim, follows Rabbenu Tam, which is why studying only Gemara is so common.
  • Sepharadim never accepted this: one learns in order, Tanakh first, then Mishnah and Gemara, then the underpinnings. It is unfortunate when people expert in Gemara and halacha lack a grounding in Tanakh.
  • As a teenager in yeshiva in Gateshead he learned nine to ten hours of Gemara a day and little Mikra. He was told that Rabbi Leib Gurwicz, father of the present Rosh Yeshiva, explained that students were assumed to arrive with all of Tanakh mastered, which is unfortunately not the reality today.

Sources quoted

  • Rambam: halakhah pesukah satisfies the second third, hence the Mishneh Torah.
  • Rabbenu Tam: Talmud Bavli is a mixture covering all three parts.
  • Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam; the Rema follows Rabbenu Tam.

In one line

Learn broadly and in order, Tanakh, then the final halacha, then its depth, rather than Gemara alone.
