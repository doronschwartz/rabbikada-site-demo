When the reader loses his voice (Siman 140)

Siman 140 deals with interruptions. Its first case: the person reading becomes mute in the middle, or has a coughing fit and cannot go on. "I've never seen it happen," said the Rabbi, but the law is clear.

The Shulhan Arukh speaks of the time when each person called up read for himself. If he had already read three verses, he simply says the closing berakhah and sits down; there is no need to reach the usual stopping place. If he cannot even do that, the next person starts again from where the first one began, with a berakhah before and after.

Why go back to the beginning and not carry on from verse ten? Because every portion read must have a berakhah before it and after it. If the second person continued from verse ten, the first ten verses would have no closing berakhah. That is also why the first aliyah does not count as one of the seven. He makes his own opening berakhah because the first person did not have others in mind when he made his. The Rambam holds he does not need a new opening berakhah, but we do not follow that.

The Rema applies the same law today, when a baal koreh reads for everyone. As the Mishnah Berurah explains, the baal koreh reads on behalf of the person called up, so the same rules carry over. If the baal koreh loses his voice, another reader starts again from the beginning of that aliyah. The person called up does not repeat his berakhah, since he has already said it.

Speaking after the berakhah

What if the person called up says the berakhah and then speaks before the reading begins? If it was for the needs of the reading, such as "where do I look?", it is not an interruption.

This is why the authorities are comfortable with the custom of wishing a simana tava to the one who begins Bereshit: it belongs to the reading, a good start to the new cycle. Still, it is better to say it before the berakhah. On the other hand, people who call out "Hazak barukh" to someone who has just started the berakhah: "That really has to be cut out."

Shown the wrong place

A common case: in Parashat Bo the reading opens with words that also appear several times a page or two earlier, in Va'era. An inexperienced baal koreh shows the wrong place, the person called says the berakhah, and the reader begins, then realises it is the wrong passage. Must the berakhah be said again?

  • One opinion: the berakhah is on the whole Torah. Go to the right place and continue, with no new berakhah.
  • Another opinion: the berakhah was on the passage in front of him. If the right place is somewhere else, a new berakhah is needed, unless it is on the same page.
  • A compromise among Ashkenazim depends on which direction the right place is.

Sefaradim never repeat the berakhah, because the one who said it had in mind the whole Torah. The Rabbi remembered this happening to him when he read in yeshiva. The reading was from the wrong "Bo el Paroh", and a member of staff had the berakhah repeated. "But we don't repeat the berakhah."

Read standing (Siman 141)

The Torah is read standing, and one may not lean on the wall, a pillar or the tevah. The source is God's words to Moshe at the giving of the Torah: "And you, stand here with Me." The only exception in the Shulhan Arukh is a very heavy person who cannot support his own weight.

The same reasoning covers someone who is disabled. The Rabbi described what he does for a congregant who uses a wheelchair and cannot manage the steps to the tevah. Before the man's aliyah, not in the middle of the reading, the Sefer Torah is brought down to him so that he too can be called up.

What counts as leaning? If the support were taken away, would you fall? If so, it is leaning. Holding the Torah in the ordinary way is not. The Mishnah Berurah allows a short reader to lean over to read the top of a tall Sefer Torah, because standing is for the honour of the Torah, and leaning over it in order to read is itself for its honour.

One voice at a time

"Another very important halacha here which unfortunately lots of people don't know": two people should not read aloud together, because two voices cannot be heard at the same time.

The Rabbi applied the principle to prayer generally. One person leads and everyone follows quietly or listens, which allows those who cannot keep up to listen. The exception the class remembered is the Megillah, where Hazal allowed two readers because people love it so much that they listen anyway.

So the baal koreh reads aloud, and the person called up reads quietly with him. If he does not read at all, there is a concern that his berakhah is in vain. Some say that since the baal koreh reads on his behalf, listening is enough, and that is how we act. But he must pay attention. Being busy rehearsing the closing berakhah is the worst thing to do.

Someone said that the reading is sometimes too fast to keep up with. The Rabbi's answer: read along as much as you can.

How quietly? The Rabbi compared it to the Amidah:

  • Many Rishonim say you should say the words quietly but hear yourself.
  • No one allows saying it loud enough for others to hear.

He admitted that he once tried not hearing himself, but stopped because he was not sure he was saying the words properly. The basic halacha is to hear yourself, and not hearing yourself is a stringency. "Fine. It's a humra." At the Torah, the person called up should hear himself, but not so loudly that he distracts the baal koreh next to him. "Err on the side of caution."

Should you read for yourself?

The Mishnah Berurah records the Ashkenazi custom that the baal koreh always reads, even if the person called up is an expert. Tosafot and the Rosh give two reasons: so as not to embarrass those who cannot read, and because many who think they can read do not know the vowels or cantillation properly, and when the rabbi tells them not to read, it leads to fights. So a blanket rule was made.

"We never accepted this." Among Sefaradim, someone called up who knows how to read is offered the chance, and should read himself. "Really, you should be reading."

How many stand at the Torah

The Shulhan Arukh says that if the hazan himself is called up, someone must stand next to him. The Torah was given through an intermediary, Moshe, so the reading too is never done by one person alone. For Sefaradim two people are enough. The Mishnah Berurah adds a third, which is the general Ashkenazi practice: the reader, the person called up, and someone standing at the side to help and correct.

Practical summary

  • If the baal koreh cannot go on, a new reader starts again from the beginning of the aliyah, and the person called up does not repeat his berakhah.
  • Speaking for the needs of the reading is not an interruption. Calling out "Hazak barukh" during the berakhah should stop.
  • If the reader was shown the wrong place, Sefaradim go to the right place without a new berakhah.
  • Read standing, without leaning, and arrange for someone who cannot stand to be called up.
  • Only the baal koreh reads aloud. The person called up reads along quietly, hearing himself, and should read himself if he knows how.
