Topic: The obligation to eat bread in the sukkah on the first night, studying in the sukkah, and when rain exempts you Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 639, paragraphs 3-5 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Thursday 19 September 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The first night is like Pesah night

During the rest of Sukkot there is no obligation to eat a meal in the sukkah at all: one may eat less than a kabesah (an egg's volume) outside, and even the Shabbat and Yom Tov meals can be fulfilled with a kezayit of bread. The first night is different.

The Gemara learns a gezerah shavah: the Torah uses the word "fifteenth" both for Pesah and for Sukkot. Just as on Pesah night there is an obligation to eat a kezayit of massah, so on the first night of Sukkot there is an obligation to eat a kezayit of bread in the sukkah. Outside Israel this applies on the second night too, rabbinically.

The one difference is who is obligated. On Pesah night men and women are obligated. On Sukkot night only men are, since it is a time-bound misvah. Women's obligation on Pesah night is unusual, because "they too were part of the miracle", and that reason belongs to Pesah alone.

The comparison also covers timing. Just as the afikoman should be eaten before midnight, so the kezayit in the sukkah should be eaten before midnight.

Kezayit or kabesah?

A good question arose: we learnt that the berakhah leshev basukkah is said only on a fixed meal, which Hazal defined as a kabesah of bread (about 60 grams). How can a kezayit (about 30 grams) be enough on the first night?

  • Most Rishonim, and the Shulhan Arukh: during the rest of the week eating in the sukkah is voluntary, so only a proper meal triggers the berakhah. On the first night there is an obligation to eat, and that obligation itself means even a kezayit warrants the berakhah.
  • The Ran (Rabbenu Nissim): the gezerah shavah only teaches that one must eat something. The amount still follows the rules of the sukkah, so one needs a kabesah.

The Shulhan Arukh rules that a kezayit is enough. But since this is a Torah misvah that comes once a year, if you can, eat a kabesah, about two slices of bread or a good piece of hallah.

Must the first piece be plain?

Some Aharonim compare further: just as the first kezayit of massah is eaten plain, so the first kezayit of hallah on Sukkot night should be eaten plain. The Rabbi pointed out the weakness: massah is eaten plain because the misvah is to taste the massah, a new food, but "bread is bread is bread". Not everything carries across, just as women's obligation does not. His suggestion: have a kezayit plain and then another with salads if you wish. When the bread is cut, make sure every man gets enough, and finish the amount within about four and a half minutes, the strictest opinion.

Gluten-free hallah. Bread made from tapioca or other starches is not one of the five grains, so it is not bread for this misvah, even if it looks like hallah.

Why the first night is unique

Someone asked: if we must eat Shabbat and Yom Tov meals anyway, what is new about the first night? The Rabbi gave two answers.

  1. Shabbat meals can be eaten outside. A kezayit suffices for a Shabbat meal and need not be in the sukkah. People in flats in central London with nowhere to build a sukkah can rely on this: on the first two nights the men make Kiddush in the shul sukkah and eat the required bread there, say Birkat HaMazon, and then go home to eat with the family without bread. On Shabbat a small amount of bread at home is fine. On the first night, even a kezayit must be in the sukkah.
  2. Discomfort exempts you from a Shabbat meal but not from the first night. If you genuinely have no appetite on Shabbat afternoon after a big lunch, you need not eat bread, because the meal would not be a pleasure. But you cannot then eat a full meal of meat and rice and say you had no room for bread. On the first night of Sukkot you must eat even if it is not enjoyable, unless you are actually ill. The same applies to someone who usually avoids bread because it bloats him.

Waiting for nightfall and eating before midnight

The Rema says that, just as on Pesah night, the meal in the sukkah may not begin until nightfall. This matters most where people eat in the shul sukkah straight after an early service, when it may not yet be night. Kiddush may be made earlier, since the requirement is the eating. Someone eating at home usually reaches nightfall naturally by the time everyone is seated.

The Rema also says not to eat a proper meal from midday on Erev Sukkot, so that one eats the evening meal with appetite. The Rabbi was not sure the comparison is compelling, since on Pesah the point is to appreciate the new taste of massah. One Aharon even suggests not sleeping on Erev Sukkot afternoon. "These are extras upon extras." The main obligation: after nightfall, a kezayit or ideally a kabesah of bread, preferably plain, eaten within a few minutes.

Studying and praying in the sukkah

Ideally one spends all one's time in the sukkah: phone calls, work, relaxing. The Gemara distinguishes between kinds of study:

  • Deep, analytical learning needs a clear and settled mind, so it may be done in the house.
  • Light reading, such as Tehillim, Humash or a novel, should be done in the sukkah when the weather allows.
  • If you have a regular havruta in the Bet HaKneset, you may continue there, just as you leave home to pray.
  • The Mishnah Berurah adds that someone researching a halachic question, who needs many books from his library, may learn at home.

On the night of Hoshanah Rabbah, when people read through the night, in Israel this should be done in the sukkah. In England, the Rabbi admitted, "it works for 10 minutes, 20 minutes, and then you get freezing."

When does rain exempt you?

Paragraph 5: you may go into the house once enough rain falls into the sukkah to spoil the food, even a dish of beans. Most Rishonim read this as a low bar: once a light drizzle is soaking through the sekhakh and dripping down, you may go in. The test he offered: if water were dripping on your bed at home, how long would you stay? "Very little."

  • The Mishnah Berurah adds that extreme heat exempts like rain, and so does cold that makes the food congeal, even if you are well wrapped up.
  • After the rain stops, continued dripping counts as rain. Wind blowing twigs into the food can exempt you if it really bothers you; an occasional twig does not. Sensitivity differs between people, and that is legitimate.
  • Heaters in the sukkah are fine.
  • Once it is raining, you may put a plastic cover over the sekhakh to protect the sukkah, and then stay under it or go inside. But under the cover it is no longer a sukkah.

Someone said he sits under an umbrella in the sukkah when it rains. The Rabbi was clear: "Sitting with an umbrella is not sitting in a sukkah, you're sitting under an umbrella." If it is raining you are exempt, so simply go inside.

Rain on the first night

The Rema adds that all this applies except on the first night. Following the Rosh and the Tur, Ashkenazim hold that the link to Pesah means the kezayit must be eaten in the sukkah even in the rain; one may eat it quickly and go back in. Most poskim disagree: the normal rules of the sukkah still apply, so rain exempts even on the first night, and that is the Sefaradi practice.

The Mishnah Berurah notes that because of the dispute, someone eating in the rain cannot say leshev basukkah. Some Ashkenazim therefore wait for the rain to stop, even until close to midnight, before Kiddush. The Mishnah Berurah objects that the festival is a time of joy; one cannot keep guests and family waiting hungry for hours.

For Sefaradim too, the obligation lasts all night. If it rained at seven and you ate at home, but at nine or even close to midnight the sky clears, the men must go out, wash, eat a kezayit of bread in the sukkah, say Birkat HaMazon and come back in. One may eat it standing.

Practical summary

  • On the first night (and the second, outside Israel) men must eat at least a kezayit of bread in the sukkah after nightfall and before midnight; if possible, a kabesah, the first part plain, within a few minutes.
  • Gluten-free "hallah" not made from the five grains does not count.
  • Light reading belongs in the sukkah when the weather allows; deep study and research may be done in the house.
  • Rain enough to spoil food, extreme heat, or cold that congeals the food exempts you. An umbrella in the sukkah does not make it a sukkah.
  • Ashkenazim eat the first-night kezayit in the sukkah even in the rain, without the berakhah; Sefaradim are exempt, but if the rain stops later that night they must go out and eat a kezayit.
