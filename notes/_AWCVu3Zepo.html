Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 4, Seifim 21-25 and 29-30 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 14 February 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

Last time the Rabbi reviewed Seif 20 and discussed the status of Mamzerim today, and whether there is any takanah for them, at least for male Mamzerim. Today he went quickly through Seifim 21-25, then skipped Seifim 26-28, which form one big sugya on an arusah and a penuyah and are for another class, and went on to Seifim 29-30, on a father who says that his child is a Mamzer.

Seif 21: a non-Jew and incest

A non-Jew has a forbidden relationship, and the child converts. The principle we have seen several times is that there is no Mamzerut among non-Jews. The relationship was terrible, but it happened while he was a non-Jew, so the child is non-Jewish and, once converted, a regular Jew. The Rabbi explained that the Shulhan Arukh's wording "a non-Jew who had relations and then converted" is not exact (lav davka). The same applies if the non-Jew never converted and the woman was Jewish: the child has no Mamzer status. The Shulhan Arukh mentions the case with kiddushin only to show that even then the child is mutar bakahal.

Seif 22: a convert and a Mamzer

A convert (ger) or a freed slave may marry a Mamzeret, and a Mamzer may marry a giyoret. The Gemara in Kiddushin learns from "kehal Hashem" that it means someone who is part of the community by birth, not by conversion, so a convert is not included in the prohibition.

Two questions. The Rabbi said he had never seen a teshuvah on whether ten converts count as a kahal for a minyan, or whether six converts and four born Jews do. We only see this distinction between a convert and a born Jew in the context of Mamzerim, which is peculiar.

Why is the child a Mamzer? The principle is that where there is kiddushin and an aveirah, the child follows the one with the fault. Here there is kiddushin and no aveirah, yet the child is a Mamzer. The Gemara learns this from an extra word in the verse, so this is a special law.

The Beit Shemuel. The Beit Shemuel adds that there is no Mamzer prohibition, but marrying a Mamzeret is still not the right thing to do, because the child will be a Mamzer. "Mutar" in the Shulhan Arukh means permitted, not recommended. The Rabbi added that the idea "do not multiply Mamzerim" is a later idea, and he was not sure of its source in the Gemara.

Seif 23: how long a convert keeps the status

A convert who is the child of two converts keeps this status, so he may marry a Mamzeret. This is not rare, since couples often convert together. There are two versions in the Gemara of how long it continues: ten generations, or until the conversion has been forgotten. The Rabbi said the Rambam follows the second version, which can be stricter or more lenient depending on the case: after four generations it may already be forgotten, or it may remain well known even for twelve or thirteen generations. It needs an unbroken line of a convert marrying a convert, since in the next seif the status is lost.

A question from the Tosafot. If there is kiddushin and no aveirah, why does a child of a convert and an Israelite become an Israelite and lose the convert status? Normally we say the child follows the father. The Rabbi explained that Tosafot hold the status does remain in that case. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh disagree, because a Mamzeret is a special exception: the Gemara had to learn it out. Where no aveirah is involved, the rule is to follow the male, but in cases of fault the child follows the more blemished parent.

Seif 24: Mamzer and Mamzeret

A definite Mamzer and a definite Mamzeret may marry each other, and by Torah law they may even marry a safek, though not by rabbinic law. The Rabbi gave the case of a safek Mamzer: a father had relations with a woman who may have been married before, and it is not clear that she was divorced. Such a person may not marry a definite Mamzer, nor a safek Mamzer. According to the Beit Shemuel this leaves him able to marry only a convert, which is a difficult result, since we find nobody in the Torah who cannot marry anyone. The Rabbi stressed that this safek is only rabbinic. He also noted that this is the Gemara behind the mahloket between the Rambam and the Rashba: whether a doubt in a Torah matter is always treated strictly. Here the Gemara makes an exception.

Seif 25: a yevamah

A widow who did not wait three months and married the brother of her late husband (yavam) then gave birth seven months later. Either way the child is kosher: if it is from the first husband, it is a child of a widow who was permitted to the yavam, and if it is from the yavam, also fine. The problem comes when she then has a second child with the yavam. The first child may have been the first husband's, which would make her relations with the yavam forbidden, so the later children may be safek Mamzerim. The Rabbi said the status depends on how many children she has had.

Seif 29: a father says "this is not my son"

The case. A woman comes to Beit Din and says the child she gave birth to is not her husband's. She is not believed: the child has a hezkat kashrut, and she has no power to take it away. The Beit Yoseph discusses the case of an arusah, where the child has no hezkat kashrut since they should not have been together, and the husband insists he had relations with her. That is a mahloket Rishonim, and will come later.

The main law. A father says of his child (or of one of his sons) that he is not his, and the child becomes a definite Mamzer. This is the din of Rabbi Yehuda in Kiddushin and Bava Batra, and the Gemara learns it from "yakir", the father recognises his firstborn. The Rabbi stressed that the context there is inheritance: the father is identifying who his firstborn is, and so "older" children become Mamzerim. This also works for any child, not just one; it can be done for several above him.

What the father cannot do:

  • If the child already has children, he is not believed on the grandchildren, and so not on his son either.
  • If the wife says the child was from a non-Jew or a slave, the father cannot contradict her, and the child is kosher.

Why is this different from a Sotah? A student asked why the father is believed here, but in the Torah there is the process of Sotah. The Rabbi's answer: a son is, in a way, the father's own, so he has the power to decide his status. The father's statement has no effect on the wife: she can still marry a Kohen. He testifies about the child, not about her, and a Mamzer is created even if she was raped. The wife is only incriminated indirectly.

Three views of the Rishonim, which the Shulhan Arukh seems to reject:

The Rabbi said he has seen contemporary Beit Din teshuvot that combine these three views.

Further limits that the Rabbi stressed:

  • If the father only says "he is not mine", and does not say he is the child of a Jewish man, the child is not a definite Mamzer, only a hezkat Mamzer (a safek), since she may have had relations with a non-Jew. A safek is only rabbinic, and can be treated leniently. According to the Rambam almost all cases are therefore only rabbinic.
  • If the father did anything to show the child is kosher, he can no longer go back, and he lost his neemanut. The class suggested examples: the father gave him a name at the brit, or called him "ben" the father in the Torah reading. The Rabbi agreed: "this is a huge let-out". Otherwise a dangerous father could cause havoc.
  • The father is believed only if he is married to the woman, not after a single night.

Seif 30: a man who says he is a Mamzer

A person who says of himself that he is a Mamzer is believed, because "a person's own prohibition is his own". But he is still forbidden to marry a Mamzeret until it is known for sure. His child has the same status as him. If he already has grandchildren, he is not believed for them, nor for his son. If he then has relations with the daughter of a Kohen, she remains kosher. This only applies to himself.

The Rabbi said Seif 31 onward, on a foundling (asufi) and a silent child (shetuki), is a big sugya, then Seifim 26-28 on the arusah. He thought they needed two and a half or three more classes to finish the Siman, and then a quick review.

Practical summary

  • A child of a non-Jew has no Mamzer status, and a convert and a Mamzer may marry. The child will be a Mamzer.
  • A convert's special status continues in an unbroken line of converts, but the length is a mahloket: ten generations or until forgotten.
  • A mother cannot declare her own child a Mamzer.
  • A father can, but only with many limits: not for grandchildren, not if the wife says it was a non-Jew, and not if he earlier treated the child as kosher.
  • If he only says "not mine", it is at most a safek, which is rabbinic.
  • A man who says he himself is a Mamzer is believed for himself and his child.
