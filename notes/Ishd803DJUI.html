Topic: Injury by man (bidei adam) versus by Heaven (bidei shamayim): Rashi and the Rambam

Sources: The Gemara; Rashi; the Rambam

Daily Halacha: Even HaEzer

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The halacha

  • The Gemara says, and all accept, that the status of pesua dakka applies only to an injury by man. If it came by Heaven, the man is kasher even if he cannot reproduce.
  • The source compares the verse of pesua dakka with that of the mamzer: no one is born a mamzer; mamzerut comes from a human act. So too pesua dakka must come about through man.
  • Rashi in one place defines bidei shamayim literally: thunder or hail, or one born that way. Elsewhere the Gemara calls one made impotent by the sun (seris hamah) kasher, and Rashi explains it is an illness. So for Rashi illness too counts as bidei shamayim.
  • The Rambam: bidei adam is where a person cut them, or a dog bit them, or one tripped onto a thorn, or someone shot a thorn or kicked a ball at him. Even with no fault or negligence at all, this is bidei adam.
  • Bidei shamayim for the Rambam: born that way, became ill, or had them cut because of illness, which seems to mean an operation that was unavoidable.
  • The Rambam's principle seems to be that any unnecessary physical force from a human being, even through an animal or object, is bidei adam; where it was necessary or came from illness or birth, it is bidei shamayim.
  • So by both Rashi and the Rambam an innocent accident can still make one a pesua dakka. More Rishonim and modern applications follow.

Sources quoted

  • The Gemara: only bidei adam, learned from the comparison with the mamzer.
  • Rashi: thunder, hail, birth or illness are bidei shamayim.
  • The Rambam: unnecessary human force, even through a dog, thorn or ball, is bidei adam.

In one line

Only an injury caused by man makes one a pesua dakka, and the Rambam defines that as any unnecessary human force, even without fault.
