Where we are

Siman 13 has two parts. Seifim 1-10 deal with the three-month wait after a divorce or widowhood (havhanah). From Seif 11 to the end the Siman deals with a pregnant or nursing woman (meuberet umeneket haverah). Today the Rabbi finished the first part with Seifim 9 and 10, and then began the second part. He did not read the Shulhan Arukh of Seif 11 yet. The plan was to go through the Gemara and the Rishonim first, and to read the Shulhan Arukh and the commentators next week.

Seif 9: a second get

A woman is divorced, and as the three months come to an end a problem is found with the get, so a second get is written to be safe (mipnei halaaz, mipnei hahumrah). From which get do we count the three months?

  • The first opinion: count from the second get.
  • Rabbenu Tam: count from the first get, the real one. This is a decree of Hazal, and the real get was given.

The Shulhan Arukh brings both. Since he brings Rabbenu Tam as the second view, the Rabbi explained that Sefaradim, who follow the Shulhan Arukh, can count from the first get. The Rema rules like the first opinion, so one waits three months from the second get. The Rema adds that the same applies where there was a rumour of kiddushin (kol kidushin) and a get was given as a precaution. There, even Rabbenu Tam would agree that one must wait.

A further question: the second get is written, but only delivered three months later. Do we count from the writing or from the giving?

  • In Seif 1-4 we saw this very dispute. The Rambam and the Rif count from the writing (ketivah), the Rosh counts from the giving (netinah). The Shulhan Arukh follows the writing. The Rema is strict and follows the giving.
  • The Beit Shemuel therefore says the Rema would require waiting from the giving of the second get.
  • The Aharonim argue that here one can be lenient and count from the writing. There is a double doubt (sfek sfekah). First, the whole matter is a decree of Hazal. Second, the Rema is strict in two separate places (a second get, and counting from the giving), but putting both strictures together, we can say the three months run from the writing.

Seif 10: marrying during the waiting period

What if the couple married before the three months passed? The Gemara does not say.

  • The Rambam: the kiddushin works and they stay together, but the man is placed under a ban (nidui) as a punishment.
  • Rashi and other Rishonim: we force him to divorce her, and they may remarry after the three months.

The Rabbi noted the difficulty for a kohen, who cannot take back a divorced woman. The Rema adds that where the couple acted by mistake (beshogeg), not knowing the law, they need not divorce but simply wait for the three months to pass. If three months have already passed by the time it is discovered, there is nothing to do. The Rabbi presented several combinations (Yisrael or kohen, deliberate or by mistake), and said we would see them again in the Shulhan Arukh.

The man who ran away. The Gemara says that if someone did kiddushin wrongly and then ran away, we do not place him under a ban. The Rishonim give two reasons:

Reason

Running away is a kind of atonement (galut mekhaperet)

Running away shows he does not want her, so the kiddushin are cancelled

The Shulhan Arukh brings the different views. That completes the first part of the Siman.

The sugya of the meuberet umeneket haverah

Hazal added a decree. If a woman is pregnant, or nursing, and her husband died, she may not marry until the child is weaned. The Rabbi went through three Gemaras.

Gemara 1: the reason. The three-month wait exists to know who the father is, but a pregnant woman has no such doubt. So why wait? The Gemara tries several reasons:

  1. She may become pregnant a second time and harm the first foetus. The Gemara asks that this applies to any couple, and answers that a couple can use contraception or rely on Hashem. The Gemara then looks for another reason.
  2. The second husband's relations put pressure on the foetus (daksa). The Gemara answers that a man cares about his own child, so we do not suspect him. A second husband, however, does not care about another man's foetus.
  3. The final reason: a new pregnancy spoils her milk, and the first baby will suffer (meakir halav). The Gemara asks why a normal couple is not forbidden to live together. The answer: a father feeds the baby with eggs and milk and supports the mother. A second husband has no reason to pay for another man's child. The widow could claim from the estate, but she is embarrassed to come to Beit Din, and the child could die.

Rashi and the Rambam differ here. Rashi says the extra food goes to the child, but the Rambam says the husband feeds the mother, so that she has milk.

Gemara 2: how long. There are views of 24, 21, 18 and 15 months. The conclusion is 24 months, not counting the day of the birth and the day of the engagement, "so essentially 24 months and two days". The Gemara has stories where the wait was shorter.

Gemara 3: when the concern is gone.

  • The child dies: she may remarry at once. Mar bar Rav Ashi raises the worry that a mother would harm her child to remarry, and dismisses it: mothers do not strangle their children.
  • The child is weaned: she still waits, because she might hurry the weaning.
  • The child is given to a nursemaid: she still waits, since the nursemaid may change her mind and give the baby back. An exception was made for a family of the Reish Galuta, the exilarch, whom nobody would defy.

The Rabbi's summary: three Gemaras, the reason, the time and the exceptions. The ruling is that death is permitted, weaning and a nursemaid are not.

The big arguments

How to count 24 months. The Rabbi asked the class how we count, based on the three months we counted as 90 days. The views:

  1. Twenty-four months of 30 days, 720 days, and in a leap year an extra month for the child's benefit, so up to 750 days. He compared this to renting a house for a year in Bava Mesia.
  2. Twenty-four lunar months, about 708 days.
  3. A solar count, based on a Midrash, about 730 or 732 days.

The Beit Yoseph rules 24 months, about 708 days, without adding a leap month. The Rema is stricter and adds the extra month in a leap year as a lehathilah precaution.

Why does the Rambam add daksa? The other Rishonim give only the milk. The Rambam gives daksa as well, even in the pregnancy. Three explanations:

  • The Maharik: the milk reason does not help a woman who is known never to be able to nurse. Daksa covers her.
  • The Beit Yoseph: the Rambam holds that both reasons stand, since the Gemara could have answered differently, and the Beit Yoseph is willing to read the Gemara that way.
  • The Noda BiYehudah: if the only reason were the milk, a couple could marry for the pregnancy, declare a limited marriage (a known option), and divorce before the birth. The reason it is forbidden is that the second husband might harm the child so as to stay married to her. The Rabbi called this "really beautiful": it joins daksa to the later reason.

Almanah and grusha. The Gemaras speak of a widow (almanah). Rabbenu Shimshon says the decree applies only to a widow. A divorced woman (grushah) waits three months only. Rabbenu Tam says the din of a widow and a divorcee is equal, and she waits 24 months. The first reason given is that a widow continues to receive maintenance (mezonot) from the estate until she claims her ketubah, so there is an obligation (shibud) to nurse. A divorcee has no such link. This was left for next week.

Practical summary

  • Seif 9: after a second get the Rema is strict and counts three months from the second one. Sefaradim may count from the first.
  • A second get written but delivered later: the Beit Shemuel is strict, the Aharonim allow counting from the writing, on a double doubt.
  • Marrying during the wait: the Rambam says the marriage stands; the Shulhan Arukh follows the view that they divorce; the Rema is lenient where it was by mistake.
  • A widow who is pregnant or nursing waits 24 months, not counting the two days. The Beit Yoseph counts about 708 days; the Rema adds a month in a leap year.
  • Death of the child permits remarriage. Weaning or a nursemaid does not, except where the Rishonim allow.
  • The case of a grushah is a mahloket that continues next week.
