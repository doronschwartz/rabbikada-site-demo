Topic: Whether lighting the candles is an acceptance of Shabbat, praying Minhah after accepting, husband and wife, and following the community Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263, seifim 10-12, with the Rema and the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in his Thursday-night Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 15 July 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Seif 10: the opinion of the Bahag

The previous class dealt with the obligation of the person and the obligation of the house: guests, people living alone, and groups lighting together. Now a very practical topic.

The Shulhan Arukh quotes the Bahag (Halachot Gedolot): once you have lit the Shabbat candles, Shabbat has begun for you and you may no longer do work. This is not stated in the Talmud. The Talmud knows of accepting Shabbat by saying so, but the Bahag adds a new idea: lighting itself is the acceptance, much as Shehiyanu on Yom Tov marks the start of the festival.

The majority disagree. The Rosh, Tosafot, the Ramban and the Rashba point to an explicit Gemara. On Friday afternoon three blasts were blown: to bring the people in from the fields, to close the shops, and then to light the candles. After the third blast they waited long enough to roast a small fish, and only then did Shabbat begin. "You clearly tell me they're lighting the candle and they're still frying fish afterwards."

A further development. Some three centuries later the Shibbolei HaLeket records that some women, having accepted Shabbat with the lighting, do not blow out the match but let it go out by itself. The Rabbi argued that even according to the Bahag, extinguishing the match should be part of the act of lighting, but this is how the custom grew.

Stipulating. Even according to the Bahag, some say a woman may stipulate, "I am lighting, but I am not accepting Shabbat", and it works. Others say it does not help.

How do we rule?

The second opinion in the Shulhan Arukh says the acceptance is by the community, at Barkhu, or today when the community says "Boi Khallah" in Lekha Dodi. Until then one may still work, even after lighting. The Rabbi taught a principle of learning the Shulhan Arukh: when he brings an opinion in the name of one authority and then writes "and some say", the second is usually his ruling.

The Rema writes that the Ashkenazi custom is that the woman accepts Shabbat with the lighting, unless she stipulates, and a stipulation in her heart is enough. The Mishnah Berurah adds that she should only stipulate when there is a need, such as lighting before taking a taxi to her parents for Friday night.

Sefaradim. The Rabbi said that the main halacha follows the view that lighting is not an acceptance at all. Even so, there is a simple way to cover all opinions: once a year, for example on Rosh Hashanah, a woman should have in mind that whenever she lights, she does not intend to accept Shabbat. That gives a double doubt: perhaps lighting is not acceptance at all, and even if it is, perhaps the stipulation works. "And that's what we tell our women": she may blow out the match, and in summer she may light at eight and still do something she forgot. Even without the stipulation she may do so, because the main ruling is that lighting is not acceptance. If she explicitly says she is accepting Shabbat, that is different.

A woman who for years thought she accepted Shabbat with the lighting may change, since she never actually said so.

Placing the match. For those who are strict, the match should be placed down gently so that it goes out by itself. That is gerama, indirect extinguishing, and is permitted.

Minhah after accepting Shabbat

The Mishnah Berurah writes that a woman who accepted Shabbat with the lighting can no longer pray Minhah, since Minhah belongs to the weekday, and should pray Arvit twice. The Rabbi said that Hakham Ben Sion Abba Shaul disagrees totally and brings many proofs. A woman, or a man, who has accepted Shabbat may still pray Minhah as long as it is before sunset, though it is better to pray earlier. Similarly, on a Friday that is Rosh Hodesh, a man who accepted Shabbat early and realises he did not pray Musaf may still pray it. "The timing of those prayers is separate to an acceptance of Shabbat."

Men. The Mishnah Berurah writes that even for Ashkenazim the custom does not apply to men who light. The Rabbi found this strange, since men and women are equally obligated in the misvah, and saw it as further reason not to be concerned.

The rest of the house, and husband and wife

The Rema adds that even if the woman has accepted Shabbat with her lighting, the rest of the household may still work until Barkhu.

The other way round. The husband goes to synagogue and accepts Shabbat at eight; Shabbat begins at eight thirty. May his wife keep cooking? The Rabbi brought Rav Moshe Feinstein: a husband cannot take a vow on his wife's behalf, and accepting extra Shabbat is like a vow. So she may continue, and the same applies the other way round.

He added one caution. Even a non-Jewish servant may not do melakhah specifically for a Jew on Shabbat. So once a man has accepted Shabbat, if his wife is doing something specifically for him, perhaps that should stop. Cooking for the family or for herself is fine. Children, who have not accepted Shabbat, may carry something home for a father who has. Another Jew who has not yet accepted Shabbat may also be asked.

Where to light. One berakhah covers the house, but it is better to light where one eats. The candles must be lit where they will be used. Lighting in an unused room and carrying them to the table does not work. If both places are in use, one may light in one and, before Shabbat, move them to the other. On Shabbat itself lit candles may not be moved.

Seifim 11-12: Arvit and the community

Seif 11. Once a person has prayed Arvit of Shabbat, even if the community has not, Shabbat has begun for him, and saying "I do not accept Shabbat" does not help. The Mishnah Berurah notes that this is unlike lighting, where a stipulation helps. It is like Shehiyanu: once said, it cannot be undone.

Seif 12. The Mordekhai rules that if most of the community has accepted Shabbat, the minority must follow. Someone at home, ill, in a town where all the synagogues bring in Shabbat at eight must stop at eight. This has no Talmudic source but was accepted. A synagogue that brings in Shabbat later is its own community and is not bound by the others. The poskim write that as long as some community has not yet accepted Shabbat, one may attach oneself to it.

The Rabbi said he is not comfortable with this. Today communities are intermingled, and someone who prays in one synagogue three times a day seems bound by it. Hakham Ben Sion was unsure whether a Sefaradi may rely on an Ashkenazi community. One later authority writes that the whole rule does not apply today, since most Jews do not go to synagogue on Friday night, and there is perhaps a hint to this in the Mishnah Berurah (seif katan 51). The Rabbi said it is "very grey" because it depends on what a community means, and one must be honest: a community one has never had any connection to cannot be used as an excuse.

Practical summary

  • The Bahag holds that lighting is acceptance of Shabbat; the Rosh, Tosafot, the Ramban and the Rashba disagree, and the Shulhan Arukh follows them.
  • Ashkenazim (the Rema) treat the woman's lighting as acceptance unless she stipulates; the Mishnah Berurah allows a stipulation only when needed.
  • Sefaradim: lighting is not acceptance. It is good for a woman to have in mind once a year that she does not accept Shabbat with the lighting, and she may then put out the match and continue working until Shabbat begins.
  • Someone who accepted Shabbat may still pray Minhah before sunset, following Hakham Ben Sion, against the Mishnah Berurah.
  • One spouse's acceptance does not bind the other, though once the husband has accepted, his wife should avoid melakhah done specifically for him.
  • Once you pray Arvit of Shabbat, Shabbat has begun for you. Following the majority of the community is a real halacha, but what counts as your community is a difficult question today.
