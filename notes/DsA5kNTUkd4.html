These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

This is the last class of Essay 2 before the summer break. After many chapters arguing that Hashem has no body and that every physical description of Him is a metaphor, Rav Saadiah closes the essay with three questions: two fairly simple, one needing real discussion. Then the chapter turns poetic and ends in love of Hashem.

Question 1: how can we speak of a God we have never sensed?

This is the atheist's classic challenge. If Hashem has no body, no touch and nothing physical, how can we believe in Him when we have never sensed Him?

Rav Saadiah's answer is short. We speak all the time about things we have never touched. We know that truth is good and deceit is bad, though neither can be seen or held. We know that something cannot be present and absent at the same time, though no one has ever felt that either. Knowing something is true does not depend on sensing it. The same is true of Hashem.

Question 2: how can Hashem be everywhere?

The Rabbi introduced this with the children's song, "Hashem is here, Hashem is there, Hashem is truly everywhere." Before creation there was only the endless light of Hashem. Once the universe was created, it took up a place. Is Hashem in that place, or did He, as it were, withdraw to make room?

Rav Saadiah is clear. Hashem existed before there was space. If space or physical things could separate Him or reduce Him in any way, He would not have created them. So His presence after creation is exactly the same as before, with no gap and no difference. The verse he brings: "Can a man hide in secret places and I not see him? Do I not fill heaven and earth?"

So Rav Saadiah offers pictures: a wall does not stop sound, glass does not stop light, and the sun still shines through the dirt of the world. These show that something can pass through a barrier without being blocked.

An old argument

Not everyone agreed. The Rabbi told of Shir HaYichud, an anonymous and very old song that Ashkenazim once recited daily and now say only on Yom Kippur. It says there is no place where Hashem is not, and that the walls and the dirt of the world do not divide or defile Him, which sounds very like Rav Saadiah.

Rav Moshe Taku, one of Rav Saadiah's fiercest opponents, assumed Rav Saadiah wrote it and called it forbidden. If Hashem is everywhere, he argued, He is even in dirty places, which degrades Him; when He created the world He withdrew, to leave room for bad as well as good. The Maharshal followed him and ruled the song should not be said. The Rema was saddened by this. The ruling rested on one very old book, he wrote, and he recalled the Rambam's remark in the Guide about the odd habit of thinking that the older a book is, the truer it must be. He saw the song as deep and said it should be recited.

The Vilna Gaon later held the same, that "His glory fills the whole earth" means His providence is everywhere. The Rabbi noted that the majority of the Rishonim and the sources are in line with Rav Saadiah, and that our minds cannot fully grasp how Hashem can be in everything while the universe exists as it does. Rav Saadiah's point is only that it is possible.

A question from the class

Someone asked where Hashem is, for those who hold He is not in the world. The Rabbi answered that on that view He is outside the universe and oversees it through hashgahah. Does that change prayer? No: we speak to Hashem however we understand Him, and the question is one of philosophical understanding, not practice.

Question 3: how can Hashem know the future?

We cannot know the future because all our knowledge starts with the senses, and no one can sense the future. We also need a cause, a reason, to know anything. Hashem does not know through senses, and for Him the present and the future are the same. So He knows the future without any need for a cause.

The Rabbi added the Rambam's simpler answer: Hashem is not in time, so for Him there is no past, present or future. "He declares the end from the beginning."

In short: we can speak of Hashem as we speak of moral truths we have never touched; He can be everywhere, somehow; and His knowledge is not like ours.

The close of the essay: love of Hashem

The chapter now becomes beautiful and poetic. When you have understood everything in this essay, says Rav Saadiah, your soul will feel strong, and you will find comfort in all its chambers. "My soul desires You at night, and my spirit within me seeks You in the morning."

One strange word

He writes that you will have a strong love with nothing to hold it back. The Hebrew word he uses for this is unusual, and the Rabbi pointed out that the Rambam uses the very same word at the end of Hilchot Teshuvah, where he describes the love of Hashem as an obsession ("always be obsessed with it"). The Ra'avad asks where the Rambam got this word, because in Tanakh the root means a song or a mistake, never obsession. The Rabbi's view is that the Rambam took it from Rav Saadiah, whom he had clearly read closely. As far as the Rabbi knew, Rav Saadiah was the first to use the word this way.

A participant suggested the root may be the one with a sin ("very great"), which would suit love, while the shin version means a mistake. The Rabbi found this fascinating, but the Rambam's text had no vowels, so there is no proof, and the Ra'avad's question suggests he had the shin.

What this love looks like

Rav Saadiah goes on. Thinking about Hashem will satisfy you more than the finest food and drink. It will make everything we do good for serving Him. We will trust in Hashem always. If He does good to us, the soul will thank Him, and if He does otherwise, it will be patient. Even if we come close to death, we will still hope in Him and will not suspect Him of any wrong. The more we think about Hashem, the more awe we will have, and the more praise and joy.

He also says we should answer those who question Hashem by working things through the intellect, not with a heavy hand. A thousand years ago, Rav Saadiah says we do not answer atheistic questions by pushing people away. We reason with them.

Praise without exaggeration

The most interesting line, he said, is that the soul will praise Hashem "rationally", not with falsehoods and exaggerations. What does that mean?

In the Middle Ages people asked whether Hashem can do the impossible: make five more than ten without adding to it, fit the universe into a ring without shrinking it, bring back yesterday. Rav Saadiah says these questions are nonsense. When heretics ask, we answer that Hashem can do anything, but what they ask about is not a thing, and asking whether He can do nothing is meaningless.

The Rabbi said Rav Saadiah is the first to state clearly that nonsense has no place in Judaism, in contrast to the Christian idea that the more absurd something is, the more one should believe it.

What we do praise

Instead we praise Hashem for real things: that He is eternal, one, living, all-powerful and all-knowing, the Creator of everything, who does nothing pointlessly and no injustice, does good to all, has no change in Him, and whose kingship will never end.

One last point. We sometimes praise not Hashem Himself but His attributes, as in "Blessed is the name of His glorious kingdom," and even the attributes of attributes. Rav Saadiah explains that this builds up love, as in the Megillah, where each added adjective adds to the grandeur. Yet after all our praises, He is above all blessing and praise.

Three lines to take away

Rav Saadiah ends the whole essay with three things to remember. Think about everything you have learned in your soul. Do not get carried away by the exact words of verses and of life. And be ready to put the words aside, as metaphors and analogies, to bring things closer and make them easier to understand, and not to be taken literally.

Recap of Essay 2

The Rabbi gave a brief review. The Introduction explained the importance of the book, and Essay 1 gave the proofs that the universe was created. Essay 2 showed that Hashem is one, living, able, all-powerful and unlike anything else, argued for His oneness against the Christian trinity, and went through the ten anthropomorphic descriptions, all to be read as metaphors. Chapter 13 then leads to a deep love of Hashem.

Summary

  1. How can we talk about God without sensing Him? We speak about moral and logical truths we have never touched.
  2. Can Hashem be everywhere? Rav Saadiah says His presence is exactly as it was before creation, though how is beyond us.
  3. How can He know the future? His knowledge is not like ours, and for Him there is no past or future.
  4. Do not praise Hashem with nonsense. He can do anything that is a thing, and we praise Him with real, true attributes.
  5. The goal is love. The study of the essay should end in a strong, steady love of Hashem.
