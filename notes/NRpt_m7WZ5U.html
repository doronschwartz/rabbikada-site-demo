Topic: Sukkot as Zeman Simhatenu: what happiness is, and how Kohelet, the sekhakh and the four species teach it Sources: The Rosh (as recorded in the Tur) and the Rambam on the timing of Sukkot; Kohelet; the Gemara on Rabbi Yohanan Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, on Zoom, Wednesday 27 September 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The time of our happiness, and why in autumn

All three festivals carry a misvah to be happy, but only Sukkot is called "Zeman Simhatenu," the time of our happiness. A few days before the festival, the Rabbi set out to understand what that happiness is and how we achieve it.

He began by asking what Sukkot commemorates. The participants offered both answers given by the Tannaim: the huts we lived in when we left Egypt with almost nothing, or the clouds of glory that surrounded us in the desert. Either way, why now? Pesah falls on the date we left Egypt, Shavuot on the day we received the Torah, Hanukah on the anniversary of the victory. Sukkot is not tied to a date. One participant answered that it is the harvest festival, which is true, but on the reason of the huts or the clouds, why do it a few days after Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur?

The Rishonim give two opposite answers. - The Ashkenazi answer, which the Rabbi gave in the name of the Rosh, Rabbenu Asher of Germany (it is recorded by his son in the Tur): if Sukkot were in the summer, sitting outside would look like camping or a picnic. It is given in autumn, when the weather turns cold and people bring things indoors, so that it is clear we sit in the sukkah for the misvah. - The Rambam: Sukkot is given now because in the summer it would be too hot to sit in a sukkah, and in winter too cold. Autumn is the most pleasant time to be outside.

Two lessons follow. First, always understand the context of the rabbi who said something. The Rosh lived in Europe, where the best weather is July and August; the Rambam lived in Egypt and the Middle East, where the summer is scorching and the autumn is perfect. "This is why whenever I give shiurim... I always say about where they're from."

Second, the two answers reflect two worldviews. In the first, the misvah is a test: Hashem makes it a little harder so we show we are doing it for the right reason. In the Rambam's, Hashem gives us the misvah at the best possible time because He wants the world to cooperate with us. The Rabbi described this as the approach the Sefaradim have always taken: the misvot are a way to live the best life, not a trap to catch us out.

What is happiness?

Philosophers have spilt oceans of ink on the question. The American Declaration of Independence speaks of life, liberty and "the pursuit of happiness": life and liberty can be given, but happiness can only be pursued.

A participant suggested you simply need to be happy with yourself, and things will follow. "That's easier said than done," the Rabbi replied: what would you tell someone who comes for advice and says he is not happy? Happiness, like a good marriage or any worthwhile relationship, is something you work on. Sukkot gives us perspectives to help.

Kohelet on Sukkot

Ashkenazim read Kohelet on Sukkot, one of the Ashkenazi customs the Rabbi said he loves. The four other megillot have set times; the Sefaradim never fixed one for Kohelet. And Kohelet is perhaps the most depressing book in Tanakh: "Everything is futile." What is the point of all our labour? The sun rises and sets, and we live and die. Shelomoh tried wine and entertainment and found no meaning in them.

Why read it on the festival of joy? One answer given is that we might become too happy, so it reminds us of death. But the Rabbi offered another approach, in which Kohelet actually leads to happiness.

Sukkot is built on things that die

  • The sekhakh: the walls of a sukkah can be anything; the sukkah is named after its roof. The sekhakh must be made from the leftovers of the threshing floor and the winepress, things that wither. Today's bamboo lasts for years, but originally sekhakh was leaves that dried out within weeks.
  • The four species: who has reached Hoshana Rabbah with aravot that still have their leaves? The hadassim last a little longer, but the etrog and lulav also wither.
  • The season: autumn, when leaves fall and days darken. He compared the old European folklore of the Grim Reaper with his sickle at the end of autumn, reaping the harvest, and noted that sekhakh is made precisely from what remains after the reaping.

Laughing and crying at Rabbi Yohanan's beauty

The Gemara describes Rabbi Yohanan as extraordinarily handsome. When he was ill, a sage who visited him burst out crying and smiling at the same time. "I am crying because your beautiful face will one day be buried. And I am laughing because now I can enjoy it."

The Rabbi drew out the depth of this. Every moment we live, we are also dying. "These last 35 minutes you've been listening to me, you're never going to get back." That can make you depressed. But it can also make you very happy, because it tells you every moment is precious. People struggle to be happy because they are always waiting for the next thing: "I'll be happy when..."

His proof: imagine we were guaranteed to live 10,000 years in good health. Would tomorrow mean as much? No. "What gives us the beauty of life is that we're going to die." Because each day passes, we cherish it.

So on Sukkot we take things that are dying and lift them up to Hashem: through death we recognise life. That is why Kohelet belongs on Sukkot. It is depressing, and at the same time it makes you happy, because if life is passing, enjoy it now.

Living in the present, with a destination

He added a caveat: "live in the present and don't think about the future" is also wrong. Think of the festivals: Pesah commemorates Egypt, Purim Persia, Hanukah the Land of Israel. Sukkot is the only one that commemorates being in no man's land, travelling through the desert, neither in exile nor in Jerusalem. The people lived in the present, but they were travelling towards a destination. The greatest happiness is to enjoy the present moment while moving towards a goal.

He closed with a short exercise: stop and feel what is around you, the chair you are sitting on, your back against it. These moments we take for granted are passing, and noticing them can bring the greatest simhah.

Summary

  • Sukkot falls in autumn: for the Rosh's school, to show we sit in the sukkah for the misvah; for the Rambam, because it is the most pleasant season. Each answer reflects its author's world.
  • Happiness is not given; it must be pursued and worked on.
  • Sukkot is full of things that wither: the sekhakh, the four species and the autumn season.
  • Like the sage who laughed and cried at Rabbi Yohanan's beauty, knowing that each moment passes is what makes it precious.
  • The greatest joy is to cherish the present while travelling towards a goal, as our ancestors did in the desert.
