Before Shavuot: Why We Read Ruth

There are many reasons for the old custom, Sephardi and Ashkenazi alike, of reading Megillat Ruth on Shavuot. One fits this series exactly.

On Shavuot we think of Sinai and the written Torah, and there's a danger of thinking that's all Torah is. No book shows the authority of the Sages better than Ruth.

לא יבא עמוני ומואבי בקהל ה׳… עד עולם. An Ammonite or Moabite may not marry into Israel, even after conversion. (Today we can't identify anyone, so all converts may marry; in theory a traceable male Moabite couldn't.)

So David was taunted by Do'eg, a Sage of his time, that his lineage was flawed: his great-grandmother Ruth was a Moabite. Only the Sages' oral tradition saves him: מואבי ולא מואבית, a male Moabite, not a female, though the Torah's wording would normally include both.

Without the Oral Torah, Ruth wouldn't be someone we respect, and David couldn't be king. So as we celebrate receiving the written Torah, we read Ruth to remember that the Oral Torah is an essential part of it.

Recap: Parts 1–3

  • Everything taught orally to Moshe was taught so that every Jew heard it at least four times; Moshe reviewed it all before he died.
  • Each generation derived new laws and passed them on with what came from Moshe. Differences arose, but mostly there was consensus.
  • A prophet has no authority in interpretation: 1,000 prophets, 1,001 Sages, the Sages win. לא בשמים היא: everyone has an equal right to derive, provided the method is right, and we follow the majority. His one power: a temporary suspension of a law, never involving idolatry.
  • Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, "Rebbi," the teacher, third century, gathered it all into the Mishnah, in five categories:

Tonight: A Tour of the Whole Mishnah

"I don't think anyone did it before him, and I don't think anyone has since. Anyone who's studied Mishnah will appreciate how beautifully he puts it."

People learn Mishnah and Gemara their whole lives and never ask:

- Why does Seder Nashim put divorce (Gittin) before marriage (Kiddushin)?

- Why is Avot, ethics, in Nezikin, damages?

- Why does Moed go Shabbat → Pesach → Yom Kippur → Sukkot → Rosh Hashanah?

"For the Rambam, before you can learn Mishnah and Gemara you need a framework. He gives you the framework."

Moed is better translated "times": מועד is an appointment. Shabbat and the festivals are appointments with Hashem. Rebbi called each division a סדר, an "order" or structure.

Inside each order: orders → tractates (מסכתא) → chapters (פרק) → individual laws, which Rebbi called הלכה. "We call each one a mishnah; the Yerushalmi calls it a halachah."

Order 1: Zera'im (11 tractates)

Why open with Berachot? "The best doctor, wanting to keep a healthy person healthy, starts with diet. You can take the best medications and spend hours in the gym, but without a good diet it's worthless." So Rebbi, "Hashem being with him," began with blessings: you can't eat without first making a blessing.

Berachot also covers blessings on mitzvot, to make the subject complete, and Keriat Shema, the only non-blessing in it, because you can't discuss the blessings of the Shema without the Shema itself. That's why it opens מאימתי קורין את שמע.

Order 3: Nashim (7 tractates)

Why open with Yevamot, a rare case?

Yibbum: if a man dies childless, his brother must marry the widow or release her through chalitzah. The Torah creates a bond (zikah) that can't just be left; otherwise she could never remarry.

"Does this still happen?" "100%. It happens in Israel. It's never happened in our beit din yet; it's one of the things I'd want to do one day." Recently married soldiers who fell: their brothers must do yibbum or chalitzah. What if there's no brother? "Then she's free."

The class tried: it's earlier in the Torah? No: yibbum is in Ki Teitzei, near the end.

The answer: it's the only compulsory one. "Marriage is a mitzvah, but at the end of the day you choose. Yibbum: once it happens, it must be done." פתח בדבר המחויב: begin with the obligatory.

"Oh wow, I've just realised something." In our printed Shas the order is Nedarim, Nazir, Sotah, Gittin, Kiddushin, but the Rambam's has Gittin before Sotah. "I have to research this."

Why does Kiddushin, betrothal, come last?

Ketubot first, fine: like Yevamot it's marriage, not betrothal. But why after divorce?

"The only answer I have": it follows the verse. Ki Teitzei: כי יקח איש אשה… וכתב לה ספר כריתת… ויצאה מביתו והלכה והיתה לאיש אחר. Kiddushin is learned from והיתה לאיש אחר, which comes after the get, and the Sages compare הויה ליציאה, marrying to leaving.

A later commentator's reason (not the Rambam's), which the Rabbi "finds funny": מקדים רפואה למכה, Hashem sends the cure before the illness. "Marriage can be beautiful, but it can also be a disaster. People wouldn't marry if they knew they couldn't get out. So put the divorce first: don't worry, you can get divorced."

(Kiddushin = betrothal / engagement, the first stage; Ketubot = marriage itself.)

Is Makkot its own tractate? The Rambam says yes. One opinion in Tosafot: it's a continuation of Sanhedrin. That matters for a siyum, in the Nine Days to eat meat, or for firstborns on Erev Pesach: on that view, finishing Makkot isn't finishing a tractate.

Summary

  1. Ruth on Shavuot: מואבי ולא מואבית. Without the Oral Torah, no David.
  2. Five categories in the Mishnah: received with/without support (never disputed); derived, fences, takkanot (disputable).
  3. Six orders: Seeds (food comes first) → Times → Women → Damages (all following Mishpatim) → Holy things → Purities (Vayikra). Then tractate → chapter → halachah.
  4. Zera'im opens with Berachot ("the best doctor starts with diet"), then follows the gifts in the order they arise, from Pe'ah standing in the field to Challah in the dough, then fruit.
  5. Mo'ed: Shabbat; Pesach (first mitzvah); Torah order (Shekalim, Yom Kippur); remaining festivals; prophetic fasts, then Megillah; Chagigah last, as it's men only.
  6. Nashim: Yevamot first, the one compulsory case; Kiddushin last because the verse puts the get first (or: the cure before the illness).
  7. Nezikin: remove the hazards first (Bava Kamma); then the judges and their tools; Avodah Zarah so judges know idolatry precisely.
