Topic: Revenge, forgiveness and sibling rivalry, through the end of the story of Yosef and his brothers Source: Bereshit 37-50, especially 50:15-21; the Rambam on the mezuzah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Holland Park, Monday 9 January 2023, just after the reading of Parashat Vayehi

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A book of quarrelling brothers

The class had just finished reading the book of Bereshit, and the Rabbi opened with Rabbi Sacks' description of it as the book of dysfunctional families. From the beginning of the Torah to the end, siblings fight. Kayin kills Hevel. Yishmael is sent away from the home of Avraham, and the brothers only come together again to bury their father. Esav wants to kill Yaakov, who runs away for twenty-two years. Rahel and Leah, two sisters, are also in competition. Then Yosef's brothers are willing to kill him and end up selling him.

Only at the very end of the book do we meet two brothers who get on: Efrayim and Menasheh. Yaakov puts his right hand on the younger, Efrayim, and Yosef protests. Has Yaakov learned nothing from favouring one son? The Rabbi's reading was that this time it was done openly and the brothers accepted it. That, he said, is why we bless our sons on Friday night with the names of Efrayim and Menasheh.

He paused on the verses about Yaakov's marriage. The Torah says Yaakov loved Rahel "more than Leah", and in the very next verse that God saw Leah was "hated". He loved both, yet the one who receives less love feels hated. "Even if you love them all equally, if you love one more than another... they will always feel it." That is what Yosef's brothers felt.

Yosef's last test

Yosef rises from slavery to become second to Pharaoh, accuses his brothers of spying, and after twenty-two years reveals himself. He forgives them, brings the family down to Egypt, and for seventeen years they live together.

Then Yaakov dies. On the way back from the burial the brothers send a message: before he died, our father told us to tell you to forgive us. The Rabbi noted that the Gemara learns from here that one may change the truth for the sake of peace. They were afraid that Yosef had only been holding back out of respect for their father, just as Esav had said he would kill Yaakov once Yishak died.

He asked the class to picture it. You are the youngest of a large family. Your older brothers mock you as a dreamer (the verses say so explicitly), then gang up on you and send you to another country. Now you are the most powerful man in the world and they offer to be your slaves. Most of us, he suggested, would at least say: fine, but you will owe me for the rest of your lives. Yosef instead says that God sent him ahead to save lives, and that what they meant for harm was turned to good.

Why we keep relationships in balance

To understand how hard this is, the Rabbi described how we try to keep every relationship fair and level. If someone invites you for Shabbat, you invite them back. If you cannot, you bring a gift. If you cannot do that, you at least say thank you, and saying it aloud matters: "If you don't say it, you won't be invited back again."

The Hebrew word for thanks, todah, shares its root with viduy, confession. In both, a person expresses what he owes, and that restores the balance.

When someone hurts us, the balance is broken the other way. The natural response is revenge, which pushes the other person down to our level again. That is why revenge is "sweet", and also why it escalates, each side pushing the other further down. Revenge is forbidden by the Torah. A sincere apology can rebalance things, but what if the other person will never apologise?

How do you forgive?

He put the question to the class. People suggested trying to understand what made the other person act that way, cutting off from them, giving them time, working on oneself, and accepting that there is a bigger plan. The Rabbi said cutting off is not forgiving: it only stops the hurt, and you are still angry when you see them.

He then described two ways of responding to real pain, even to abuse or trauma. One is to live as a victim: why me, why did he do this to me, replaying it over and over. The other asks: this has happened to me, what can I now do with it? He contrasted a blame culture of rights and grievances with a culture that asks what I can learn and improve.

Two responses, two world views

The Rabbi said the difference between these responses is the difference between monotheism and dualism. We say Shema Yisrael twice a day, at a brit milah, at a wedding and on the deathbed. Surely nobody here believes in two gods? But Yihud Hashem, the unity of God, means that nothing happens in my life without His will and without some purpose. "We believe that in Judaism. Do we live our lives like that? No, we don't, let's be honest."

He asked why we put up a mezuzah. People answered tradition, protection, and a reminder. He quoted the Rambam: a person who puts up a mezuzah for protection gets no protection from it. Its purpose is that every time we go in and out we meet the Shema and remember that God is one. You go out into a harsh world where things go wrong, and you come home tired, and the mezuzah reminds you that what happens has meaning.

He added two cautions. There are aspects of life that are random, since God's providence does not extend equally to everything, a subject for another time. And the question of why bad things happen to good people is a separate discussion. He was speaking about human relationships.

This, he said, was Yosef's secret. He did not ask "why did they do this to me?" but "what can I make of where I am?", in Potifar's house and in prison. Someone who lives that way finds it easy to forgive, because it really did turn out for the good. He quoted a line he attributed to Mark Twain, that the greatest revenge is forgiveness: if what you did to me genuinely did not bring me down, it failed. But if you forgive in order to win, that defeats the purpose.

Someone asked how one could forgive an abuser. The Rabbi said he was not telling anyone they must. But instead of wallowing, a person can ask what to do with the experience, perhaps to protect others, and that in time may lead to a fuller forgiveness. As one participant put it, it can be "more forgiving the world than forgiving the individual". He admitted, "I can give a shiur about this, and then at the end of the day when things happen it's difficult."

Asked whether Yosef should have helped his brothers improve spiritually, he quoted the Baal Shem Tov: in your own spiritual life, take charge; in your own material life, trust in God; for other people's material needs, act as if there is no God and it is up to you; and their spiritual life is between them and God. He added that the testing of the brothers with Binyamin and the goblet was Yosef's way of seeing whether they had changed.

The seven tears of Yosef

He ended with a question: who cries most in the Torah? Yosef, seven times. He cries when he hears the brothers admit their guilt; when he sees Binyamin; before revealing himself; on his brothers' necks; on meeting Yaakov; when Yaakov dies; and finally, "Yosef wept when they spoke to him", at the brothers' message after the burial.

The first six are tears of longing, love, reunion and bereavement. Why the last? The Rabbi suggested that Yosef was more hurt than the brothers. He thought they had truly made up seventeen years earlier. Now he saw they still did not trust him. Power creates a barrier: people in high positions always wonder whether they are loved for themselves or for what they can give. Yosef had forgiven, but they had not moved on. "Sometimes those people are the people who need most the recognition of who they are as people."

Summary

  • Bereshit is a book of sibling rivalry; only Efrayim and Menasheh finally get on, and we bless our sons with their names.
  • We keep relationships balanced through reciprocity, gifts and thanks; revenge tries to rebalance a hurt and only escalates.
  • The victim asks "why me?"; the believer asks "what now?". The Rabbi linked this to Yihud Hashem, which the Shema and the mezuzah keep before us.
  • Yosef could forgive because he saw purpose in what happened; his last tears show that even the powerful need to be trusted for who they are.
