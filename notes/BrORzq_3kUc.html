Topic: The historical background to the Meiri's responsum against the ban on philosophy and science, Provence and Catalonia, 1232 to 1305 Source: The Meiri's teshuvah to Abba Mari of Lunel; the ban of the Rashba, July 1305 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom for the Sephardic Brotherhood of America, Tuesday 6 April 2021, the first of two parts

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The question and the Meiri

This was the Rabbi's first lecture for the Sephardic Brotherhood of America, given in partnership with the Spanish and Portuguese community in the UK. Over two weeks he planned to study a teshuvah of the Meiri. This first session gave the history; the text itself was left for the following week.

The question running through it all: is there a contradiction between being well versed in the science and philosophy of one's time and being a pious, believing Jew?

The Meiri, Rabbi Menahem ben Shlomo, was born in 1249 and completed his great commentary on the Talmud, the Beit HaBehirah, written in the style of the Rambam, in 1300, at the age of fifty-one. He asked his listeners to watch for four themes in the teshuvah:

  1. When does one community have jurisdiction over another?
  2. Do bans and excommunications achieve anything?
  3. When may the Torah be read as allegory, and how far?
  4. Should religious Jews pursue philosophy?

From Andalusia to Languedoc

Jews in Muslim lands came to know Greek philosophy as it had been passed on to the Arabs. Already in the tenth century, Rav Saadiah Gaon's Emunot VeDeot drew on the Islamic Kalam. The trend continued with Rav Avraham ibn Ezra, Rav Avraham ibn Daud (the first Raavad, author of Emunah Ramah, not the Raavad who argued with the Rambam) and on to the Rambam. Not every hakham of Muslim Spain went this way: Rabbenu Bahya ibn Pakuda in Hovot HaLevavot and Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi in the Kuzari sought God by other paths, but the lecture followed the philosophical line.

Sharing a map on screen, the Rabbi showed the regions that matter. In 1147 radical Muslims from North Africa persecuted the Jews of Andalusia in southern Spain. Some went to Christian northern Spain, but a significant group reached Languedoc in the south of France, whose Jews had no Arabic and had never met this learning.

The hakhamim of Languedoc were intellectually open. They commissioned translations into Hebrew, and five generations of the Ibn Tibbon family did the work: first Rav Saadiah, Rabbenu Bahya, Rav Shlomo ibn Gabirol and the Rambam (Rav Shmuel ibn Tibbon translated the Moreh Nevuchim in 1204), then mathematics, medicine, physics and astronomy. By the end of the thirteenth century Languedoc was a hub of philosophical and scientific inquiry.

Just across the border, Catalonia, with Barcelona, belonged to the Kingdom of Aragon. There, with the Ramban and later the Rashba, Kabbalah was becoming central. "Two countries right next to each other sharing very different philosophies", and that, he said, is the crux of the story.

The first controversy, around 1232

Once the Moreh Nevuchim appeared in Hebrew, unease grew. Rabbi Shlomo ben Avraham of Montpellier, together with Rabbenu Yonah of Gerona, persuaded the rabbis of northern France to ban all the Rambam's works. Their complaints were threefold: the Rambam's followers were rationalists who explained everything, even prophecy, naturally; they allegorised the Torah and denied the historical truth of its stories; and they were lax in keeping misvot.

Today, the Rabbi said, this is hard to imagine: a yeshiva student who has not opened the Mishneh Torah has not really experienced yeshiva. The hakhamim of Languedoc were furious, and they put Rabbi Shlomo of Montpellier himself under a ban. The Radak of Narbonne was among the Rambam's strongest defenders. The affair ended very badly, with the public burning of the Rambam's books, "a very, very sad time in our history." After that the Rambam regained acceptance.

The second controversy, 1303 to 1305

By the end of the thirteenth century the Rambam himself was no longer in question. The problem was his followers. And this time nobody sought to ban philosophy altogether. Even those who wanted a ban accepted philosophy and science as valid, but wanted to restrict them for the young, who lacked the maturity to face their challenges. They also objected to extreme allegory, alleged denial of creation and miracles, and the idea of a synthesis between Torah and philosophy.

The leading figure was Abba Mari of Lunel, also called Astruc HaYarhi, from yareah, the moon, after Lunel. Surprisingly, he was a philosopher who loved the Rambam and saw the Moreh Nevuchim as the authoritative statement of the Torah's inner meaning, identifying Maaseh Merkavah with philosophy and Maaseh Bereshit with the sciences, as the Rambam did. But he was a moderate who felt others had gone too far. He later gathered the 127 letters of the controversy in a book, Minhat Kenaot.

He did not turn to the rabbis of Montpellier or Narbonne, the Rabbi asked, but to the Rashba in Barcelona. Why? Because he knew that nobody in Languedoc, after 150 years of this learning, would support his proposal to ban the study of philosophy and science under the age of thirty. So he looked for a sympathetic ear elsewhere.

It was a strange alliance. For the Rashba, Maaseh Merkavah meant Kabbalah, not philosophy. When Abba Mari wrote in shock that people were preaching the secrets of the Merkavah in public, the Rashba replied that he did not think they were revealing any secrets, since philosophy is not the secret of the Torah. "When you have a goal, you will often join forces with those who share very different views."

Among the accusations, one misvah came up again and again: tefillin. Abba Mari claimed some philosophers held that one need only think about the message of tefillin, not wear them. "Whether this is true or not, I don't know."

The Rashba's ban and the counter-bans

At first the Rashba said it was beyond his jurisdiction. He sent a letter asking the hakhamim of Montpellier to ban Greek and Arabic philosophy until thirty, which Abba Mari read out in the synagogues of Languedoc to little effect. When Kalonymus ben Todros of Narbonne agreed to support the ban, the Rashba became more involved.

In July 1305 the Rashba and thirty-three hakhamim of Catalonia issued a ban, for fifty years, on anyone under twenty-five studying the Greek and Arabic works of science and philosophy, in Arabic or in translation. He attached a call to Languedoc to do the same, and a ban on the alleged allegorists there. The Rosh, originally from Ashkenaz, stayed silent, since he held that philosophy is forbidden at any age and so could not endorse a ban implying it was permitted after twenty-five.

Languedoc was enraged that the Rashba was interfering. Its hakhamim, the Meiri among them, issued counter-bans: on anyone who, because of the Rashba's ban, kept his son from studying philosophy or science, and on Abba Mari for giving them a bad name abroad. Abba Mari replied by banning those who had banned him, invoking the Gemara's rule that one who bans another without cause is himself banned. Into this came the Meiri, the leading authority of Perpignan, with his teshuvah to Abba Mari, which the class would read the following week.

Summary

  • Andalusian Jews fleeing in 1147 brought Greek and Arabic philosophy to Languedoc, which became a centre of Torah, philosophy and science; neighbouring Catalonia became a centre of Kabbalah.
  • The first controversy, around 1232, sought to ban all of the Rambam's works and ended with their burning.
  • The second, in 1303 to 1305, did not oppose philosophy itself but sought to restrict it for the young, led by Abba Mari, a moderate Maimonidean who turned to the Rashba.
  • The Rashba's ban on study under twenty-five provoked counter-bans in Languedoc, setting the scene for the Meiri's teshuvah.
