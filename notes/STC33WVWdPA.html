A new series: Torah Fundamentals

This was the first of a four-part series called Torah Fundamentals, timed for the weeks when we read Beshalah, Yitro, Mishpatim and Terumah. The planned topics were: how much of the Torah was given at Har Sinai; the relationship between Har Sinai and the Oral Torah; reconciling Torah and science, including evolution; and literalism, meaning when we have licence to say a passage is metaphorical and when we do not. A source sheet was shared with those on Zoom.

The Rabbi began with the Rambam's eighth principle of faith: the entire Torah is from heaven, given to Moshe, who acted like a scribe. But how and when was it given?

He noted that "Torah" has three meanings: the five books; the whole of Tanakh; and all Torah learning, including Mishnah, Gemara, halakhah and Midrash. Tonight's subject was the five books.

The day of the revelation

Shemot 19 describes the arrival at Har Sinai on Rosh Hodesh Sivan and three days of preparation. Chapter 20 gives the Ten Commandments, on the sixth or seventh of Sivan. Then Parashat Mishpatim opens, "These are the judgments which you shall set before them," and three chapters of laws follow, covering everything from damages to property law.

In chapter 24 Moshe tells the people all the words of Hashem, they answer "we will do," and Moshe writes everything down, builds an altar and makes a covenant. When did this happen? The Rashbam, Ibn Ezra and Ramban all say the same thing, in disagreement with Rashi: the Torah here is in chronological order. On the very day of the revelation, after the Ten Commandments, Hashem gave Moshe the laws of Mishpatim, Moshe taught them to the people, and he wrote them down.

So on that one day, before the whole nation: - They heard the first two commandments directly from Hashem, and the other eight through Moshe. - Moshe received and relayed Parashat Mishpatim. - Moshe wrote the Torah from Bereshit until the end of Mishpatim.

"So it's a busy day," he remarked.

Three periods of forty days

Straight afterwards Hashem tells Moshe to come up the mountain the next day to receive "the tablets of stone, and the Torah and the misvah." The Gemara in Berakhot reads this as including Mikra, Mishnah, the Prophets and Writings, and Gemara, all given to Moshe at Sinai. Yet Moshe only begins to receive this the next day, and stays for forty days. This shows, the Rabbi said, that "given at Sinai" does not mean given on the day of the revelation. It means given in the setting of Sinai, over a period of time.

Then comes the golden calf. Working through the dates: - Moshe goes up the day after the revelation and comes down after forty days, on the seventeenth of Tammuz, and breaks the tablets. - The next day he goes up again for forty days to pray for atonement, returning on Rosh Hodesh Elul. - He carves new tablets and goes up a third time, coming down forty days later on Yom Kippur. That is why Yom Kippur is a day of forgiveness and atonement.

Only the first forty days were for learning; the second and third were for atonement. Someone asked whether Moshe forgot anything in the meantime. The Rabbi said no: the Gemara is clear he could not forget what he learnt then. The episode where Moshe forgets something, in Shemini, is later and is not about what he received in those forty days.

Teaching the people

So Moshe has learnt everything, but only begins to teach it about eighty days later. Rashi on Shemot 34:32, quoting the Midrash, describes the order of teaching: Moshe teaches Aharon, then Aharon's sons, then the elders, then all the people, and repeats it so that Aharon hears it four times, his sons three, the elders twice and the people once. That takes time.

Even then he is still receiving new instructions. The building of the Mishkan is commanded after Yom Kippur and continues until Rosh Hodesh Nisan, followed by the laws of the korbanot in Vayikra. The people leave Sinai on the twentieth of Iyar of the second year, eleven and a half months after the revelation. During that year Moshe was teaching what he had learnt.

Some laws were given even later. The last verse of Bemidbar says, "These are the misvot and the judgments which Hashem commanded... in the plains of Moav by the Jordan." The laws of inheritance for the daughters of Selofhad, and the rule about not marrying between tribes, only came when a question arose, long after Sinai.

What does "given at Sinai" mean?

The Rabbi offered three ways to understand it. 1. The Torah was given in the setting of Har Sinai over the course of that year, not in one day. 2. The revelation prepared the people to receive the Torah. He cited the Ramhal: the whole Torah was not given there; the revelation was a purification that made them ready. This answers a question from the Haggadah: if they had come to Sinai and not received the Torah, it would have been enough. The experience itself made them into a vessel. 3. Saadiah Gaon's approach: the whole Torah is contained in the Ten Commandments. The Rabbi added that even so, they were not given the tools to extract it that day, so it still had to be taught over time.

A participant asked about the principle that the Torah is not always in chronological order, and Rashi's view that the Mishkan was commanded before the golden calf. The Rabbi agreed that Rashi reads these chapters differently. But the principle is only used when you have to. According to the Rashbam, Ibn Ezra and Ramban, these chapters are in order.

Another participant suggested this is why we call Shavuot the time of the giving of the Torah, not the receiving. The Rabbi agreed: Shavuot is not just a commemoration. "It's actually a day when we begin the process of receiving the Torah again."

When was the rest written?

We know Bereshit to Mishpatim was written on the day of the revelation. For the rest, the Gemara in Gittin brings two views: - Rabbi Yohanan: the Torah was given scroll by scroll. After each episode the relevant section was written, and at the end they were joined. - Resh Lakish: the rest was written as one, at the end of the forty years, towards Moshe's death.

The last eight verses, which describe Moshe's death, are another debate: either Moshe wrote them himself, or Yehoshua wrote them.

Summary

  • On the day of the revelation the people heard the Ten Commandments and received Parashat Mishpatim, and Moshe wrote the Torah from Bereshit to the end of Mishpatim.
  • Moshe learnt the rest during his first forty days on the mountain; after the golden calf and two more periods of forty days, he only began teaching it after Yom Kippur.
  • Most of the Torah was taught over the year the people spent at Sinai, with some laws given later in the plains of Moav.
  • "Given at Sinai" can mean given in that setting over a year, or that the revelation made us ready to receive it, or, with Saadiah Gaon, that all of it is contained in the Ten Commandments.
  • Receiving the Torah is a process, not one day, as it was for Moshe himself.
