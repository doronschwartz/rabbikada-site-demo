Topic: Who may blow the shofar on behalf of others: children, the deaf, women, and the intention of blower and listener Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 589 (with a summary of 586-588) Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 22 September 2022, the last class of 5782

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The chapters in between

The Rabbi summarised the three simanim skipped since the last class:

  • 586: which shofar may be used. In short, the horn of any kosher, domesticated animal other than a cow. A ram's horn is best, but "if you're ever stuck", others may be used.
  • 587: blowing into a pit, not relevant today.
  • 588: the time for the shofar. The main point is that it must be blown by day, not at night.

The principle: only someone obligated can blow for others

Siman 589 has nine paragraphs, all built on one principle: whoever is not obligated in a misvah cannot fulfil it on behalf of others. Someone who has already heard the shofar may still blow for others, because the question is not whether he has already done it but whether he is, in principle, a person obligated in this misvah.

Heresh, shoteh and katan

In the language of Hazal a heresh is always a deaf-mute unless stated otherwise. Someone with no way to hear or speak, in their times, had no way to gain knowledge, so he was given the status of a shoteh. A person who is only deaf or only mute is a normal adult, obligated in all the misvot.

The exception is the shofar. Since the misvah is to hear, a person who is deaf but can speak is exempt from the shofar and cannot blow for others. "This again underscores what we discussed last week: the misvah is to hear the shofar, not to blow the shofar." A mute person who can hear is obligated, so he may blow for others while someone else says the berakhah.

The Rabbi added that for more than two hundred years poskim have written that deaf-mutes today, who have schools and education, are obligated in misvot like anyone else, since Hazal's reasoning no longer applies. He would still be wary of such a person blowing on behalf of others.

Hearing aids and implants

The Shulhan Arukh already mentions people who heard through a kind of trumpet held to the ear, and allows it. For modern hearing aids and implants, the poskim say to remove the device: if you can still hear something without it, even by coming close, you fulfil the misvah and can blow for others. If you hear nothing without it, you have the status of a deaf person. So people who wear hearing aids should ideally take them off before the shofar and come close enough to hear the pure sound.

The thirteen-year-old

Can a boy who became bar misvah on Shabbat blow on Rosh Hashanah? Rava's hazakah presumes that a thirteen-year-old has two hairs, the physical sign of adulthood. But the Gemara says this presumption only works for rabbinic misvot. So on the first day, when the shofar is from the Torah, a thirteen-year-old cannot blow for others unless signs of maturity are clearly visible. On the second day, which is rabbinic, he can. This is why he may be called to the Torah or lead the prayers, and why a young bar misvah should not read Parashat Zakhor for the community. From age twenty one may assume adulthood.

Women and the shofar

Women are exempt, since the shofar is a positive misvah bound by time. Women coming to hear the shofar is "a lovely thing", but it is a custom, not an obligation.

When a good practice becomes a vow

Someone asked about a woman who has heard the shofar every year and this year cannot go. The Rabbi explained a principle that applies to men too: a good practice done three times, or once with the intention of continuing, can have the status of a vow (neder).

  • Temporary suspension needs nothing. A woman with a new baby who plans to return when the children are older, or someone undergoing treatment who pauses a personal fast, is not abandoning the practice.
  • Permanent stopping requires hatarat nedarim before three people, with an opening (petah): "Had I known it would be so hard, I would not have taken it on."

The hatarat nedarim said on Erev Rosh Hashanah includes a declaration about the future, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph sometimes uses it as an extra reason to be lenient. But it only works if you understand what you are saying, and it only covers vows you do not remember. A specific vow you know about must be stated explicitly and needs a petah.

May a woman blow, and who says the berakhah?

The Shulhan Arukh says women may blow for themselves. Why would we think otherwise? Because the shofar is a musical instrument and mukseh on Yom Tov, and once the misvah is done one may not blow for fun. The Rabbi recalled seeing someone at a Rosh Hashanah gathering blowing a huge shofar while people danced, which is not allowed. The point is that even a misvah a woman is not obliged in is still a misvah, so the restriction does not apply.

A man may blow for women. On the berakhah:

Berakhah for women

Shulhan Arukh

Women do not say it, and no one says it for them

Rema (Ashkenazim)

Women may say it, but only for themselves: the woman says the berakhah and then the man blows

A man who has already fulfilled the misvah should not say the berakhah for women. If a man who has not yet heard the shofar is present, the blower may say it for all. Sefaradim follow the Shulhan Arukh, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph was very much against women saying the berakhah. However, Spanish and Portuguese women still say it, as did many Moroccan women and some in Iraq. His guidance: if you do not know your custom, do not say the berakhah, and once a family has stopped, it does not go back.

Unusual cases

  • Androginus and tumtum. An androginus (with both male and female features) may blow for another androginus, but not for a man. A tumtum, whose sex is hidden, cannot blow even for another tumtum, since one may be male and the other female. The Sis Eliezer (Rav Eliezer Waldenberg) wrote that someone born with both may have their sex determined by surgery and hormones; the Rabbi stressed that this teshuvah is speaking about such a case.
  • Half slave, half free. Someone owned by two partners, one of whom freed him, cannot blow for himself and must hear it from someone else.
  • A vow not to benefit from someone. If Reuven vowed not to benefit from Shimon and Shimon blows for the community, Reuven fulfils the misvah, because "misvot were not given for enjoyment". But if Reuven asks Shimon to blow specifically for him, it does not work.

Intention of the blower and the listener

  • Someone practising does not fulfil the misvah, and neither does anyone who happens to hear him. The Rabbi practises at home before Rosh Hashanah, and if he then missed shul he would need to hear it again.
  • Blowing as music does not count.
  • Both must have intention: the blower to fulfil the misvah for those listening, and the listener to fulfil it.
  • In shul the default (stam) is that the blower intends anyone who hears and the listener intends to fulfil the misvah. Someone walking past the shul or sitting at home needs specific intention. If someone is blowing at home for an ill person, a passer-by does not fulfil the misvah, since the blower had in mind only the person in front of him.

Practical summary

  • Only someone obligated in the shofar can blow for others. A deaf person cannot; a mute person who hears can.
  • Hearing aids should ideally be removed for the shofar and the listener should come close. If he cannot hear anything without them, he is treated as deaf.
  • A newly bar misvah boy should not blow for others on the first day unless signs of maturity are visible; on the second day he may.
  • Women are exempt but may hear and blow. Sefaradim follow the Shulhan Arukh that women do not say the berakhah; Spanish and Portuguese and some Moroccan families do. If unsure, do not say it.
  • A good practice kept regularly may need hatarat nedarim to stop permanently, but not to pause it.
  • In shul, intention is assumed; outside shul you must intend to fulfil the misvah, and a home blowing does not cover passers-by.
