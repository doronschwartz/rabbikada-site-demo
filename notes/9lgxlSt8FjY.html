A winter on the 13 principles

After the break for the Hagim, the Rabbi opened a new series. The previous years had been spent on Shabbat and then kashrut. This winter, until Pesah, would be given to the Rambam's 13 principles. "I really feel that these principles aren't given enough attention." Some of what they would learn might be controversial, he warned, but everything would be sourced in the texts.

He set out five parts for the evening: who the Rambam was; the importance of belief; what the principles are; the Ani Maamin, the Yigdal and the meaning of emunah; and why the principles matter.

Who was the Rambam?

"From Moshe to Moshe there arose none like Moshe," people said soon after his death. What made Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon unique was his breadth. Other great figures excelled in one field: halacha, commentary, philosophy or derash. The Rambam was all of these: - The Mishneh Torah, the first systematic code of the whole Talmud. The Rabbi explained why this mattered: the Gemara is scattered, and you cannot decide halacha from one line, because another passage elsewhere may say something else. The Tur and the Shulhan Arukh built on it. - The Moreh Nevuchim, a work of philosophy still studied in universities today. - The Commentary on the Mishnah. - A physician. He taught that rabbis should not take salaries, and lived by it. In a letter to Shemuel ibn Tibbon he describes his day: early to the Sultan's palace, then home to long queues of Jews and non-Jews waiting for medical advice until night. Only on Shabbat could he teach the community.

His life: born in Cordoba in 1135; fled the Almohads at about 13 to Fez; then to the Land of Israel in 1165; then to Egypt, where by about 33 he led the community of Cairo. He received questions from Yemen to Syria. The Rabbi mentioned the tradition that King Richard the Lionheart invited him to be his physician, at the very time of the massacres of Jews in York and Lincoln. He died at 69 and, according to most opinions, was buried in Teveria.

What also stands out is his order and rationality: everything has a beginning, a structure and an end, and "don't just accept things because you're told". The Rabbi compared him in this to the Ramhal. And every camp in Judaism claims him, from the yeshiva world to Modern Orthodoxy, because he speaks to people at every level.

For the Rambam, belief comes first

The Rambam was the first to set out the Jewish beliefs as a system. The Talmud assumes them but never lists them.

The Rabbi suggested that for the Rambam belief mattered even more than practice, and showed it with a "spot the difference". The Talmud (Yevamot 47) describes how a convert is accepted: we teach him some of the light and some of the stringent misvot. The Rambam (Hilchot Issurei Biah 14) adds something not in the Talmud: "we inform him of the fundamentals of the faith, the unity of God and the prohibition of idolatry." For the Rambam a person cannot fully join the Jewish people without the right beliefs.

This connects to his view of the World to Come. Most Rishonim held that we will have refined bodies there. The Rambam, influenced by Aristotle, held it is pure souls, because a person's identity is his intellect and understanding.

The principles, and three groups

The Rambam does not call them ikkarim but yesodot, foundations: "You build a house without a solid foundation, the house collapses." The Rabbi previewed each one and the questions it raises, from "can God create a stone He cannot lift?" to the 1705 controversy at Bevis Marks, when Hakham David Nieto was accused of denying the unity of God.

He gave the structure that would run through the series: - Principles 1-5: God Himself. - Principles 6-9: revelation, God's instructions for the world He made. - Principles 10-13: the consequences of keeping those instructions or not.

"It makes sense... a creator would have given instructions, and then you'd expect consequences."

Not everyone accepted the number 13. Rabbi Yoseph Albo counted three roots: God, Torah from heaven, and reward and punishment. The Radbaz asked a sharper question: if the whole Torah is true, how can some parts be "principles" and others not?

Why these 13 are different

The Rabbi gave three answers: 1. The Radbaz himself: the whole Torah is true, but some parts are more fundamental than others. 2. Ignorance is no excuse. The Rambam calls even an observant Jew who imagines God with a body a heretic. The Raavad protests that "greater and better men" held this mistaken belief. The answer: for the rest of the Torah, it is enough not to deny it. These 13 one must actually know. 3. They make you part of the community. The Rambam ends his essay: whoever holds these principles belongs to the people of Israel, and we must love him even if he sins. Whoever denies one has left the community.

But "once a Jew, always a Jew"? The Talmud says, "Even though he sinned, he is Israel." The Rabbi distinguished lineage, which can never be lost, from belonging to the community, which depends on belief. His analogy: a British-born citizen remains a citizen whatever he does, but may not share the values that make him part of British society.

The Rambam ends his essay by urging the reader to read it again and again, "ten times" if need be.

Emunah: from belief to knowledge

The Yigdal, from the early 14th century, puts the principles into poetry. The Ani Maamin, by an unknown later author, condenses them into short prose lines. Both say "with complete faith": not a hedged bet. The Rabbi rejected Pascal's wager. Eliyahu on Mount Carmel told the people to stop hopping between two opinions.

Emunah means loyalty and steadfastness, as with Avraham, "and You found his heart faithful". Its highest form is knowledge. None of us has been to a distant country we have only heard of, yet we do not "believe" it exists; we know it. That is emunah shelemah.

He illustrated this with a responsum of Rav Moshe Feinstein. Religious parents asked whether they could eat at the home of their non-observant son, who promised everything would be kosher. Normally such a person cannot be relied on as a witness. But if the parents are certain their son would never deceive them, they may eat there, because this is no longer reliance on testimony; it is knowledge. Today, he noted, the question is often the other way round.

"Emunah is not blind faith." It is not "I believe because it is absurd". It is something that becomes part of your bones.

Questions

Someone asked about misvot whose reasons we do not know. For the Rambam, the Rabbi said, there is no misvah without a reason; a hok is one whose reason we do not yet understand. Another asked why, if the principles are so important, the Talmud does not list them. Because the Talmud is organised that way for everything, Shabbat included; it was the Rambam who brought order. And how does one reach this knowledge? Through thought and study over a lifetime. "These things take time... the person really feels Hashem in his bones."

Summary

  • The Rambam combined halacha, philosophy, commentary and medicine, and brought order and reason to all of them.
  • He was the first to set out Jewish belief as a system, and for him belief is at least as vital as practice.
  • The 13 principles fall into three groups: God, revelation, and the consequences of our deeds.
  • Unlike the rest of the Torah, these must be known, not only not denied; they define belonging to the community.
  • Emunah means loyalty, and at its height it becomes knowledge.
