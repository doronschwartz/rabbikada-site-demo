Kli rishon and pouring from it

The class was introduced as "Spicing Up the Hamin". The Rabbi had been asked to stay away from Sefaradi and Ashkenazi divides; he would try, he said, but in Hilchot Shabbat it is hard to find laws that are unanimous.

Cooking on Shabbat is not only on the fire. A kli rishon, the first utensil, is a pot or urn that was heated on the fire. Even after it is taken off or switched off, while it is still hot it can cook. Putting something raw into it is a Torah prohibition of cooking. That is straightforward.

The next level is irui kli rishon, pouring from a kli rishon. May I put a teabag in a cup and pour onto it straight from the urn? This is a major dispute among the Rishonim, which comes up in several places in the Talmud. Rabbenu Tam, Rashi's grandson, held that pouring has the status of a kli rishon and cooks. The other view, which Tosafot cite in the name of the Rashba (the Rabbi noted this is an earlier Rishon, not the famous Rashba of Barcelona), held that the Torah forbade only putting food into the kli rishon itself.

The Shulhan Arukh rules that one may not pour hot water from a kli rishon onto spices in a plate. All the later authorities, Sefaradi and Ashkenazi, accept this. So pouring from the urn onto your teabag is forbidden, most likely by the Torah.

Kli sheni: where the divide comes from

A kli sheni is the second utensil. You pour water from the urn into an empty cup. Now there is no stream: the water has landed. May you put tea leaves into that cup? The Rabbi wanted to show "the evolution of halacha" and how divides come about.

The Gemara says that something not cooked before Shabbat may be rinsed with hot water on Shabbat, but not soaked. On the Rashba's view this is simple: you may not soak it in a kli rishon, but you may pour from one. But Rabbenu Tam forbids pouring from a kli rishon. So how can the Gemara allow rinsing?

Tosafot answer that according to Rabbenu Tam the Gemara must mean pouring from a kli sheni. That would imply that you may pour from a kli sheni but not soak food in it. Yet the Gemara elsewhere says plainly that a kli sheni does not cook. Tosafot give two answers:

  1. By Torah law a kli sheni does not cook, but the Rabbis forbade putting raw food into it, because it looks like cooking.
  2. A second answer, which the Rabbi did not go into, avoids the problem altogether. On that answer putting food into a kli sheni is permitted.

So there is a double doubt (sfek sfeka). First, maybe pouring from a kli rishon does not cook at all, as the Rashba held. Second, even on Rabbenu Tam's view, Tosafot have two answers on a kli sheni. This is why it matters, he said, to know the lenient sources even where we do not follow them: they can join with other doubts later.

The first to rule like Tosafot's first answer was the Magen Avraham in the seventeenth century. The Mishnah Berurah follows him, which is why Ashkenazi practice today is to avoid putting raw food into a kli sheni. "But it's not so simple that was always the case."

Sefaradim, he said, never had a problem with a kli sheni, because of the double doubt. Rav Ovadia Yosef adds another reason: even on Tosafot's first answer the prohibition is only rabbinic, and in a doubt about a rabbinic law we are lenient. He adds that someone who wants to be stringent may be. But the halacha is that you may put even raw tea leaves into the cup of hot water poured from the urn. He mentioned that the Hazon Ish forbids even a third utensil in some cases when it is boiling hot, but left that aside.

Why not just measure the temperature?

Someone asked why we do not simply go by the heat. The halachic test is yad soledet bo, the heat at which a hand would recoil, today often put at about 45 degrees. Above it, forbidden; below it, permitted. Why count cups?

Tosafot explain that the walls of a kli rishon were heated on the fire, and they help the cooking. A kli sheni does not share those walls. So even if the water in the cup measures 90 degrees, it does not cook in the Torah's sense. The Rabbi's understanding was that the Torah forbade cooking the way it was done in the Mishkan, in a pot whose walls contributed to the cooking. Once that structure is gone, it is a different act. "It's nothing to do with temperature. It's got to do with the structure of the Melakhah."

Davar gush: the hot potato

The last idea is the davar gush, a solid item. The usual example is a potato. If I take a steaming potato out of the pot onto a plate, and then onto another plate, it is in a third utensil. May I now season it?

The Maharshal, in the sixteenth century, was the first to say no. Liquid loses its heat when it is poured from one vessel to another, but a solid holds its heat. So a solid stays forbidden while it is above yad soledet bo, however many times it has been moved. The Mishnah Berurah rules this way. That is why many Ashkenazim will not season hot kugel until it cools.

Sefaradim never had a problem with a davar gush. Going back to Tosafot's reasoning, once the food has left the pot it has lost the structure of the Melakhah of cooking. "We as Sefaradim appreciate the importance of hot food, enjoying your hot food on Shabbat."

So, can you spice your hamin?

  • While the hamin is on the hot plate, raw spices are forbidden by the Torah.
  • If the pot is taken off, it is still a kli rishon. Raw spices are still forbidden, according to everyone.
  • Serve the hamin onto your plate, and the plate is a kli sheni. For Sefaradim this is permitted. Even Ashkenazim who avoid a kli sheni, he said, make an exception for spices.
  • If the hamin is thick and solid, those who follow the Maharshal treat it as a davar gush and wait until it cools.

Do not give up hot food

Some people decide it is all too complicated and eat only cold food on Shabbat. The Rabbi recalled a candidate for conversion who told the Beit Din that she could not answer the questions on cooking because she simply stopped eating hot food on Shabbat. That is wrong, he said. It takes away from Shabbat. The early Rishonim even wrote that someone who refuses hot food on Shabbat morning should be suspected of heresy. The Karaites read "do not kindle fire in all your dwellings" to forbid even a fire left burning, so they ate everything cold.

Questions

Processed or dried spices? Processing or grinding does not help. What matters is whether the food has been through fire or cooking before. If it has, it counts as cooked and may go into anything except a kli rishon that is on the fire. If not, it is still raw. Whether drying in the sun counts is a question he left open. Instant coffee has been cooked; Turkish coffee is raw.

Cold tea essence? A liquid that has gone completely cold can be cooked again, unlike a solid. But tea essence helps even Ashkenazim: pour hot water from the urn into a cup, and then add the cold essence. Water is one thing everyone agrees a kli sheni does not cook.

Taking out a teabag? One condition for selecting (borer) on Shabbat is not using a utensil, and a teabag works like a filter. But taking it straight out to get rid of it is permitted by everyone, because the filtering is an unintended, inevitable result. Leaving it hanging over the cup to drip would be using it as a filter.

Practical summary

  • A kli rishon cooks even off the fire, and pouring from it is treated as cooking by Torah law.
  • Sefaradim may put raw food into a kli sheni. Ashkenazim who follow the Mishnah Berurah avoid it, except for spices and water.
  • Sefaradim may season a hot solid once it is out of the pot. Ashkenazim following the Maharshal wait until it cools below yad soledet bo.
  • Food that has been cooked before, like instant coffee, may go into a kli sheni for everyone.
  • Remove a teabag directly; do not let it hang and drip.
