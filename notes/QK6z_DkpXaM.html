Where we are

The Rabbi said Siman 9 is short but important, and he wanted to finish it in one shiur. It is not only halachic: it shows how Ashkenazi and Sephardi Rishonim approach the words of Hazal differently. Siman 8 was left for the next class.

The sugya

The Mishnah (Yevamot) says a woman may marry a second husband. The implication is that she may not marry a third: after two husbands have died, she is in a problem. The Gemara discusses a dispute:

  • Rebbi: a hazakah is established after two times.
  • Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel: only after three times.

Normally we rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that a hazakah needs three. But in matters of life and death, like brit milah, we rule like Rebbi, and the Gemara says so for this case too. So a woman whose two husbands have died is a Katlanit (muhzeket). The Rabbi noted that Abaye did marry a woman whose two husbands had died, so he evidently held like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel.

The two reasons.

Amora

Reason

Meaning

Rav Huna

Mayan gorem

Something in her body: relations with her cause the husbands' deaths

Rav Ashi

Mazal gorem

Her bad luck: it has nothing to do with relations

Two practical differences follow. If a woman was only betrothed and her husband died, there was no relations, so on Rav Huna's view there is no problem, but on Rav Ashi's view mazal already operates from the kiddushin. And if a husband died in an obvious accident (for example he fell from a tree), Rav Huna's reason cannot explain it, whereas bad luck can. Mazal gorem is therefore the broader and more prohibitive approach. We follow Rav Ashi, who is the later authority (basra), so even a husband's death after only kiddushin counts.

Rashi. Rashi extends the same dispute to divorce: a woman divorced twice or three times. The Rabbi said this is a lone opinion (shitah yehidah), that all others, including the Tosafot, reject it, and that this is not what the Shulhan Arukh rules. Death and divorce are never added together.

The Rambam's responsum: this is superstition

The Rabbi asked: suppose a man married a woman whose two husbands died, against what Hazal said. Must he divorce her? The Rosh says yes: whether the reason is relations or mazal, he is in trouble.

The Rambam says something the Rabbi called incredible. In a responsum (Peer HaDor, which the Rashba tried to explain) he says that not only is there no need to divorce, but even a woman who has been married to several husbands who died may marry again. The Rambam answers a questioner who compared this to brit milah: "Milah has a real risk, there is blood. How can you compare it to something that is only derekh nihush ve-kishuf (a way of omens and sorcery), which in certain eras weak-minded people accepted?" He says plainly that this is not even advisory, and there is no prohibition here. He adds mispar baalim (any number of husbands), so he was not limiting it to two, as some later tried to say.

What he did. The Rambam writes that Beit Din does not officiate at a third marriage, because it is not advisable. But once she has been betrothed in front of two witnesses, the Beit Din completes the process: it writes the ketubah and the Sheva Berakhot are said. He says this was his own practice in Egypt, and the practice of the Rif and the Ri Migash. Where there is yibbum (a misvah) the Beit Din does officiate. The Rabbi's description: officially we do not do it, we "look the other way", and then complete it.

How the Rambam reads Hazal

The Rabbi used this to show a larger difference. Many Ashkenazi Rishonim read Hazal literally: if Hazal said do not do something, it is a prohibition. The Rambam reads in context. He gives examples we have seen: the bavuah de-bavuah in Gittin (he understands an echo), and hatraah al yedei shed in Makkot (he explains it as a warning heard from behind a corner). In his letter on astrology he says Hazal spoke to the masses and that the Gemara is not monolithic: if one sage believed something, we need not accept it.

The Rabbi added his own honest remark: it seems likely that Hazal did mean these things, since the Gemara places mazal in the same breath as brit milah and other matters of life, and the Rambam could not accept this but also could not say Hazal erred. So he reinterpreted them. Another point of the Rabbi's own: the Rambam himself said he is doing a haramah (a workaround), but one does not do a haramah on a real Torah prohibition.

"I believe in my flow coming directly from HaKadosh Barukh Hu. So I don't have to worry about this."

The Rabbi cited this idea on the Gemara's "ein mazal leYisrael": a person who lives directly with Hashem is not under the influence of mazalot, but most people do not live that way, so Hazal speak to them as they are. He called this a beautiful idea.

Shulhan Arukh and Rema

The Shulhan Arukh quotes the Rambam: Ishah shenisset, a woman who married. It does not mention one who was only betrothed (nisrasah) and does not mention one whose husband died by accident. Seemingly it limits the din as much as it can. The Rema adds "or was betrothed", since the Rishonim rule like Rav Ashi, and says a woman with two husbands who died may not remarry (ishnei anashim lo tinasei).

The Rema then adds exceptions: if the husband was killed, or died in a plague (magefah) in the city, it does not count. The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel both ask a difficulty: the Rema mixes two views. His first ruling, that kiddushin alone counts, follows mazal gorem. But his exceptions for a clear cause of death (execution, plague) only make sense on mayan gorem. The Rabbi said someone stabbed or killed in a plague is not an example of bad luck. The Rema also quotes a yesh omerim about divorce, which is Rashi's view.

The Rosh on mazal. The Rosh explains that mazal means parnasah: a woman with bad mazal is one fated to poverty, and in those days a woman had no independent income. The Noda BiYehudah draws the conclusion that a wealthy, independent woman is not subject to this and may marry a third and fourth husband. The Rabbi said no one disagrees with this explanation, and he called it the accepted one.

Points from the Pithei Teshuvah and the commentators

  • Warned by Beit Din. The Rivash holds that even if Beit Din told him not to marry her and he did, he need not divorce her. Most Rishonim treat it as a danger (sakanah) and require divorce; the Beit Shemuel brings this at the end, and the Rabbi read it as leaning strict.
  • Asked a shailah. The Hatam Sofer, quoted in the Pithei Teshuvah, says that if he asked Beit Din and they gave a psak, he must divorce her, because asking shows he takes it seriously, and it is not just a rumour for him. The Rabbi called this a nice point, connecting it to Rav Moshe's idea that one who does not take such things seriously need not worry about them.
  • Two sisters. The Hida asks about a third sister when two sisters' husbands have died. A bodily (mayan) problem might run in a family, but we follow mazal, which does not, so she is not affected.
  • Zaken. A husband who died of old age does not count; the Pithei Teshuvah defines old age as 70 and above.
  • Yibbum. It is clear we are lenient where a yibbum is involved.
  • A plague. The Radbaz says a plague counts only if the majority of the city died.
  • A man whose wives died. No problem for a man: the Gemara sees this only as a problem for a woman. One view in the Helkat Mehokek says that if his wives died of the same illness he should be careful, but the Beit Shemuel rejects this: the problem is never with him.

The ketubah of a Katlanit

The Rabbi worked through who gets a ketubah, with questions from the class.

  • A man who knew she was a Katlanit and married her, as the Rambam permits, and lived with her, owes her the ketubah: he accepted her.
  • After the second husband's death: she gets the ketubah. We do not rule like Rebbi in monetary matters, so for the ketubah she is not yet a Katlanit.
  • After the third husband's death: if he knew, she gets the ketubah; if he did not know, she does not (it was a mekah taut). She tricked him.
  • The Helkat Mehokek asks that, on the Rambam's view, there is no mekah taut at all. The Beit Shemuel answers that the third husband's estate can say kim li ke-Rosh, and that she can in turn say kim li ke-Rambam if asked to return the ketubah of husbands one and two.

Practical summary

  • A woman whose two husbands have died is a Katlanit: she should not marry a third, following Rebbi, and Rav Ashi's mazal gorem means even a husband who died after kiddushin counts.
  • The Rambam and the Rosh differ in practice: the Rosh requires divorce if she married; the Rambam says nothing needs to be done and that Hazal's concern is only nihush.
  • The practice that follows from the Rambam is that Beit Din does not officiate at a third marriage but completes it after the kiddushin.
  • Old age, plague and obvious causes of death are not counted; a wealthy independent woman is not affected (Rosh and Noda BiYehudah).
  • For the ketubah, she keeps the second husband's, and the third husband's only if he knew.
