Topic: The melakhah of Kosher (tying): where it was in the Mishkan, and the dispute of the Rishonim on which knots are forbidden Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 317, seif 1, and the Gemara in Shabbat Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in his Monday-night series on the Melakhot, Monday 18 February 2019 (Kosher and Mattir, Part 1; the recording begins partway through)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where was tying in the Mishkan?

The recording opens in the middle of the Gemara's search for Kosher in the Mishkan. One suggestion is the tying of the curtains to the pegs, but that is not a permanent knot. Another is the weavers of the curtains, who tied a thread that tore; but then where is untying? The Gemara concludes: those who caught the hilazon tied and untied the knots of their nets.

The hilazon was needed for the tekhelet dye of the wool threads in the curtains of the Mishkan. The Rabbi noted that whether we have the hilazon today is a debate for another time. Rashi adds that the nets were sometimes untied to make a bigger or smaller net.

Did they really fish in the desert? Some say the 39 melakhot are what one would have to do to build the Mishkan, not necessarily what was actually done. But the Radbaz takes it literally. Bnei Yisrael were at Har Sinai for about a year, and building the Mishkan fell within that year, whether, as Rashi holds, it was commanded after the sin of the golden calf, or in the order of the parashiyot we were then reading. The Radbaz says Har Sinai is not far from the Red Sea, so they really went out with nets to catch the hilazon.

Where the laws are found

Unlike many melakhot, the laws of tying are not scattered. They are in one chapter of the Gemara and one siman of the Shulhan Arukh, 317. Almost the whole melakhah is in seif 1; the rest of the siman is mostly rabbinic details, apart from seif 5, which is important.

"What I'm going to say tonight is the introduction, but it's the foundation of all Melekhet Kosher." The Rabbi urged the class to understand it thoroughly, even to write it down, because they would return to it again and again over the coming month.

Three kinds of knot

The Gemara is clear that there are three kinds of knot: one forbidden by the Torah, one forbidden by the Hakhamim, and one completely permitted. The question is how to define them. The Rabbi first set out the two views, deliberately leaving the terms vague, before defining each.

The Rif and the Rambam. A knot is forbidden by the Torah only with two conditions together: - Maaseh uman, a professional, craftsman's knot. - Kesher shel kayyama, a permanent knot.

If only one is present, it is rabbinic: a craftsman's knot tied for ten seconds, or a simple knot tied permanently. If neither is present, a simple knot that is not permanent, it is permitted. Their proof is the Mishnah's examples, the knots of camel drivers and sailors, which are both permanent and made by experts.

Rashi and the Rosh. Professional or not makes no difference at all. The camel driver's and sailor's knots are mentioned only because such knots are normally permanent. What matters is time: - Permanent: forbidden by the Torah. - A long time, but not permanent: rabbinic. - A very short time: permitted.

The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rif and the Rambam. The Rema does not simply rule like Rashi and the Rosh; he adds their stringencies, so that Ashkenazim take on the strict side of both views.

Defining the terms

Professional. The Rabbi left this for later, since it depends on a ruling of the Rema. He said that a single knot is certainly not professional. Whether a double knot is, is debated, and "you've probably heard that a double knot is a professional knot, but you'll see it's not at all" so simple.

Permanent. Most Aharonim, and the Meiri and the Ritva, say kayyama means forever. One view brought by the Beit Yosef says half a year or a year counts as permanent, but the halacha follows the first view.

"Forever" does not mean you consciously want the knot to last forever. The Shulhan Arukh HaRav puts it well: it means you tie it with no intention of untying it, even if in the end circumstances force you to. When you tie the knots of new sisit, perhaps in a week the garment will tear and you will untie them to move them to a new one. But that is not your intention, so it is a permanent knot. The Gemara's nets show the same: they were sometimes untied, but they were still permanent knots.

A dispute within the Rambam

A craftsman's knot that is not permanent is rabbinic according to the Rambam, even for an hour. But what about a simple knot tied for a long time, say a month?

The simple reading. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, the Biur Halacha, the Shaar HaSiyun and the Mishnah Berurah all read the Rambam plainly: as long as it is not permanent and not professional, it is permitted. So a simple knot for two weeks is fine. Someone asked about ten years. The Rabbi answered that as long as it is not "forever", it is not permanent.

The stricter reading. The Shulhan Arukh HaRav reads the Rambam as forbidding a simple knot that has some permanence, more than about a week, because it comes close to a permanent knot. The Shulhan Arukh itself does not say, and the question is what "not permanent" means. The Rabbi showed that the Mishnah Berurah, in the seif katan and in the Shaar HaSiyun, clearly follows the first reading, which he said is the view of most Aharonim.

The practical differences

Putting it together, the two main views differ in at least two cases, perhaps three:

  1. A permanent simple knot. For the Rambam it is rabbinic; for Rashi and the Rosh it is a Torah prohibition. Here the Rambam is more lenient.
  2. A professional knot for a very short time. For the Rambam it is rabbinic; for Rashi and the Rosh it is permitted. Here the Rambam is stricter.
  3. A simple knot for a month. For Rashi it is rabbinic. For the Rambam it depends on the dispute above: permitted according to the Mishnah Berurah, forbidden according to the Shulhan Arukh HaRav.

Practical summary

  • Kosher in the Mishkan was the tying and untying of the nets used to catch the hilazon.
  • Untying is governed by the same rules as tying.
  • The Rif, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh: a Torah-level knot must be both permanent and professional; one of the two is rabbinic; neither is permitted.
  • Rashi and the Rosh: only time matters; permanent is Torah-level, a long time is rabbinic, a short time is permitted. The Rema adds their stringencies.
  • "Permanent" means tied with no intention of untying it, even if it may one day be untied.
  • On the simple reading of the Rambam, followed by the Mishnah Berurah, a simple knot that is not permanent is permitted even for weeks.
