Topic: Why the Torah bars a pesua dakka or kerut shofkhah from marrying a Jewish woman

Sources: Sefer HaHinukh; Megillat Esther; the Rambam, Moreh Nevuchim and the Eight Chapters; the Rishonim

Daily Halacha: Even HaEzer

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The halacha

  • Pesua dakka is an injury to the testicles, kerut shofkhah to the organ itself. The Rishonim give different reasons for the bar on marriage and the prohibition of castration.
  • First, Sefer HaHinukh: to deter deliberate castration. Kings castrated men to guard their women, as in Megillat Esther, where Ahashverosh's women were in the charge of Hegai, the king's eunuch; some men even underwent castration voluntarily for employment.
  • So the Torah forbids castration, since the purpose of life is to have children, and makes it socially and matrimonially undesirable by barring such a man from marrying a regular Jewish woman.
  • This explains why an injury from Heaven (bidei shamayim) does not bar marriage, while one by man (bidei adam) does. Not every barred case involves wrongdoing, for example a man who trips onto a thorn, which would probably count as bidei adam, but the general principle is deterrence.
  • Second, the Rambam in the Moreh compares it to brit milah. Milah restrains the reproductive drive without destroying it, the golden mean of Aristotle and of the Rambam's Eight Chapters. Castration suppresses it entirely, defeating the purpose, so such a man may not marry.
  • Third, simply: the purpose of marriage is to have children, so one who cannot may not marry a regular Jewish woman.

Sources quoted

  • Sefer HaHinukh: the law deters voluntary castration.
  • Megillat Esther: Hegai, the king's eunuch in charge of the women.
  • The Rambam, Moreh Nevuchim: milah weakens, castration destroys; the golden mean.

In one line

The bar is explained as deterring castration, as preserving the balance milah represents, or because marriage is for having children.
