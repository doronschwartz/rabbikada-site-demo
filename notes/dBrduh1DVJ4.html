Part A — Makeh Be'Patish (The Final Hammer Blow)

Completing an object

The last blow that gives a built object its finished form. Any finishing touch is forbidden:

  • Opening the stitched-shut pockets of a new suit.
  • Cutting open the uncut pages of a new book.
  • Threading shoelaces into new shoes for the first time (re-inserting laces that came out is fine).
  • Stuffing new feathers into a pillow (putting back feathers that came out is fine).
  • Inflating a new ball for the first time (re-inflating a deflated ball, even with a pump, is fine). Balloons — no, as you would come to tie them.
  • Breaking off a splinter from a detached branch to use as a toothpick — you've made a tool.

Repairing

Repairs are also Makeh Be'Patish — e.g. bending back a bent piece of cutlery, oiling a creaking door (WD-40). Q: why may laces and feathers be put back, but repairs not? — Repair is forbidden where it takes skill and effort; simply putting something back where it belongs takes none.

Q: Tying a new necktie? — Fine; the tie is already a finished item.

Toveling a utensil on Shabbat

Some say immersing a new food utensil in the mikveh "completes" it. Others: the utensil itself is finished — the need for tevilah is external, a spiritual status. The Shulchan Aruch permits toveling on Shabbat (where carrying is permitted). One who wishes to be stringent should do it before Shabbat; if forgotten, give the utensil to a non-Jew as a gift and borrow it back — a non-Jew's utensil needs no tevilah (this works indefinitely).

Three rabbinic extensions

  1. Folding clothes on their creases — in Chazal's time it improved the garment. Fold a tallit against its creases (not on them) — don't leave it lying around, which is disrespectful. Many have a custom to fold on the creases, since folding today is no professional act; the Shulchan Aruch does say not to, so only with an established custom. No difference whether the tallit is worn only on Shabbat.
  2. Spraying perfume on clothes — molid rei'ach, creating a new scent in the garment. On the body or hair is fine.
  3. Musical instruments — forbidden, lest one come to repair them. This underlay the 19th-century Reform–Orthodox controversy over a non-Jew playing an organ in shul (argued as "shevut deshevut for a mitzvah"); the Chatam Sofer and others opposed it strongly. The same applies to non-Jewish bands at simchas on Shabbat.

Dancing and clapping in rhythm: part of the music decree. The Rema permits (today we don't know how to repair instruments) — hence Ashkenazi practice. The Shulchan Aruch forbids — for Sephardim: no dancing (lifting the feet, as at a wedding) or clapping to a song. Clapping to wake someone or to quieten shul is fine; walking in a circle isn't dancing. On Simchat Torah dancing is permitted. If someone won't accept a well-meant rebuke on a rabbinic matter, the Rema says better to stay silent (mutav sheyihyu shogegin).

Part B — Mav'ir and Mechabeh (Kindling and Extinguishing)

Fire was used throughout the Mishkan (e.g. smelting gold). Driving a car — the engine combusts — is a Torah prohibition.

Swimming on Shabbat

Open water (sea, lake) — swimming (both feet off the ground) is forbidden by all: Chazal feared one would make a float or raft. Wading in the sea with your feet on the ground is fine — even up to the neck (not in clothes not made for water).

An enclosed pool — five possible issues:

  1. Squeezing hair — just don't; wrap a towel round it.
  2. Wringing the swimsuit — a swimsuit is made to get wet, so wetting it is fine; just don't wring it (or use spin-dryers).
  3. Drying with a towel — towels are made to get wet; fine.
  4. Carrying — at an outdoor pool in a public area, dry yourself fully before walking around, or you carry the water.
  5. Hot water — the main issue: Chazal forbade bathing in hot water even if heated before Shabbat (people were heating water on Shabbat and claiming otherwise). Hot = above lukewarm; Chacham Ovadia: about 37°C and below is lukewarm and permitted — only if heated before Shabbat. Water being heated on Shabbat (as in many hotels) is forbidden even if lukewarm — check first.

Strict halacha: swimming in an enclosed, lukewarm pool heated before Shabbat is permitted, with those precautions. Whether it suits the spirit of Shabbat is another question — for a child on holiday, reasonable; an adult every week, not ideal (though better than sleeping all afternoon). We don't invent new decrees: "if you start forbidding what is permitted, people end up doing what is really forbidden."

  • Q: Natural hot springs (e.g. Tiberias, Ein Gedi)? — The Shulchan Aruch discusses the springs of Tiberias: permitted.
  • Q: Wet sand? — Walking on it is an unwanted pesik reisha, fine; playing with wet sand is Lash, forbidden.

Hot mikvaot

Many mikvaot are hotter than 37°C (the men's mikveh in Golders Green about 43°C). The Korban Netanel (17th century) argues Chazal forbade bathing, not immersion — a novel view Sephardim prefer not to rely on. So men should use only a lukewarm mikveh on Shabbat morning, and women should preferably immerse on Friday night before nightfall (during bein hashemashot).

Electricity

  • Chazon Ish: completing a circuit is Boneh — a major novelty, rejected by many (e.g. Rav Shmuel Wosner).
  • Mav'ir: scientifically, a closed circuit isn't fire. But an incandescent (filament) bulb glows red-hot — Torah-level Mav'ir.
  • Makeh Be'Patish: completing a functional circuit — some forbid even LEDs on this basis.
  • Chacham Ovadia: electricity (apart from glowing filaments) is rabbinic — very important for when a non-Jew may be asked, and for dimming an LED.
  • Q: Ashkenazim who ask a non-Jew to switch on a light? — Two separate issues: instructing a non-Jew and benefiting from his work. Even for Sephardim, one may not explicitly ask for a light where there is no existing light; details in the final shiur.

Part C — Medicine on Shabbat, Part 1

Why medicine is restricted

Historically medicines were herbal and had to be ground. People anxious about their health (adam bahul al gufo) might grind herbs on Shabbat, so Chazal made a blanket decree against medicine for minor ailments. Today almost all medicine is ready-made and few know how to compound remedies — the Chayei Adam argues the decree no longer applies. We do not follow him, but his view is an extra factor for leniency in some cases. Five levels of illness — next week.

Pikuach nefesh — danger to life

Where there is any possibility of danger to life, one is obligated to break Shabbat.

Rav Chaim of Brisk, famously strict, told a congregant who felt faint on Yom Kippur to eat. Challenged on his "leniency", he replied: I'm not lenient — I'm stringent about the mitzvah to guard life. Breaking Shabbat for pikuach nefesh is a stringency, not a leniency.

  • Applies to any physical danger, for Jews and non-Jews alike.
  • Mental health too: a young child locked in a toilet, crying and panicking — one may break even Torah prohibitions (call the fire brigade, break the door), since the trauma can last a lifetime.
  • A rabbi who hasn't taught his community this is responsible if someone hesitates and harm results.
  • With a shinui? The Rema: if there is no rush, do it with a deviation (e.g. dial with the weaker hand). The Shulchan Aruch — which we follow: no need for a shinui; and never where it endangers others (e.g. driving). (Childbirth is different — final shiur.)
  • Jew, not non-Jew: even if a non-Jew is present, it is better done by a Jew — it is taken more seriously, and shows how important pikuach nefesh is. The quicker, the greater the reward.
  • Maggid Mishneh: for a patient in danger, Shabbat is overridden even for needs not directly medical — e.g. switching on music he asks for — since a stronger spirit helps the fight against illness.

Postscript — a sukkah roof in the rain

Pulling a cover over the sukkah when it rains on Shabbat creates a tent unless it was already open at least a tefach before Shabbat.

Summary

  • Makeh Be'Patish: no completing objects (new pockets, pages, laces, balls) or repairs; toveling permitted (or gift it to a non-Jew); fold a tallit against its creases; no perfume on clothes; no instruments; Sephardim don't dance or clap to music.
  • Swimming: never in open water; an enclosed lukewarm pool heated before Shabbat is strictly permitted (don't squeeze hair or swimsuit, dry before walking outdoors) — consider the spirit of Shabbat. Avoid hot mikvaot on Shabbat.
  • Electricity: per Chacham Ovadia rabbinic, except glowing filaments.
  • Pikuach nefesh: an obligation, including mental health; no shinui needed; better done by a Jew.
