Topic: Shenayim mikra veehad targum: reading the weekly parashah twice with a translation Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 285, seifim 1-7, with the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in his Thursday-night halacha class, Thursday 20 October 2022, straight after Simhat Torah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A neglected obligation

Before resuming the laws of the Sefer Torah, the Rabbi devoted the first class after Simhat Torah to a chapter that is often neglected: the duty to read the parashah twice and the Targum once. Shabbat Bereshit is the moment to start "on the right foot", especially for a Hatan Bereshit.

Two separate obligations. The Shulhan Arukh says that even though a person hears the parashah in synagogue every week, he must read it himself. The public reading is a hovat sibbur, an obligation on the community. If you had to step out, or were unwell and missed shul, the community has still read it. Reading it yourself, twice with translation, is a hovat yahid, an obligation on each individual.

With the community. The Mishnah Berurah explains that this means every week, and at the same time as the community, not too early and not too late. The Gemara promises that whoever does so will have his days and years lengthened.

Long days

The Rabbi explained this promise. The Torah speaks of arikhut yamim, length of days, and of Avraham who "came in days". Long days are days full of meaning and purpose. Someone who reads the parashah every week, and learns its lessons, especially in Sefer Bereshit, lives a fuller life. He cited the Nesiv's introduction to Bereshit: before receiving the Torah in Shemot, we must learn how to behave, often by learning from the mistakes of the Avot and Imahot, and we must be willing to see them.

Asked whether this is a reward, he answered that it is better seen as a consequence. Like someone who goes to the gym and loses weight, a person who learns the Torah's lessons and lives by them finds that his days become longer. "You may have lived 60 years, but you've lived a longer life than an 80 year old because your days have been more well spent." Someone asked how this fits "to read". He answered that the Hakhamim were never interested in mere recital; that is exactly why the translation matters.

How to read

Can the public reading count? The Mishnah Berurah says hearing it is not enough; you must read it yourself, unless you read along word for word with the reader. Other poskim say that hearing it, while paying close attention to a precise reader, can count as one of the two readings. The Rabbi said one may rely on this if necessary.

In what order? Some say each verse twice and then its Targum; some say each paragraph twice and then the Targum; the Vilna Gaon did it differently again. Do whatever makes most sense to you. The Rabbi said he likes to do each verse with its Targum, as it is easier to follow and more meaningful. Asked whether it is tedious, he said: "It's not tedious. If you do it with English, it's not tedious."

The Arizal would read from a Sefer Torah on Friday morning after the service, with a student beside him reading the Targum aloud, which he repeated.

Even "Atarot veDivon". The Shulhan Arukh says even verses that are only place names should be read twice with their Targum. The Rabbi understood this as consistency: getting into the habit of reading every verse twice with its translation.

What counts as Targum today?

The Shulhan Arukh rules, following the Rosh, that learning Rashi counts as Targum, since Rashi explains the verses. "A God-fearing person" reads both the Targum and Rashi. The Rabbi noted this is one of the rare places where the Shulhan Arukh gives his own opinion. The strict halacha is that Rashi fulfils the obligation; reading Onkelos as well is the extra.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that simply reading a translation in another language, which only renders the words, does not fulfil Targum. Targum is more than the words: it adds context and insight. Ashkenazi women used to read Seenah UReenah, which is acceptable because it explains as well as translates.

If Rashi is too hard, read another commentary that goes through the verses. Today, he suggested, an English work that explains each verse, such as Rav Aryeh Kaplan's translation with its notes, or the Stone edition reading the commentary at the bottom, would work. Reading only the English on the facing page would not. Someone asked why not just read the translation if that is what one understands. He answered: so that you become fluent in the Hebrew, "you start to see the Hebrew words for it."

He summarised the recommendation: read the parashah twice, once can be in synagogue, and then either Targum, or Rashi, or an English commentary that explains each verse. "Make the Friday nights meaningful." And this counts as real Torah learning, the basis of everything. Half jokingly, he said that someone who went through the whole Torah with understanding by the end of the year would be excused from his shiur.

When to read

History. In the Gemara's time there were no fixed 52 parashiyot; some communities read the Torah over three years, and a traveller reported communities in Egypt still doing so in the eleventh century. "The parashah" means whatever the community is reading that week.

When does the week begin? The Mishnah Berurah explains that "from Sunday" really means from Shabbat afternoon, once Minhah of Shabbat has read the next parashah. In a big city, even if your own shul has a late Minhah in summer, you may begin after lunch, because the community as a whole has begun. In a town with only one synagogue, you wait for its reading.

When does it end? Ideally finish before Shabbat lunch, before the community moves on at Minhah. If not, there is a view that one may complete it until Tuesday night, and in the worst case one makes it up. The point is to do it in the same week as everyone else, "all on the same page".

Catching up. Someone who became religious mid-year, or who travelled and missed a week, does not need to make up the whole year; he simply starts now. The Rabbi warned that Bereshit is always the hardest week, especially when Simhat Torah falls on Thursday and one can only begin on Friday morning.

Teachers, taamim, and the Yom Tov reading

Reading quietly during the public reading is not considered disturbing.

Teachers who teach the parashah to children all week are exempt from reading it again, because they have gone through it twice with understanding. This shows the misvah is not "some kind of ticking box exercise" but about fluency and understanding. The Rabbi said that someone who gives a daily class on the day's aliyah, explaining it and teaching Rashi, would likewise fulfil it.

Taamim. The Mishnah Berurah quotes the Pri Megadim that the cantillation is not essential for shenayim mikra. The Rabbi suggested that in Sefaradi lands people always learned with the taamim, and said one should read Tehillim and any verse with the taamim, because they give the meaning. He gave the example of the zakef katan as a comma: "You cannot read and translate past the zakef katan."

Yom Tov. One does not need to read the special Yom Tov readings.

Practical summary

  • Every individual must read the weekly parashah twice and the Targum once, in addition to hearing it in synagogue.
  • One of the two readings may be done by following the reader carefully in synagogue, if necessary.
  • Rashi counts as Targum; if Rashi is too hard, use a commentary that explains each verse, not a bare translation.
  • Begin from Shabbat afternoon and aim to finish before Shabbat lunch; if not, by Tuesday night.
  • If you missed weeks, simply start now; teachers who teach the parashah are exempt.
  • Choose a commentary for the year and start this Friday night.
