Where we are

We are in the second part of Siman 13, the law of a pregnant or nursing woman (meuberet umeneket haverah). Hazal decreed that after her husband dies she waits until the child is weaned, 24 full months, not counting the day of death and the day of the engagement. The reason is that a new pregnancy would spoil her milk and endanger the first child. Last week we saw the Gemaras, the ways of counting the 24 months (the Beit Yosef rules 24 months, the Rema adds a month in a leap year), and the Rambam's extra reason of daksa, the pressure on the foetus. Today the Rabbi covered the three arguments among the Rishonim. The Shulhan Arukh, the Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel are for next week.

  1. A divorced woman (grushah).
  2. A woman whose child's father is unknown.
  3. A woman who is no longer nursing, for various reasons.

1. The divorced woman

The Gemara speaks of a widow. Its proof is that it asks why the child's food is not claimed from the estate (yorshim), which implies the husband died. So what about a woman divorced while pregnant or nursing? The Rishonim have two main views. Looking at the reasons, the Rabbi explained that there are really three or four.

Rabbenu Shimshon (Tosafot): a divorcee may remarry. A wife must nurse as one of the tasks she does in return for being supported by her husband (mezonot). When he dies, she goes on receiving support from the estate until she claims her ketubah, so there is still an obligation (shibud) to nurse. A divorcee has no support and no obligation, so there is no reason for her to wait. The Beit Yosef adds a second reason: the first husband is alive and is responsible for his child, so if the milk is spoiled, he can provide for it.

Rabbenu Tam: a divorcee also waits 24 months. He makes two points.

  • The first reasons in the Gemara, the second pregnancy and daksa, apply equally to a widow and a divorcee. The Gemara did not distinguish. The last reason only added, it did not cancel them.
  • Even a widow has cases with no obligation, for example if she claims her ketubah. So the obligation cannot be the whole point. Reading him simply, it is practical: a mother will go on nursing out of instinct, even without any duty, and a new marriage and pregnancy would endanger the baby.

The Rashba (and, as the Rabbi described it, the Riaz) in between. Rabbenu Tam is only talking about a divorcee whose baby is attached to her (makirah), meaning she has already begun nursing and it would be dangerous to hand the baby to someone else. There a real obligation can exist, and the husband must pay her for nursing. In other cases even Rabbenu Tam would permit. The Rabbi noted that this differs from how Tosafot present Rabbenu Tam, as a dispute in every case.

The Rabbi asked the class a question: if the first husband dies afterwards, within the 24 months, does the reason of the husband being around still hold? The class explored it and he said we would return to it.

2. The unknown father (nisbarah biznut)

She became pregnant outside marriage and we do not know the father. If we do know the father, the dinim are those of a divorcee: is there an obligation, is she makirah? Where the father is unknown:

  • Rabbenu Shimshon: clearly permitted. It is no stricter than a divorcee.
  • The Mordekhai: Rabbenu Tam's reason is the possible obligation. A divorcee could one day have an obligation, but here there is no father, so there never will be. Yet he concludes that she must wait in all cases, because the real reason is practical: a mother wants to continue nursing even with no obligation.
  • The Mahari Mints: permitted. The mother's instinct is not the reason. The reason is an obligation, and here there is nobody to be forced to pay and Beit Din cannot be asked to pay. If we started funding such things from the public purse, everyone would ask. He adds that if she marries, at least someone looks after her and the baby. Even Rabbenu Tam would agree.

So there are two views about Rabbenu Tam on the unknown father. The Rabbi said we can already see a sfek sfekah developing for the practical ruling.

3. She is no longer nursing

Recap of the Gemara. She gave the baby to a nursemaid, weaned it, or the baby died. If the baby died, she may remarry. If she weaned it, she may not, in case she hurries the weaning. If she gave it to a nursemaid, she may not, since the nursemaid may go back. Only a family of the Reish Galuta was an exception, because a nursemaid would never defy them.

Can this work today? The Rabbi asked whether a contract could create the same fear. The class said that there has to be real fear (pahad), for instance serious consequences. The Rabbi followed up with the sources.

  • Rabbi Yaakov of Krakow, cited by the Mordekhai: a nursemaid who is bound by a neder, a vow before the public that she cannot be released from, is as good as the Reish Galuta. Even those who forbid the nursemaid may allow this after the fact.
  • The Maharam of Rothenburg disagrees. He gives two arguments. First, people fear a human ruler more than a vow before Hashem, even though the vow is far more serious (like the words in Berakhot: "if only you would fear Heaven as you fear people"). More fundamentally, the Gemara's conclusion is that only the Reish Galuta works. Even if the Reish Galuta were re-established, nothing else can be compared to it.
  • The Magid Mishneh asks why the Rambam and the Rif do not mention the Reish Galuta. Either they agree with the Maharam, or they held that it was a specific case, and since there was none in their time, there was no point mentioning it.
  • The Rosh agrees with the Maharam but for a different reason. He does hold that the Reish Galuta exception works, because the fear is real and everyone knows it is a unique institution, so nobody will confuse it with a nursemaid in an ordinary case. But we do not extend it to other cases, or to a neder, because it would be confused with ordinary vows and promises. The Maharam's reasoning is more basic.

Cases of no concern (the Rosh). If there was never a concern of hurrying the weaning, the Rosh permits remarriage. For example, she weaned the baby while her husband was still alive, for no reason connected to remarriage. The Gemara's concern was that she would act to be free to remarry, and while her husband lived there was no such motive. The Rosh adds the cases of a woman whose breasts do not function, and a woman whose milk stopped during her husband's lifetime.

The Rabbi then showed three ways to read the Rosh.

  • The Tur: each of these is an independent permission, even if she hired a nursemaid (this was done during the husband's lifetime).
  • The Beit Yosef: he reads the Rosh differently, because the Rosh himself in a responsum says the nursemaid is in addition. He reads it as two cases (no ability to nurse, and milk stopped during her husband's lifetime), and in each she must also have hired a nursemaid, since the child needs a practical solution.
  • The Korban Netanel: a middle view. Where the loss of ability is obvious to everyone, the second condition is not needed. Where it is not publicly known, one needs the nursemaid as well.

The Rabbi's final point: the Mordekhai says that although the law is that a neder nursemaid does not help, after the fact it works. The Maharam says it does not work even after the fact. The Rabbi emphasised that the view of Rabbi Yaakov of Krakow is a minority view, so we do not rule in this way lehathilah.

Summary of the Rishonim

The Rabbi's own summary was: weaning during the husband's life works definitely, and so does a woman whose breasts do not work or whose milk stopped (the Beit Yosef adds the hired nursemaid). The neder nursemaid is a minority view, permitted only after the fact according to the Mordekhai.

Practical summary

  • A widow waits 24 months. A divorcee is a three-way mahloket among the Rishonim (Rabbenu Shimshon permits, Rabbenu Tam forbids, the Rashba permits unless the baby is attached to her).
  • A woman with an unknown father: Rabbenu Shimshon permits; for Rabbenu Tam the Mordekhai is strict and the Mahari Mints lenient.
  • The nursemaid from the Reish Galuta is an exception, and the Maharam and the Rosh do not extend it to other fears or to a neder.
  • The Rosh permits remarriage where she was not nursing in her husband's lifetime. The Beit Yosef requires a nursemaid to be hired as well in some cases.
