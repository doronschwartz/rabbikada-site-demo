  • Tzelem = essence; what makes something what it is

The Question Posed

If man was created with full intellect (tzelem Elokim) in Chapter 1, why does the snake in Chapter 3 offer "knowledge of good and evil" as if it's something new? And why would the "punishment" for the sin be the granting of this intellect — the very thing that defines a human?

Tonight's Mission

We are now going to dissect Rambam's answer in detail — work through it line by line, develop its implications, and watch as it transforms our entire reading of Bereishit.

PART 1 — Premise One: Adam Had FULL Intellect Before the Sin

והשכל שהשם השפיע על האדם והוא שלמותו האחרונה הוא שקיבל האדם לפני חטאו

The intellect that Hashem bestowed upon man — his ultimate perfection — was received before his sin.

What This Establishes

  1. The intellect humans received in Chapter 1 (b'tzelem Elokim) is complete — not partial
  2. This intellect is the basis on which Hashem could command him
  3. "You cannot give commandments to animals or those without intellect" — the very existence of the command (don't eat from the tree) proves the human had the rational capacity to receive and understand it

ולגבי השכל נאמר לאדם שהוא בצלמו ובדמותו, ובגללו דיבר אליו השם וציווה אותו

Because of the intellect, he was created in His tzelem and His demut — and because of it, Hashem spoke to him and commanded him.

PART 2 — Premise Two: TWO Domains of Knowledge

This is the radical insight of the chapter. There are two completely different domains of "knowing":

ובשכל מבחינים בין האמת לבין השקר. וזה היה לאדם באופן המושלם. אך המגונה והנאה הוא מפורסמות לא ממושכלות

With intellect one distinguishes between truth and falsehood — and this Adam had completely. But ugly/beautiful (good/bad) belongs to mefursamot — social consensus — NOT to intellectual understanding.

The Critical Distinction

Domain

Type of Knowledge

Hebrew Term

Examples

Muskalot

Objective intellectual truth

אמת ושקר (true/false)

"The sky is blue." "2+2=4." "World War II ended in 1945." Mathematical equations. Scientific facts.

Mefursamot

Subjective social consensus

טוב ורע (good/bad)

"Nudity is shameful." "Honor your elders." Most norms of behavior. Aesthetic judgments.

Why This Distinction Matters

משום שאין אומרים השמים כדורים נאה, והארץ שטח מגונה — אלא אמת ושקר

We don't say "the sky being spherical is beautiful" or "the earth being flat is ugly" — we say true or false.

Hebrew itself encodes this:

  • For facts → we use אמת ושקר (true/false)
  • For aesthetic/moral matters → we use טוב ורע (good/bad)

Intellect Only Handles Truth/Falsehood

ובשכל מכיר האדם את האמת מן השקר וכן הוא בכל הדברים המושכלים

The intellect distinguishes truth from falsehood — and this is how it works for all intellectual matters.

Intellect gives you objectivity. It doesn't (and can't) give you subjective social-good-and-bad. That's a different faculty entirely.

PART 3 — The Before/After Picture

Before the Sin

וכשהיה במצבו המושלם עם תכונותיו הטבעיות המושכלות שבעבורם נאמר עליו ותחסרהו מעט מאלהים

When man was complete with his natural intellectual capacities — to the extent that David in Tehillim calls him almost a mini-god (ותחסרהו מעט מאלהים — "You have made him a little less than the divine") because of his resemblance in intellect to Hashem...

What Adam Could NOT Do Before the Sin

לא היה לו כוח כלל העוסק במפורסמות, ולא היה יכול להשיגם

He had no capacity whatsoever to engage with mefursamot — and could not perceive them.

The Nudity Example

עד שאפילו הגנאי המפורסם ביותר — גילוי הערוה — לא היה גנאי בבחינתו, ולא השיג גנותו

Even the most-agreed-upon "ugly" thing — uncovered nakedness — was not "ugly" in his perception. He had no way to perceive its ugliness.

Why? Because "ugly" is a social/aesthetic category, not an intellectual one. Nudity is just a fact — true (we are uncovered) or false (we are covered). Whether it's "ugly" or "shameful" is a mefursamot judgment that Adam's intellect simply did not produce.

Adam's mind didn't operate in those categories at all.

The Sin Itself — Not Just Eating

אך כאשר חטא ונטה אחר תאוותיו ודמיונותיו והנאותיו החושים הגופניים

But when he sinned — and turned after his desires, his imaginations, and his bodily sensual pleasures...

The Quote from the Verse

כי טוב העץ למאכל וכי תאוה הוא לעיניים "The tree was good for eating and desirable to the eyes"

This is the language of sensual perception — eyes, taste, desire. He veered from the intellect (which sees emet/sheker) toward the senses (which judge tov/ra).

What "Cheit" Actually Means

The word חטא does NOT mean "sin" in the archaic English sense. The shoresh means to miss, to veer off the target.

The Tanakh uses this root to describe shooting:

velo yachati — "he would shoot and never miss"

Cheit = veering off direction

Teshuvah = returning to direction

You're driving on the motorway, you veered off course — cheit. You're coming back on track — teshuvah. Two of the most important words of the Yamim Noraim, both mistranslated.

The Sin Was the Turning, Not the Eating

The actual sin was the veering of attention — toward the sensual, the imaginary, the desirable-to-the-eyes. The eating was merely the manifestation of this internal turning.

The Punishment — Measure for Measure

נענש בכך שנשלמה ממנו אותה השגה שכלית

He was punished by having that intellectual perception diminished from him.

The Logic — Mida K'neged Mida

The Sin

The Consequence

Veered after physical/sensual perception

Now perceives the world through good/bad (the realm of the senses/society)

Abandoned objective truth-seeking

Becomes immersed in subjective social judgments

Turned the face toward the desirable

The face is now fixed in that direction (will develop below)

ולכן לא ציית לציווי שבשל שכלו נצטווה בו

Therefore he did not obey the commandment which he had received because of his intellect.

The Acquisition — A New Faculty

ונקנתה לו השגת המפורסמות

He acquired the perception of mefursamot — the faculty to engage with social good/bad.

ושקע בראיית דברים כגון הם

And he became submerged in seeing things this way.

The word שקע is striking — he didn't just gain this perception, he sank into it. It became the dominant mode of his engagement with reality.

The Realization

ואז התוודע לו גודל מה שאיבד והופשט ממנו ולאיזה מצב הגיע

Then he realized what he had lost, what had been stripped from him, and to what state he had descended.

The realization came after the sin, not before. He didn't know what he was giving up until it was gone.

PART 4 — Understanding the Snake's Temptation

If eating from the tree caused a loss of intellectual clarity and an immersion in subjectivity — why would Adam want to do it? What was attractive about the snake's offer?

The Verse Restated

והייתם כאלהים יודעי טוב ורע "You will be like Elohim, knowing good and evil"

The Three Meanings of Elohim Become Critical Here

If Elohim meant God here, the verse would imply: "You will become like God." But that doesn't fit — they were already in God's tzelem with full intellect.

But Elohim has three meanings. Onkelos understood it as Meaning #3 — important/prominent people, lords, judges:

ותהון כרברבין — "You will be like great ones"

The Snake's Real Offer

Not divinity. Sovereignty.

The snake's promise: "Yes, you'll lose some intellectual clarity. The world will become more subjective and blurrier. BUT — you will become the arbiters. You will be the judges of good and bad. You will gain moral autonomy."

Why This Was Tempting

When everything is intellectual (emet/sheker), you submit to objective truth. The truth simply is.

But when everything is moral (tov/ra) and the criteria are mefursamot — social consensus — you get to decide. You become the lord of values. You become elohim in the third sense — like a judge who interprets and arbitrates.

Adam wanted moral sovereignty.

He traded objective submission for subjective autonomy. The trade was:

  • Loses: clarity, certainty, the perception of objective truth
  • Gains: moral sovereignty, arbiter status, autonomy in determining values

A Mapili's Observation on the Word "Etz"

The word עץ (tree) shares the same root as עצה (counsel, advice).

What do judges and lords do? They administer etzah — counsel and judgment. They are sources of advisement.

When the woman saw the tree (עץ), it represented the etzah (counsel) — the ability to give moral advice, to be morally sovereign. The "tree" wasn't just botanical; the language hints that what she desired was the judicial position the eating would confer.

This is suggestive — not definitive. The Moreh will later raise the question of whether the entire narrative is metaphorical anyway.

PART 5 — "Their Eyes Were Opened" — A New Perception

The verse:

ותפקחנה עיני שניהם וידעו כי ערומים הם "And the eyes of both of them were opened, and they knew they were naked"

The Crucial Question

Why does it say their eyes were "opened" (פקחנה) and not that they "saw" (יראו)?

לא נאמר ותפקחנה עיני שניהם ויראו, משום שמה שראו לפני כן הוא אשר ראו אחרי כן

It doesn't say "their eyes were opened and they saw" — because what they saw before was what they saw after. The same physical eyes, the same objects.

Nothing Was Removed from Their Eyes

לא היה כיסוי שעל עיניו והוסר

There was no veil on their eyes that was removed.

What Did Change?

אלא נוצר לו מצב אחר שבו הוא רואה כמגונה מה שלא היה כמגונה קודם לכן

A new condition was created in which he now perceives as "ugly" what was not "ugly" before.

The change was not in what they could see, but in how they perceived what they saw. The same nakedness, but now classified through tov/ra rather than just emet/sheker.

Pikoach — A Word of Perception, Not Vision

ודע שאותה מילה — כלומר פיקח — משמשת רק במשמעות של גילוי הבחנה, לא של התחדשות של החושים

Know that this word — pikoach — is always used in the sense of revelation of perception, not of new sensory creation.

Hagar in the Desert

ויפקח אלהים את עיניה ותרא באר מים "God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water"

The water was there the whole time. Hashem didn't miraculously create water. She didn't have the perception to see it because she was exhausted, despairing. The "opening" was an opening of her perceptual capacity, not of her physical eyes.

Yeshayahu — The Future Redemption

אז תפקחנה עיני עורים "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened"

Same eyes, same eyesight. But after the redemption, we will perceive the hand of Hashem in a way we don't now. Now we live blindly to His presence — then we will see what was always there.

Why a "Pikeach" Means a Wise Person

The Talmud uses pikeach as the opposite of a shoteh (fool). Why does "one whose eyes are open" mean "wise"?

Because pikoach doesn't mean to see physically — it means to perceive truly. A wise person isn't one with better eyesight; they're one who perceives what others miss.

The Linguistic Parallel: Hear vs. Listen

English distinguishes:

  • Hear — sound waves entering the ear (passive, mechanical)
  • Listen — actually attending to and understanding the sound (active, perceptual)

Hebrew uses shema for both — but the meaning is "listen, perceive":

  • Shema Yisrael — not "Hear, Israel" but "Listen, perceive, focus, Israel"

The verse:

פקוח אזניים ולא ישמע "Ears are open, but he does not listen"

The sound waves are entering. But there's no perception. Same with:

עיניים להם ולא יראו "They have eyes but they do not see"

These verses use the language of perception (not mechanical vision) to describe spiritual blindness.

PART 6 — "You Changed His Face" — The Punishment Made Personal

The verse from Iyov speaking of this very event:

משנה פניו ותשלחהו "You change his face, and you send him away"

The Question

According to Rambam, nothing physically changed in the human. His eyes are the same. His face would be the same. So what does "you change his face" mean?

Rambam's Explanation

כאשר שינה את מגמת פניו — גורש

When he changed the direction of his face — he was banished.

Why "Panim" Means Both Face and Direction

שכן פנים הוא מלשון המונח הגזור מפנה. משום שהאדם מכוון אל פניו לדבר שהוא מכוון אליו את רצונו

The word panim (face) derives from panah (to turn). Because a person turns their face toward what they want.

Your face shows where your desire is directed. Panah = to turn. Panim = the place that turns. The face is the visible manifestation of where the soul is oriented.

The Sin Redefined

כאשר שינה את מגמת פניו, וכיוון אל עבר דבר שהיה עליו ציווי קודם לכן שלא יכוון אליו — גורש מגן עדן

When he changed the direction of his face — turning his attention toward something he had been commanded not to direct his attention toward — he was banished from Gan Eden.

The Sin Was the Direction Itself

Notice what this is saying:

  • The sin was not primarily the eating. The eating was the manifestation.
  • The sin was the turning — the change of panim, the new orientation toward the sensual, the imaginary, the desirable.
  • The eating was the natural consequence of having already turned.

The Punishment Is the Sin

וזהו העונש המוטעם מבחינה עניינית לחטא

This is the punishment that fits the sin in its essence — measure for measure.

You turned your direction. Therefore your direction is now fixed in this new place. You sank into sensual perception. Therefore you will now live submerged in the world of sensual perception.

The punishment for veering after the physical is to be subjected to a life governed by physical/social judgments.

PART 7 — The Consequences in Practice

The Rambam now traces how the sin manifests in everyday human existence:

שמשום שהותר לו לאכול מהדברים הטובים ולהיות בשלוה...

Before the sin: he was permitted to eat from the good things and to be in tranquility.

וכאשר נהיה חמדן והולך אחרי הנאותיו ודמיונותיו, ואחר מה שנעשה לו לאכול — נמנע ממנו הכל

When he became a person of desire, following his pleasures and imaginings, and after the food he had been forbidden — everything was withheld from him.

The New Conditions of Human Life

והוכרח לאכול את המאכל הגרוע ביותר, שלפני כן לא היה לו כמזון

He was forced to eat the most degraded food — which previously was not food for him. (Animal food, basic vegetation — what he hadn't eaten before.)

וגם זאת אחרי המאמץ

And even this only with great effort.

The verses:

וקוץ ודרדר תצמיח לך "It will grow thorns and thistles for you"

בזעת אפך תאכל לחם "By the sweat of your brow you will eat bread"

וישלחהו ה' אלהים מגן עדן לעבוד את האדמה אשר לוקח משם "And Hashem God sent him from Gan Eden to till the ground from which he was taken"

The Animal-Likeness

והשוותו לבהמות במזונו וברוב מצביו

He became equated to animals in his food and most of his conditions.

ואכלת את עשב השדה "And you shall eat the vegetation of the field"

The Verse from Tehillim

אדם ביקר בל ילין נמשל כבהמות נדמו "A human who does not maintain his honor — he is likened to the beasts that perish"

A human who doesn't possess yakar (intrinsic dignity, self-grandeur) — who acts according to the lower impulses — becomes like an animal.

And that's exactly what happened to Adam through the sin.

The Final Word

ישתבח בעל החפץ — תכליתו וחכמתו אינם ניתנים להשגה

Blessed be He who wills — His purpose and wisdom are not fully attainable.

The Rambam is signaling: we've gone as far as the intellect can carry us in this question. The deeper why of the whole scheme is beyond us.

PART 8 — Three Important Questions on the Chapter

Question 1 (Jonathan): Where Is "Subjectivity" in the Text?

"Where in the Rambam's text do we find the idea of subjective morality?"

Answer

In the line:

המגונה והנאה הוא מפורסמות לא ממושכלות

"Good/bad (the ugly and the beautiful) belongs to mefursamot — not to muskalot."

  • Mefursamot literally means "things accepted by social consensus" (from פרסום — publicity/widespread acceptance)
  • Muskalot means "things known through intellectual reasoning"

This is precisely Aristotle's distinction — the Rambam is importing Aristotelian ethics: general moral judgments are opinions held by general consensus, not deductions of pure reason.

This does NOT mean Torah ethics are subjective. It means the category of "good and bad" — as humans naturally apply it — operates by consensus rather than logical proof.

Question 2 (Mark): Are Basic Mitzvot Like "Do Not Murder" Also Subjective?

Answer

This is unclear in the Rambam, but here is the resolution:

The Rambam (in Shmoneh Perakim) and Saadia Gaon distinguish:

  • Mitzvot Sichliyot (rational mitzvot) — including murder, adultery, theft — these are so clear they fall under muskalot, not mefursamot
  • Mitzvot Shimiyot (received mitzvot) — those that require revelation to know

So the foundational moral truths — don't murder, don't commit adultery, don't steal — are objectively true even within Rambam's framework. They fall under muskalot.

What Rambam is discussing in the Bereishit narrative is the broader category of moral judgment — including aesthetic and social norms — that came into being with the sin.

Question 3 (Mark): Why Bring "Judges" Into It at All?

"Why do we need 'judges' for good and bad? Can't intellect also adjudicate?"

Answer

You don't need judges for mathematics.

  • No one needs a judge to decide if 2+2=4
  • No judicial review of a chemistry equation
  • Math exams are graded objectively — true or false

But you need judges for law, ethics, politics, narrative.

  • Legal essays, political opinions, ethical dilemmas — these go to judicial review
  • That's the entire judicial system — interpreting and applying values
  • This is the domain of Elohim (Meaning 3) — important arbiters

The snake's offer is precisely this: you will become like judges. You will be the ones who interpret and decide values. Sovereignty over the moral order.

Question 4 (Jonathan): Wasn't Adam Enlarged by Gaining Moral Choice?

"Before the sin, Adam had clarity but maybe less choice and responsibility. After the sin, he has moral sensibility, the ability to choose. Isn't that an enlargement?"

Two Responses

1. The Rambam's Position The Rambam does not seem to view this as enlargement — he views it as yerida, a descent. The whole framing is measure for measure punishment. Adam realized what he lost, and what he gained was the consequence of his sin, not a positive achievement.

2. Rav Dessler's Different View (alluded to in the shiur) Rav Dessler (Michtav Me'Eliyahu) argues something close to what Jonathan suggested — that Adam sinned in order to gain greater moral responsibility, and this could be understood as a kind of Kiddush Hashem. Rabbi Kada noted he doesn't fully understand this position and doesn't think it accords with Rambam.

3. The Ramchal's Important Point The single command (don't eat from the tree) is reflective of all mitzvot — it's not really one isolated choice. Ramchal explains the Tree of Knowledge represents Yichud Hashem (the unity of Hashem) — and all mitzvot and aveirot are about Yichud Hashem. So Adam did have meaningful choice before the sin; the single command represented the totality of moral choice. This argues against the "more responsibility now" reading.

PART 9 — Was There Bad Before the Sin? (Free Will Question)

A serious objection to the chapter:

"If 'good and bad' only came into being through the sin, how could Adam have free will before the sin? Free will requires choosing between good and bad. So either bad existed before the sin (contradicting the chapter), or Adam didn't really have free will (but then how could Hashem command him?)."

The Nefesh HaChayim's Resolution

Rav Chaim Volozhin in Nefesh HaChayim addresses this directly:

הנמצא, כי קודם החטא לא היה כולל רק מכוחות הקדושה לבד, לא מכוחות הרע. אבל אחר החטא נכללו ונתערבו בו גם כוחות הטומאה והרע

Before the sin, man was composed only of forces of holiness — no forces of evil within him. After the sin, the forces of evil were incorporated and mixed into him.

The Critical Move

Free will existed before the sin — but the "bad" was external, not internal.

אמנם, לא שהיה ענין בחירתו מחמת שכוחות הרע היו כלולים בתוכו, כי הוא היה אדם ישר לגמרי

His free will did NOT come from evil being mixed within him — he was completely upright.

וכוחות הרע היו עומדים לצד ובענין בפני עצמו חוץ ממנו

The forces of evil stood off to the side, as a separate matter outside of him.

The Snake Was External

והיה בעל בחירה להכנס לכוחות הרע חס ושלום

He had free will to enter into the forces of evil, just as a human has the choice to jump into a fire.

כמו שהאדם הוא בעל בחירה להכנס לתוך האש

Like a human has free will to jump into fire — the fire is external, but you have free will to expose yourself to it.

לכן כשירצה השטן להחטיאו, הוצרך הנחש מבחוץ לבוא ולפתותו

Therefore when the satanic force wanted to cause him to sin, the snake had to come from outside and entice him.

Compare to Today

לא כמו שעתה שהיצר מפתה את האדם בתוך האדם עצמו, ונדמה לאדם שהוא עצמו הוא המושך את עצמו

Not like today, when the yetzer ha'ra tempts a person from within the person themselves — and it appears to the person that he himself is pulling himself toward the temptation.

The Sharp Distinction

Before the Sin

After the Sin

Free will between holiness (internal) and evil (external)

Free will between competing internal voices

Bad was like a fire — you knew not to jump in, but you could

Bad whispers from inside your own consciousness

Snake had to come from outside

Yetzer Hara is inside

Adam knew bad was destructive

We confuse bad for good because it sounds like our own voice

You wouldn't choose suicide because you know it's destructive

We constantly choose against our own welfare because the temptation sounds like us

Before the Sin: Bad Like Suicide

A human today knows that suicide is destructive. Even if available, you don't do it because you know it's wrong. Adam viewed all evil this way before the sin. It was a real option, but a self-evidently destructive one. The free will was real, but the choice was clear.

The Snake's Achievement

The snake's job was not to create evil but to make evil look attractive. The tree was good to look at, desirable to the eyes — the snake made the destructive thing look beautiful. Adam, exercising free will, chose to ingest the external evil — and from that point, evil became internal.

The cake outside you — everyone tells you it's poisonous, you know it's poisonous, but it looks delicious. You ingest it. Now the poison is inside you. That's the sin of Adam — he ingested the evil, and now it's part of every human being.

Summary of Chapter 2

The Main Argument

  1. Adam had full intellect from creation — b'tzelem Elokim means with the rational faculty of objective truth-seeking.
  2. There are two domains of knowledge:
  • Muskalot — intellect — true/false — objective
  • Mefursamot — social consensus — good/bad — subjective
  1. Before the sin, Adam operated only in muskalot. Nakedness was not "ugly" because "ugly" is a mefursamot category that didn't exist for him.
  2. The sin was the turning of attention toward the sensual, imaginative, desirable. The eating was the manifestation; the sin was the directional change.
  3. The punishment is measure for measure — he turned his face toward the senses, so his face is now fixed in that direction. He is now submerged in the world of mefursamot.
  4. The snake's offer was sovereignty, not divinity — "You will be like Elohim" = "You will be like judges/lords" — arbiters of moral truth. Moral autonomy in exchange for intellectual clarity.
  5. "Their eyes were opened" is a perception change, not a vision change. Same eyes, same objects — new categorical framework.
  6. "You changed his face" — panim derives from panah (to turn). The face is the direction of the soul's desire. Adam changed his orientation; the punishment fixed him in the new orientation.
  7. Result: Humans are now subjected to the world of subjective evaluations. They must work for food, struggle for clarity, live under norms — animal-like in much of their existence.
  8. Free will existed before the sin (per Nefesh HaChayim) — but evil was external, not internal. The snake came from outside. After the sin, evil is internal, and we mistake our yetzer for our own voice.
