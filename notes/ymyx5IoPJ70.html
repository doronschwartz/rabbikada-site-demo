Topic: How the Maggid section of the Haggadah is built, and what each paragraph is doing Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Wednesday 13 April 2022, shortly before Pesah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Maggid is a springboard

The night before, the Rabbi had taught the laws of the Seder. He repeated one point from that class: Maggid should be treated as a springboard for discussion, not as a text to get through. The misvah is to talk about the Exodus, and it lasts the whole night. If you fall asleep, you fall asleep, but you go to bed thinking about Egypt. It is a mistake to think that once Maggid is done we are free to talk about anything.

The Haggadah is a collection of rabbinic statements, but not a random one. His aim in the half hour was to show the order, so that at the Seder we know why each paragraph is there. He added that the Seder night is not the time for clever questions about why a word is spelled one way or another. It is the time to talk about Egypt and the miracles.

Part one: question, answer and introduction

  • Ha Lahma Anya is a later introduction. The Mishnah in Pesahim has Maggid begin with Mah Nishtanah. It is in Aramaic, on a simple level, so that anyone who still has nowhere to go understands the invitation to come now.
  • Mah Nishtanah is not four questions but one: why is this night different? Then four proofs that it is.
  • Avadim Hayinu is the father's answer: we were slaves and Hashem took us out, and that is why we dip, lean and eat maror. This is one of the paragraphs that must be said, since it fulfils the basic misvah of telling the story in question and answer. And even if we are all wise and know the whole Torah, we must still tell it, and the more the better.
  • The story of the rabbis in Bnei Berak proves what was just said. A maaseh in the Talmud usually comes to prove a point. These sages spent the whole night telling the story, until their students came to say it was time for Shema.
  • Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah shows that there is a misvah to remember the Exodus every night of the year. All the more so tonight, it must be done properly.
  • Barukh HaMakom and the four children. Now we learn how to tell it. The Rabbi preferred "four children" to "four sons," since banim in the Torah means children in general. Each child is spoken to differently.

That, he said, is the end of part one: a synopsis with question, answer, proof and introductions.

Part two: from the beginning, through four verses

Mithilah. Following the Gemara, we begin with the shameful part and end with praise. Shlomo HaMelekh says that one who sits in darkness sees the light. To appreciate freedom, we start from the bottom, even our ancestors' idolatry, and then Hashem's choice of Avraham and the covenant between the pieces: your descendants will go down to Egypt and come out. We bless Hashem who kept His promise.

Vehi SheAmedah. The Rabbi said he is bothered when children come home explaining that "vehi" stands for the six orders of the Mishnah, the five books and the Ten Commandments. "That's not what was intended." Vehi, "it," is the covenant just mentioned. It is what has stood for us in every generation, when they rose to destroy us.

Se Ulmad, "go out and learn," gives an example: Lavan wanted to destroy Yaakov, and Hashem warned him not to harm him.

Then comes the core. The Haggadah takes four verses from the bikkurim declaration in Devarim 26, beginning "Arami oved avi," and expounds them phrase by phrase. A good Haggadah prints the four verses in bold so you can follow. Each verse is quoted, and then each part is explained with a supporting verse:

  1. Verse one: we went down to Egypt, compelled by the word of Hashem; we came to sojourn, not to settle; seventy souls; we became a great nation, distinct there; many, like the plants of the field.
  2. Verse two: the Egyptians did us harm, afflicted us, and placed hard labour on us.
  3. Verse three: we cried out; Hashem heard our voice and saw our affliction, our toil (our children) and our oppression, which the Rabbi understood as the mental stress and persecution.
  4. Verse four: Hashem took us out with a strong hand and outstretched arm, great awe, signs and wonders. "Not through an angel, not through a seraph, not through a messenger," but Hashem Himself.

He explained some apparent breaks in the flow. The passage on "I will pass through the land of Egypt" is a short side-explanation of a verse just quoted, still very relevant, but a step aside from the word-by-word structure. Another midrashic paragraph found in some Haggadot is not part of his community's custom, and in his view was added because someone liked it, since it interrupts the exposition.

He asked whether anyone knew what "blood, fire and pillars of smoke" means. It is a verse from Yoel, quoted only to prove that "wonders" means blood. The Ritva offers another reading: it all describes the plague of blood, which was boiling and gave off smoke.

Then a second explanation of verse four: its five phrases, each counted twice, make ten, the ten plagues. This closes part two.

Part three: the plagues and gratitude

Now we list the ten plagues, and the sages count further: Rabbi Yose HaGelili says fifty at the Sea, since a "finger" struck Egypt and a "hand" struck at the Sea; Rabbi Eliezer counts four elements to each plague, Rabbi Akiva five. Someone asked why the numbers matter. They are different ways of looking at the miracles, and they lead us to burst out in thanks.

So comes Dayenu: each step alone would have been enough. Then "all the more so": how many times over Hashem's kindness is doubled. A participant asked whether Dayenu is a fourth part. The Rabbi said he would call it part three b, since it flows from the counting of the plagues.

Part four: Pesah, Massah and Maror, and reliving it

Rabban Gamliel says that whoever has not mentioned three things has not fulfilled the obligation: Pesah, Massah and Maror. Each is explained with its verse. The Pesah offering, because Hashem passed over our houses; massah, because there was no time for the dough to rise; maror, because they embittered our lives. "Even if you didn't follow everything which you just said, you need to say the following."

Then the conclusion: in every generation a person must see himself as if he left Egypt. After hours of telling the history, the point is not just to talk about it but to relive it now. After that come the thanks and praise of Hallel, and the berakhah.

Summary

  • Maggid opens a whole night of discussion about the Exodus; it is not a text to finish.
  • Part one is a short version: question, answer, proof and introductions.
  • Part two starts from our lowly beginnings and expounds four verses from Arami Oved Avi word by word.
  • Vehi SheAmedah refers to the covenant with Avraham, not to an acronym.
  • The plagues lead to Dayenu, and Rabban Gamliel's three things lead to reliving the Exodus ourselves.
