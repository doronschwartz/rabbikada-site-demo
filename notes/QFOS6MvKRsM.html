Part 1: The Five Elements

1. There is a Creator

"Straightforward. Whether creation was from nothing or from pre-existent matter as old as God (as Ibn Ezra and others held) is Principle 4."

2. He is perfect

Harold Kushner's When Bad Things Happen to Good People: the leading Conservative rabbi argued God wants to stop evil but can't. "According to the Rambam, and everyone, that's a misunderstanding of God. His essence is perfection, which means omnipotence."

Can God do the impossible? (Discussed with Rav Saadia on Monday nights.) Can He make a rock He can't lift?

No, and that's no imperfection. "It's semantics: we hear can't and think weakness. Hashem can't catch flu: He's not physical. Rav Saadia's example: He can't make a square a triangle."

The Rambam (Moreh): we don't call a man weak because he can't lift 100,000 weights. Nor is God incapable because He can't make Himself corporeal, create His equal, or make a square whose diagonal equals its side.

The dissent: Rebbe Nachman of Breslov: the philosophers say God can't make a triangle a square; I say He can, for His ways are hidden. "Rebbe Nachman hated philosophers and doctors: both of which the Rambam was. A minority view, not logically sound."

3. He depends on nothing

A consequence of perfection. אדון עולם אשר מלך בטרם כל יציר נברא: He reigned before anything was formed.

"Hashem doesn't need our prayers." "Contrary to Christian theology. We sometimes talk as if Hashem is waiting for our tefillot. Prayer is transformative, for our own sake: a meditation for our intellectual and moral development." "If I didn't know Hebrew and read the English of the siddur, through a Western, Christian lens, I wouldn't want to pray: holy, holy, holy, we worship You… as if God needs us. It's quite babyish."

4. Everything depends on Him, continuously

Not a God who created and left (deism). "Even if you believe in a perfect, independent Creator who then left the world, that isn't Hashem." המחדש בטובו בכל יום תמיד מעשה בראשית.

The imagined universe. "Create a beautiful universe in your mind. It exists only while you're thinking of it. Someone tells you some news, your attention slips, it's gone." So Hashem actively sustains every element, every moment. "No carpenter who makes something and walks away."

5. His existence is necessary

From the Mishneh Torah: וה׳ אלהים אמת: "Two plus two is four, not merely logically: it must be. That's emet."

Yigdal and Ani Ma'amin lack perfection; Ani Ma'amin has continuous involvement (עשה ועושה ויעשה).

Part 2: Arguments for a Creator

"It's not my expertise; I'll share what's in the Rishonim."

Three caveats:

  1. No proof will be 100%. If it were, there'd be no free will. We seek beyond reasonable doubt.
  2. Unconscious bias.Aldous Huxley, the prominent British writer, admitted that he and his friends wanted the world to have no meaning: meaninglessness freed them from morality, above all sexual morality.
    The Gemara (Sanhedrin 63b): יודעים היו ישראל בעבודה זרה שאין בה ממש, ולא עבדו עבודה זרה אלא להתיר להם עריות בפרהסיא. They knew idols were nothing; they worshipped them to permit forbidden relations openly.
    Rav Elchanan Wasserman on ולא תתורו אחרי לבבכם ואחרי עיניכם: לבבכם = heresy; עיניכם = promiscuity (Berachot). Why in one verse? We stray after the heart so that we can stray after the eyes.
  3. Tanach never tries to prove God. "It's a given throughout Tanach and Talmud. Only the Rishonim began offering proofs."

Argument 1: the cosmological argument

The most common: Rav Saadia, the Rambam (Moreh), Rav Yosef Albo, Rabbeinu Bachya ibn Pakuda (Chovot HaLevavot).

Simply: the computer, the table: made by a technician, a carpenter; who made the carpenter? His parents; theirs… A dependent thing can't be the final cause, so trace back to Hashem.

The standard refutation (Kant): the argument assumes everything needs a cause, then concludes with something uncaused. "Why posit God at the end? Posit an infinitely old universe." "Fair. But at least that's now 50/50: agnostic, not atheist."

The Rambam's deeper version (Moreh)

Step

1

Everything in the universe is physical and finite: bounded, however vast

2

The physical and finite must have a cause and can't be infinitely old

3

So we must posit something non-physical and infinite: the מצוי ראשון

What "infinite" really means:

"33.333…: each step is finite." A blob that doubles every second would, in an hour, fill the universe; in a thousand years be billions of universes. Still, at any moment you could measure it. "Indefinitely extendable, not infinite. Bigness isn't infinity."

Anything measurable is finite. Only zero has no edges, and zero is negation. Infinity is like zero, but existent: no space, no time, no shape.

So the universe, being physical, can be very, very old, but not infinitely. Once there was no space, no time, nothing. We need something above space, time and boundaries, which the physical depends on: that's the infinite God. Not huge: beyond all boundaries.

Against Aristotle. "Until the 1960s, many believed like Aristotle that the universe is infinitely old. Now we know it had a beginning. The Rambam was proven right." He revered Aristotle, even implying something like prophecy, yet argued here. "If I thought Aristotle were right philosophically, I'd reinterpret בראשית ברא אלהים. That's not why I disagree. I think he's philosophically wrong.**"

Robert's challenge: matter is never destroyed, only changed into energy. Infinite forward in time. Then why not backward?

"The Rambam also writes there's no reason the world must end. I hear that. Let me think about it. None of these are foolproof."

Argument 2: design (teleological)

The Rambam and Chovot HaLevavot: the order of the universe, the sun at just the right distance, can't be accident.

Rabbeinu Bachya: you find a page of fresh ink. A dog knocked over the quill: a 0.001% chance. Logic says someone wrote it.

The Rambam (Yesodei HaTorah): love of Hashem comes from contemplating how beautiful and precise the world is.

Avraham (the Midrash): a burning palace: מי בעל הבירה? Who owns this palace? Hashem appeared and told him.

Objection: evil and suffering. "That needs an explanation, a different discussion. It doesn't negate the design we clearly see elsewhere."

Evolution: "Absolutely acceptable, provided you posit a Creator behind the process."

Other arguments

  • Tradition (the Kuzari): the chain back to Sinai; the Torah couldn't have been falsified.
  • The ontological argument (Christian; "I haven't seen it in the Rishonim"): a dragon combines things we know: a horse, birds' wings, fire. But nothing in experience resembles God. How could humans conceive Him if He didn't exist?
  • Fulfilled prophecy. "The State of Israel is one of the strongest proofs. A miracle in itself."

"You don't need to prove love"

Rav Soloveitchik, quoting Kierkegaard: a bride and groom in each other's arms don't need to prove their love. "Some things you just feel."

"A philosopher argued nothing exists except in your imagination; the only certainty is that you yourself think. You can doubt even me. But I know you're here. So too with Hashem. We work through the arguments, but the deepest emunah is when you feel His embrace."

"When I was younger I spent a lot of time on these questions; they bothered me a lot. Some people have doubts, and that's acceptable. But living with Torah and mitzvot, many come to just feel Hashem's presence. That's the deepest emunah."

How He created

"Nothing precludes a frum Jew believing Hashem created through a Big Bang, or through evolution, or both. Principle 1 says there's a Creator of the first matter."

Rebbe Nachman (Likutei Moharan II:52), in substance: it's fitting that there be objections to God's existence, because He is so exalted above our minds. "I don't fully understand it, but: never push people away. If they struggle, help them."

A coming debate. "In January I'll debate a neighbour, a professor of science and avowed atheist, publicly, with the community. He'll know more science than me. But some things you feel, and rationally it's beyond reasonable doubt: where did first matter come from? Where did consciousness begin?"

Part 3: Principle 2 Begins

היסוד השני: יחוד השם יתברך, כלומר שנאמין שזה שהוא סיבת הכל אחד. ואיננו כאחד הזוג, ולא כאחד המין, ולא כאיש אחד שהוא נחלק לאחדים רבים, ולא כגוף הפשוט האחד במנין שמקבל החלוק לאין תכלית. אבל השם יתברך אחד באחדות שאין כמותה אחדות. וזה היסוד השני, מורה עליו מה שנאמר: שמע ישראל ה׳ אלהינו ה׳ אחד.

Note: not simply "God is one," but the Cause of everything is one, and not "one" in any ordinary sense:

Not one as…

Because

One pair

A pair of socks splits into two socks

One species

"The human race" contains many

One person

A person has many parts

One simple body

Even it is endlessly divisible

אחד באחדות שאין כמותה אחדות: one with a unity unlike any other unity. What that is, he doesn't say here; in the Mishneh Torah he writes it's almost impossible to explain.

Yigdal: אחד ואין יחיד כיחודו, נעלם וגם אין סוף לאחדותו

A precise encapsulation: no unity like His, hidden, without end

Ani Ma'amin: שהבורא… הוא יחיד ואין יחידות כמוהו בשום פנים, והוא לבדו אלהינו, היה הוה ויהיה

Same, plus the only God

Summary

  1. Principle 1 has five elements: a Creator; perfect (omnipotent, but can't do the impossible, no imperfection; contra Rebbe Nachman); depending on nothing (Hashem doesn't need our prayers); sustaining everything continuously (no deism: the imagined universe); necessary (emet).
  2. Arguments: never 100% (free will); beware unconscious bias (Huxley; idolatry to permit immorality; heart before eyes). Tanach never tries.
  3. Cosmological (Saadia, Rambam, Albo, Chovot HaLevavot), refined by the Rambam: the finite can't be infinitely old, so the infinite is beyond space and time. Proven right against Aristotle. (Robert: matter seems endless forward; "let me think.")
  4. Design (ink on paper; מי בעל הבירה); evil is a separate question; evolution is fine with a Creator.
  5. Tradition, ontological, prophecy (Israel).
  6. The deepest emunah is felt, like love (Soloveitchik/Kierkegaard). Never push away those who struggle (Rebbe Nachman).
  7. Principle 2 begins: one with a unity like no other: not a pair, species, person or body.
