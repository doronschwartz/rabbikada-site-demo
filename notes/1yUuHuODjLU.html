Topic: The origin and meaning of spilling wine at the Ten Plagues, and the superstitions that grew around it

Sources: Rabbi Eleazar of Worms (the Rokeah); the Arizal

Daily Halacha: Pesah, Wednesday 17 April 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The halacha

  • Sepharadim pour from the cup into a basin at each of the 16 points (dam, esh vetimrot ashan, the ten plagues, and Desakh Adash Beahav), emptying the cup, and the wine is thrown away. Ashkenazim sprinkle with their fingers at the same points.
  • It is first mentioned around the 13th century by Rabbi Eleazar of Worms in Germany, spread among Ashkenazim, was taken on by the Arizal, and through him spread to the Eastern Sepharadim.
  • A later, 19th-century reason: we lessen our joy (wine) because the Egyptians perished, as we are not meant to rejoice at the downfall of the wicked.
  • The original reasons were somewhat superstitious: that the plagues should not fall on us. This led to customs such as the Jews of Kurdistan pouring the wine onto the property of enemies, and the Iraqi custom of naming an enemy at each pour.
  • Whoever has this custom may continue it, but there is nothing dangerous about the wine. Refusing to speak until it has been poured away and the like are superstitious and wrong.
  • The point is to bring the plagues alive, especially for children, and to recall the kindness of HaKadosh Barukh Hu. Western Sepharadim, who did not generally take on the customs of the Arizal, never adopted it.

Sources quoted

  • Rabbi Eleazar of Worms: the first record of the custom.
  • The Arizal: adopted it, so it spread to Eastern Sepharadim.

In one line

Spilling wine at the plagues is a fine custom to bring the story alive, but the superstitions attached to it are wrong.
