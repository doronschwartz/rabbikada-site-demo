It fills a gap in his Derech Hashem series at Tiferet Eyal, where Chapter 3, paragraphs 1-5 were taught but not recorded.

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

After a break, the Rabbi spent the first part of this class reviewing everything learned since the end of October, so that anyone who had missed a class could get "a whole holistic picture", and then continued in Chapter 3.

Chapter 1: the six fundamentals

The Ramhal set out to organise Jewish thought "in a clear and orderly fashion", so that we understand where each idea comes from. The first chapter gives the six fundamentals every Jew needs to know and believe. The group recalled them together:

  • Hashem exists.
  • He is perfect, and beyond our grasp apart from that.
  • His existence is necessary: He has to be.
  • He is absolutely independent, dependent on nothing else.
  • He is simple: all His qualities exist as one essence, not as separate parts.
  • He is one.

Chapter 2: the purpose of creation

The purpose of creation is to bestow good. Hashem is perfect, so the good He wishes to give is the perfect good, and only He has it. Since there cannot be two gods, the solution is to let His creatures cleave to Him and so share in His goodness.

The Rabbi's analogy: imagine the Queen wanted every citizen to have the best she has. She cannot make everyone a queen, but she could allow millions of people to meet her and bask in her presence. In the same way, we cleave to Hashem by becoming better people.

Why not simply let us plug in? Because what we earn becomes part of us. Hashem wants us to own our goodness, not receive it as an external gift, so He created good and bad and gave us the choice. Reward is not arbitrary: by choosing the good we become more attuned to Godliness, and so closer to its root. The chapter ends by placing us between perfection (good character and the use of the intellect) and deficiency (bad traits and materialism), to choose.

Chapter 3: body and soul

Hashem made us from two opposites: a pure, intelligent soul, the neshamah and the yeser tov, and an earthly body, the guf and the yeser hara. There is a constant tug of war between them, and our task is for the soul to take control. The struggle lasts only for our lifetime, while the reward is for eternity. "If you understand everything which we've said this evening in the first 10 minutes, you've basically got the whole Derech Hashem so far."

A participant felt the focus on the world to come was foreign to her. The Rabbi agreed it is a real point: we must not focus on Olam HaBa alone, because Hashem also wants us to make this world better. But every Orthodox thinker agrees that Olam HaBa is the main thing. As the Mishnah says, this world is like a corridor: prepare yourself in the corridor so that you can enter the palace.

Why does the Torah not state it explicitly? Because it was taken for granted, like many other things. When the Torah says Avraham "was gathered to his people", it plainly assumes an afterlife. The idea that your soul outlives your body, he said, "is the most Jewish concept that there is."

In the world to come, the soul leads

In this life the pull is equal, so that we can choose. Think of Neilah on Yom Kippur: the soul feels close to Hashem while the stomach watches the clock. Or a dark, cold morning before a nine o'clock lecture: the body wants to stay warm, the soul thinks of the future. In the world to come the time of work is over. You will have a body, but it will follow the soul rather than pull it down.

The Rambam held that the world to come is souls alone; the Ramhal holds there will be a body, though a refined one. The Rabbi's analogy: someone returns from two years on the moon. One view says throw the spacesuit away; the other says it kept you alive, so keep it with respect, though it no longer controls you.

Adam before the sin

Moving into paragraph 5, the Ramhal says mankind today is not as it was in Adam's time: the sin changed both man and the world. Adam was created exactly as described: body and soul, with good and bad before him, standing evenly between them. The bad was the tree. On a deeper level, the snake's promise that eating would make them like God denied that Hashem alone is in control.

Had Adam chosen the good, he would have been perfected at once, and that would have been the end of the period of work, which then was one day. The next, short paragraph adds that the soul has a further power beyond giving life and intellect: it can refine and purify the body, "elevation after elevation." Had Adam not sinned, his soul would have refined his body, and he would have gone straight to the state of the world to come.

Why do we die?

So why must our bodies die? After the sin, the bad entered Adam himself. A corrupted body cannot be refined by the soul in this life, so it must go through death and then be reunited with the soul, which then elevates it. Had Adam not sinned, there would have been no death. The next class continues with the three ways the sin changed us.

Summary

  • The six fundamentals of Chapter 1: Hashem exists, is perfect, necessary, independent, simple and one.
  • We are created to receive the ultimate good by cleaving to Hashem, and we earn it by choosing it.
  • Body and soul struggle for our lifetime; in the world to come the soul leads and a refined body follows.
  • Had Adam chosen well, his soul would have refined his body at once, without death.
  • Because the bad entered us through the sin, the body must die before the soul can refine it.
