1. Borer: fish and chicken bones

Borer means selecting, and the basic principle is that it only applies when you select from a mixture. Ten identical apples are not a mixture, so setting five aside, even hours ahead, is no problem.

The question is a piece of fish or chicken with its bones. The Mishnah Berurah (Biur Halakhah, siman 319) notes that people regularly remove the bones in the kitchen, and tries to find a justification for them. One argument comes from the Yam Shel Shelomo (the Maharshal): there is no borer with almonds and peanuts in their shells, because it is the way they are made. A piece of fish with bones is one thing, not a mixture of fish and bones, and the bones are not "waste" taken from a mixture.

The author himself calls this only a merit for those who are lenient and says we cannot rely on it, which is why the Ashkenazi custom is to be strict.

Sephardim rule differently. The Ben Ish Hai, the Kaf HaHayim and Rav Ovadia Yoseph all follow the Yam Shel Shelomo, so a Sephardi may take the bones from fish or chicken (and, in the same way, the green stalk from a tomato) because it is one type. - The Ben Ish Hai adds a condition: it must be close to the meal, as part of eating. - Rav Ovadia Yoseph holds that, since there is no mixture, even a long time before the meal it is permitted. - Important limit: this applies only to food that came in its natural state. If several people have eaten, left bones and some chicken on their plates, and these are gathered onto one plate, there is now a new mixture of chicken and bones. Taking out the bones there is borer, even for Sephardim.

A participant asked whether something unwanted is automatically "waste". The Rabbi answered that first there must be a mixture. With five apples and five oranges, the oranges I do not want are waste. Ten identical apples are not a mixture, so the question never begins.

2. Tohen: noise (Hashmaat Kol)

Grinding is a forbidden melakhah. Some authorities held that putting wheat in a millstone before Shabbat, to grind on Shabbat, is forbidden, because the noise makes it look as if the work is being done on Shabbat. The Rema rules that way, and later authorities extended it to anything that makes noise.

Ashkenazim therefore avoid switching on something noisy, such as a washing machine or dishwasher, before Shabbat so that it runs on Shabbat. The Shulhan Arukh is clear that it is permitted to put wheat in the millstone just before Shabbat and let it grind, so Sephardim may use these machines.

The Rabbi added the following: - Rav Ovadia Yoseph permitted it even for Ashkenazim in a case of need. He mentioned soldiers who come home only half an hour before Shabbat and want clothes washed. - An alarm clock is not a problem even for Ashkenazim, because everyone knows it was set before Shabbat. - A radio or similar device that nobody knows was set in advance would be a problem, except in a time of danger or war, when it is fine. - A phone that takes a message is no problem: the caller makes the noise, not me.

3. Kosher: tying knots

The Gemara forbids tying a camel driver's and a sailor's knot on Shabbat. Everyone agrees that a permanent knot is forbidden from the Torah.

The disagreement is what makes a knot a Torah prohibition: - The Rif, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh: both conditions are needed, professional (kesher uman) and permanent (kesher kayam). If only one is present, it is a rabbinic prohibition. If neither is present, it is permitted. - Rashi, the Rosh and the Rema: the essential element is permanence. A knot that is nearly permanent is forbidden by the rabbis, and so is any knot that lasts about as long as that.

The Rema's practice is therefore stricter. Ashkenazim may tie a knot only if it is not professional and will be untied within 24 hours. The Rema mentions both 24 hours and seven days, and the later authorities conclude that 24 hours is the right measure. A double knot is treated as professional, whereas the ordinary single knot and bow of a shoelace is allowed.

Sephardim follow the Rif and the Rambam. Theoretically a Sephardi can tie a non-professional knot even for a longer time, as long as it is not permanent.

Practical summary

  • Borer with fish or chicken bones: Sephardim may remove them as the food is one type, with the Ben Ish Hai requiring it to be near the meal and Rav Ovadia Yoseph allowing it even earlier. Ashkenazim do not rely on this.
  • Do not gather bones and leftovers from several plates and separate them: that is a new mixture, forbidden for everyone.
  • Noise: Sephardim may start a washing machine or dishwasher before Shabbat. Ashkenazim avoid it, except in case of need according to Rav Ovadia Yoseph.
  • An alarm clock is permitted for all.
  • Knots: Ashkenazim tie only a single, non-professional knot that will be undone within 24 hours. Sephardim may tie a non-professional, non-permanent knot even for longer.
