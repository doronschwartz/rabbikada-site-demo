A new idea for Hanukah

The Rabbi opened by calling this one of the most beautiful ideas he had ever learnt, new to him that year. He said it was mostly not his own: it comes largely from Matis Weinberg, a thinker in America who is not widely known. It has many details, so he asked the class to follow closely and promised it would change our Hanukah.

What are the special misvot of Hanukah? Lighting the menorah at night, and saying Hallel in the morning (with the special Torah readings connected to it). They seem unrelated. By the end, he said, we would see they are two sides of the same coin.

The darkness of Greece and the hidden light

The Zohar says the saddik is the foundation on which Hashem created the world, and that this foundation is called light.

At the start of the Torah, "darkness was on the face of the deep," and then Hashem says, "Let there be light." The Midrash reads that darkness as Greece, which darkened the eyes of Israel with decrees, telling them to write on the horn of an ox that they have no portion in the God of Israel. This is a paradox, since we owe the Greeks so much of Western culture: philosophy, science, democracy and art. Yet the hakhamim call it darkness.

The light of the first day cannot be physical; there is no sun yet. The Gemara says that with it Adam could see from one end of the world to the other, and when people sinned Hashem hid it away for the righteous. It is a light of consciousness and understanding. And the saddik here does not just mean a very religious person; it means someone who sees the world in the way he went on to describe.

Greek wisdom takes things apart, down to the smallest particle. The light of the saddik sees each detail as part of a whole: the klal, not only the prat.

The olive An olive looks like simple food. Squeeze it, and it turns out to contain oil, which gives light. We begin to suspect there is light hidden in everything, waiting to be expressed. The Ramhal speaks of this in many places. When you see the world like this, the chaos of the beginning of creation becomes order.

The Midrash asks why the dove brought Noah an olive leaf of all things, bitter and without fragrance. The dove was saying: better something bitter from Hashem's hand than something sweet from yours. For the saddik, the light of the olive, finding meaning and connection to Hashem in the simplest things, is worth trading any nourishment for. "Insight is the soul of the sight."

Thirty-six: the hidden light shone for 36 hours: created with Adam on Friday morning, it shone the twelve hours of Friday and the 24 hours of Shabbat before being hidden. On Hanukah we light 36 candles, not counting the shamash.

The Oral Torah: darkness that becomes light

Midrash Tanhuma says Hashem gave the Torah "al pi hadevarim," by mouth: this is the Oral Torah, which is hard to learn and compared to darkness, yet those who toil in it "see a great light."

Why is it darkness? Because on its face it is endless detail, and details alone are boring. That is why so many people do not enjoy learning Gemara and halakhah. But when you find the principle that makes all the details fit together, "there's nothing like that light." This is the battle between the Greeks and the Hashmonaim. And the 36 candles also correspond, he said, to the 36 tractates of the Talmud.

Minut, heresy: "min" means a species, a category. Heresy is seeing things in compartments rather than as a whole. Science tells you what but never why; however beautiful and unified the description, what remains is "nature bereft of soul." Rabbi Sacks used to say that science tells you what and religion tells you why; this, the Rabbi said, is the depth of that. The Rambam opens his Mishneh Torah with the foundation of all foundations: there is a First Being, a light that envelops all the details.

Tehillim 45, "You are fairer than all men, grace is poured on your lips," which, he noted, Christian theology famously reads as a reference to its own messiah, refers according to Rashi to the true Torah scholars. Their beauty is the life a true hakham brings to lifeless details.

The menorah and the ark: the Greeks defiled the menorah and banned Torah study, yet we never hear that they touched the ark or the Torah scroll. The ark is the Written Torah; the menorah, with its light, is the Oral Torah. A revelation from heaven sitting in the ark did not threaten Greece. What did was "the Torah is not in heaven": human beings grappling with it and bringing it to life. He noted that halakhah requires a person to give up his life rather than worship idols or commit adultery, but not rather than see a Torah scroll desecrated. What we give our lives for is the Torah lived.

A participant suggested the connection to integrity, seeing things as they really are rather than as they appear. The Rabbi agreed: "that's precisely the point."

Hiddur: the beauty of the individual

The halakhah requires one candle per night. Mehadrin, from hiddur, beautifying, adds a candle each night. Hiddur misvah applies to every misvah all year: a beautiful tallit, a beautiful lulav. He mentioned the Spanish and Portuguese custom of tying coloured ribbons on lulavim, red for rabbis, blue for hatanim and yellow for wardens. Hiddur lets you express yourself in the misvah. Only on Hanukah did the hakhamim make it part of how the misvah itself is defined. He stressed that someone who lights one candle a night is doing the misvah fully, lekhathilah, not second rate.

Hodaah, Hallel and light

The Gemara in Berakhot says no one thanked Hashem until Leah, who named her fourth son Yehudah: "This time I will thank Hashem." Of course people had thanked Hashem before. Rashi explains that the matriarchs knew there would be twelve tribes, three each from four wives. Leah's fourth son was more than her share, and for that she gave true hodaah.

Hod also means beauty. True hodaah is thanks for what is especially yours, beyond the normal portion. "Hodaah is to gratitude what hiddur is to misvah": both are the expression of the individual.

The Gemara says that saying Hallel every day is like blasphemy. Why? The Gemara in Pesahim reads "Praise Hashem, all nations" as: if the nations praise Hashem for what He did for us, how much more must we. Hallel is for the special, for kindness that overflows. That is when praise becomes true hodaah.

Finally, the root of Hallel (he, lamed, lamed) also means to shine: "Behilo nero alei roshi," when His lamp shone on my head, in Iyov. Praise and light are the same word. A hodaah so overflowing that, as we say in Nishmat, even mouths as full as the sea could not express it, has one source: the primordial light of creation, containing all potential. He mentioned briefly that this fits beautifully with the Big Bang.

So the menorah brings out the primordial light, and the Hallel expresses the special, overflowing thanks that goes back to that same light. "It's all one thing."

Questions

  • A participant connected the idea to the Ramhal on body and soul. The Rabbi added Yefet, father of Greece, who covered his father's body and was rewarded with burial for his descendants: care for the body alone. Shem was rewarded with sisit, whose blue thread leads you from the colour to the heavens and to Hashem: seeing the soul within.
  • Asked whether the menorah meant is the Temple's or ours, he said the concept of light is the menorah of the Temple, while the specific 36 candles only fit our Hanukah menorah.

Summary

  • Greek wisdom, for all its beauty, sees the world as fragmented detail; the hidden light of the first day is the insight that sees the whole and the soul within it.
  • The olive shows that light is hidden in everything, and the Oral Torah is darkness that becomes great light when its details are understood as one.
  • Hiddur in misvot and hodaah in thanks are both the expression of the individual, built into the very definition of the Hanukah lights.
  • Hallel means both praise and shining: the overflowing thanks of Hanukah and the light of the menorah are one.
  • The Rabbi credited most of these ideas to Matis Weinberg.
