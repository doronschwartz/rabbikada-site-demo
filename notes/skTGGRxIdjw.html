One chapter for a whole festival

With Shavuot just over a week away, the class turned to Siman 494, "the order of prayer on the festival of Shavuot". Pesah and Sukkot each fill dozens of chapters of the Shulhan Arukh; Shavuot has one chapter of three paragraphs.

For Pesah and Sukkot the Shulhan Arukh gives a date. Here it gives none: Shavuot is simply "the fiftieth day of the Omer".

The prayers are like those of Pesah, but with the full Hallel. Two Sifrei Torah are taken out, and five are called up for the Ten Commandments. The maftir is from Parashat Pinehas, which describes the offerings of Shavuot and the two loaves. The haftarah is the heavenly chariot of Yehezkel, because the giving of the Torah was a time of great prophecy.

Waiting for nightfall: where it comes from

The Mishnah Berurah adds that we delay Arvit on Shavuot night until nightfall, so that the seven weeks of the Omer are "complete" (temimot). The Rabbi said plainly: "I have to tell you, this is very difficult," and traced its history.

  • Rabbi Avraham Horowitz, the father of the Shelah HaKadosh, about 450 years ago, was the first to say it. The Torah requires seven complete weeks. If you bring in Shavuot early, as we do on a summer Friday night, you cut the last hours off the forty-ninth day.
  • The Magen Avraham (Rabbi Avraham Gombiner, about 400 years ago), the main Ashkenazi commentator on Orah Hayim, put it into halacha, but only for Kiddush: wait until nightfall to make Kiddush.
  • The Taz (Rabbi David Segal), about fifty years later, went further. Arvit already brings in the next evening, so Arvit too should wait for nightfall.
  • The Mishnah Berurah follows the Taz.

These authorities lived in Poland and Belarus, with long summer evenings like England, and knew exactly what they were asking: Arvit at ten and a meal at eleven. In Gateshead, the Rabbi recalled, that left perhaps an hour for learning before dawn. "So you always have to understand where they're writing, what they're writing."

Why the Sefaradim never took it on

Already when the idea was first raised, others disagreed. The Rabbi found two arguments especially strong.

First, the Rishonim and the Shulhan Arukh say clearly that on Pesah night you must wait for nightfall to eat the massah, and on Sukkot night to eat in the sukkah, because those are Torah misvot tied to the night. If the same applied to Shavuot, why would they not say so? "That's a very strong ta'anah in my eyes."

Second, there is a misvah of Tosefet Shabbat ve-Yom Tov, adding time to the beginning of Shabbat and Yom Tov. How does that fit with keeping the forty-ninth day whole? Those who keep the custom answer that the added time only forbids melakhah and does not really begin the next day. Sefaradi authorities wrote that there is no such custom: the Degel Mahaneh Efrayim, by Rabbi Efrayim Laniado, a Hakham of Aleppo, and Rabbi Hayim Palachi in Turkey. They brought Yom Tov in early, went home to eat, and came back to learn all night, which an early start makes easier. The Ben Ish Hai does bring the custom of waiting, but Rav Ovadia Yoseph wrote that there is no need, especially in countries like this one.

For the Rabbi this is a question of competing values. Making women and children wait until eleven at night for Kiddush and their meal takes away from simhat Yom Tov, which is itself a Torah misvah. "You want to be mahmir, from an Aharon from 500 years ago. Beautiful. But then it's going to affect other things." If you can wait, and your wife and older children are happy to, that is lovely; but it has to be balanced.

Someone asked why this is different from praying Arvit early on a weekday. The Rabbi explained that an early weekday Arvit does not make the next date begin, whereas Shabbat and Yom Tov have the power to draw the day in early.

In an ordinary year, he said, his practice is Minhah and Arvit at eight, so that people reach home after sunset and make Kiddush at about nine.

When Shavuot falls on Mosei Shabbat

In 2022 Shavuot began on Saturday night, which raises a different problem. Arvit on Mosei Shabbat includes Havdalah, so praying Arvit at half past eight would mean making Havdalah well before Shabbat ends.

The Shulhan Arukh allows Arvit and Havdalah from plag haminhah on Mosei Shabbat only in extenuating circumstances, such as a traveller who must be at the bus stop the moment Shabbat ends. Another is a mourner whose relative died on Shabbat: after Shabbat he will be an onen and unable to pray Arvit or make Havdalah, so he does both before Shabbat ends. Elderly people and children who go to sleep early are also in this category. An early Havdalah is made over wine alone, without the flame and without besamim, because it is still Shabbat.

So the plan for the congregation was Arvit at 9:45, relying on the earlier opinions about nightfall for the purpose of prayer, and then Kiddush at home after proper nightfall, about 10:10. An elderly or unwell person who cannot wait should pray, make Kiddush, eat and go to sleep. On Mosei Shabbat his Kiddush has four blessings instead of five: wine, Kiddush, Havdalah and Shehehiyanu, without the blessing on the flame. If he finishes his meal after nightfall, he can say the blessing on the flame then.

Someone asked about eating before Yom Tov, with Kiddush and bread later. Not eating from the tenth hour of Erev Yom Tov is midat hasidut, a good practice, but strictly it is permitted.

Staying up all night

The Mishnah Berurah quotes the Zohar: the pious stayed awake all Shavuot night learning Torah, and the Ari promised that whoever does so will have a good year. The reason given is that the Bnei Israel slept the night before the Torah was given, and we repair it.

The custom is first recorded in the Zohar, and the Magen Avraham was the first to bring it into halacha. "It's a good thing to do if you have the ability to do so." But, the Rabbi stressed, "there is no breach of halacha if you don't do so."

Hakham Gaguine, author of the Keter Shem Tov and later Hakham of the Spanish and Portuguese community, wrote that in Cairo the rabbis and dayanim spent the night "hitting each other" to stay awake, and that the custom in his time was to stay up half the night. A senior rabbi in Gateshead told the Rabbi he goes to sleep on Shavuot night, ever since he stayed up once as a young man and woke at six the next evening. "You have to be honest with yourself."

What should one learn? The Zohar describes "touring" the whole Torah: Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim, then Gemara, Midrash, Zohar and Kabbalah. That is the Tikkun Leil Shavuot. Its core is a few verses from the beginning and end of each parashah and a little of each of the other books. The long additions can be left out, and the essential Tikkun can take about an hour or an hour and a half. The Rabbi's own view was that whoever stays up should do the Tikkun, as those who started the custom did, and then learn properly. For those not staying up, he suggested a short Tikkun of about an hour, then sleep and Shahrit later. The custom is for men, and sitting around chatting all night is no better than going to sleep.

Berakhot through the night

A berakhah made on food or drink early in the night covers the whole sitting, even hours later, as long as you stay in the building and did not decide you had finished. Leaving for a walk and coming back would need a new berakhah. Someone who stayed awake all night washes his hands in the morning without the berakhah, as the Sefaradi practice follows the opinions that tie it to sleep.

The Mishnah Berurah also allows Shahrit on Shavuot morning as soon as dawn breaks, which is why many Ashkenazi communities begin very early.

Practical summary

  • Shavuot has one chapter in the Shulhan Arukh: full Hallel, the Ten Commandments, maftir from Pinehas, and the chariot of Yehezkel for the haftarah.
  • Waiting for nightfall to pray Arvit and make Kiddush is an Ashkenazi custom from the last 450 years; Sefaradi authorities and Rav Ovadia Yoseph did not require it. Where it costs the family their Yom Tov meal, the Rabbi did not think Sefaradim should insist on it.
  • When Shavuot begins on Mosei Shabbat, the community prays Arvit late (9:45 in 2022) and makes Kiddush after nightfall. The elderly or unwell may pray, make Kiddush and eat early, with four blessings and no blessing on the flame.
  • Staying up all night is a good custom, not an obligation. Do it only if it will not ruin the next day.
  • If you stay up, do the core of the Tikkun and then learn properly. One berakhah on refreshments covers the night unless you leave the building.
