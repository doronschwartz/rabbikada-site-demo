From principles to practice

The first three shiurim covered the principles of deciding Hilchot Shabbat: melekhet mahshevet, permanence, productiveness, and shinui. For the next three the Rabbi chose melakhot that apply those principles, that show stark differences between Ashkenazim and Sefaradim, and about which there are common misconceptions. Planned for tonight were Kosher, Zorea and Shohet; there was time for the first two.

Kosher: what makes a knot forbidden

Untying mirrors tying. Whatever level applies to tying a knot applies to untying it: a Torah-level knot is a Torah prohibition to untie, a rabbinic knot is rabbinic, and a permitted knot may be untied.

In the Mishkan. Two explanations: the nets used to catch the hilazon for the dye, or knotting the threads together during weaving.

The Mishnah says one is liable for "the knots of camel drivers and the knots of sailors". The camel driver's ring through the camel's nose, and the sailor's knot at the end of the boat, were tied once and left for years. The Gemara reconciles three baraitot: one Torah-level, one rabbinic, one permitted. The Rishonim explain them differently.

The Rabbi noted that the Sefaria translation, "as they are permanent", follows Rashi and the Rosh; the Rif and the Rambam would not translate it so.

The Shulhan Arukh (317:1) rules like the Rif and the Rambam. The Biur Halacha reads him simply: a knot that is neither professional nor permanent is permitted "even if made for a period of time". So tying a bag of hallot in the freezer with an ordinary knot for a few weeks should be permitted. This was the view of the Maamar Mordekhai, Rabbi Akiva Eiger and many others.

A story. The Maamar Mordekhai, Rav Mordekhai Karmi, asked the Hida for an approbation. The Hida wrote a glowing one, without reading the book, which turned out to criticise his own Mahazik Berakhah. The Hida later answered those criticisms in the Birkei Yoseph. "You only give a haskamah when you read the book."

The Rema rules like Rashi and the Rosh: beyond seven days is certainly rabbinic, and below seven days there is a dispute, some holding that more than 24 hours is forbidden. So Ashkenazim say any knot tied on Shabbat must be tied with the intention to untie it within 24 hours.

A stricter reading. The Shulhan Arukh HaRav reads the Rambam as forbidding even a simple knot that has some permanence. The Rabbi said this is not the simple reading, and was surprised that Hazon Ovadia takes it into account and says, in the first instance, to have in mind to untie within seven days.

Shoelaces, double knots and the Sefer Torah

Shoelaces. The Shulhan Arukh says a bow is not a knot at all and is permitted. The Rema adds that even a single knot under the bow is permitted, which is exactly a tied shoelace. The Mishnah Berurah brings Aharonim who disagree, which is why Ashkenazim say to untie shoelaces within 24 hours. The Rabbi counted how many doubts that involves. For Sefaradim there is no reason to be strict: tying shoelaces is permitted, certainly with intention to untie within seven days, and arguably for longer, because a bow is not a knot.

Double knots. The Shiltei HaGibborim, quoted by the Rema, says that since we do not know what counts as professional, a double knot may be one, and Ashkenazim do not tie a double knot even for a minute. The Hida dismisses this: few people were particular, and a double knot is not a craftsman's knot. Hazon Ovadia and Hakham Ben Sion follow the Hida; the Ben Ish Hai is strict, "as is his way". So for Sefaradim a double knot may be tied with the intention to untie within seven days.

The Sefer Torah. Ashkenazim, Spanish and Portuguese, and some Moroccan communities tie the bands of the Sefer Torah. Someone said the bands are usually a single knot and a bow, which is simply permitted. A Sefer Torah taken out only on the festivals is harder, since the knot stays for months. The Rabbi said it is still permitted, because the Shulhan Arukh, from the Gemara, permits a rabbinic knot for the sake of a misvah, and tying the Sefer Torah securely is a misvah need. Even a double knot is permitted, as long as it will be untied in the foreseeable future. A Sefer Torah being put away for genizah is a permanent knot.

Sisit and the eruv. Retying the loose knot at the end of the sisit during prayer is a permanent knot, which is forbidden even if you intend to untie it after Shabbat, because such knots are normally left. The same is true of an eruv that came down. Ashkenazim allow asking a non-Jew to retie it for the public need. For Sefaradim it is simpler: it is not professional, so only rabbinic, and for a misvah need a rabbinic knot is permitted. He was unsure whether retying sisit counts as a misvah need, since the misvah requires only one knot.

Rubbish bags. Some forbid knotting a bin bag that will never be untied, as a permanent knot. Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach (Shulhan Shlomo) permits it, since it is as if it will be undone. A single knot alone, with nothing on top, is certainly permitted.

Zorea: water on the grass

Avot and toladot. The Rambam explains that an av is the melakhah exactly as in the Mishkan, or very close, like planting seeds or a tree; watering is a toladah. "It's a bad mistake to think that a toladah is only rabbinic." Both are Torah prohibitions; the difference only mattered for sacrifices.

Washing hands outdoors. At a summer meal in the garden, may you wash for bread over the grass? The class answered: pesik reisha. The Rabbi agreed, and since it is a Torah prohibition, it is forbidden even if you have no interest in watering, as in your friend's garden. So is pouring out a cup of water onto the lawn. But washing over concrete so that the water trickles onto the grass is permitted, because that is pesik reisha with gerama. The prohibition applies to water, not wine; with drinks that are mostly water today, he thought it probably applies.

Pouring water on the lawn while it is raining? Many permit it, since the grass is already full, though some Aharonim are strict. The Rabbi agreed the logic is with the lenient view, which the Petah HaDevir holds.

Potted plants at home. Watering is forbidden, but only rabbinically, since a plant in a pot does not draw from the ground, unless the pot is perforated and on the ground.

Flowers in a vase

The Rema, quoting the Maharil, says one may put flowers in water on Shabbat, but not flowers that will open in the water. The Rabbi found this very strange: Zorea applies only to something attached to the ground. The Maharikash and others dismiss it; others explain that it only looks like planting. Hazon Ovadia is strict in the first instance not to put closed flowers in water, though those who do have what to rely on.

Filling the vase. The Mishnah Berurah adds that even open flowers may only be put into water already in the vase, not into newly drawn water, based on the Mishnah about returning a lulav to water on Yom Tov, because of tirhah, unnecessary exertion. The Rabbi said this is hard to understand today, when water comes from a tap. Hazon Ovadia brings the Rashba: tirhah only applies to something mukseh, like the lulav after its misvah. Flowers are not mukseh, so one may fill the vase and put open flowers in.

Practical summary

  • Untying follows tying. The Rif, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh need a knot to be both permanent and professional to be a Torah prohibition.
  • Ashkenazim untie any knot within 24 hours. Sefaradim: a simple knot is certainly fine for seven days, and arguably longer.
  • Shoelaces with a bow are permitted for Sefaradim; a double knot is permitted with intention to untie within seven days. Ashkenazim avoid double knots entirely.
  • Sefer Torah bands may be tied even with a double knot, as a misvah need, if they will be untied in the foreseeable future. Do not retie a loose sisit knot.
  • Do not pour water or wash hands over grass; over concrete that drains into the grass is permitted.
  • Sefaradim may fill a vase and put in open flowers; Hazon Ovadia advises not putting in closed flowers.
