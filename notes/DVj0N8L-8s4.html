Topic: Accepting immigrants from the former Soviet Union who say they are Jewish

Sources: Rav Ovadiah Yoseph, Teshuvah; the Ritva; Tosafot; Piskei Din of the Rabbanut and the Beth Din HaGadol

Daily Halacha: Even HaEzer

The halacha

  • Rav Ovadiah Yoseph wrote a thorough Teshuvah on Jews arriving after the fall of the Soviet Union. The Ritva's reason (people do not lie about what can be verified) does not apply, since this is hard to verify.
  • But the main reason is Tosafot's: most who come as Jews are Jewish, which applies here too. So he ruled they can be accepted and marry into the community, and strictly need not even be checked, though if something seems suspicious one must check.
  • This was the Rabbanut's original approach, as the written rulings (Piskei Din) of the courts, especially the Beth Din HaGadol, show.
  • Many came under the Law of Return (Hok HaShevut) without claiming to be halachically Jewish, which is fine; the question concerns those who claim to be Jewish.
  • Later rulings grew stricter: checking for some Yiddish, traditions or documents. One Dayan wrote that most now coming are not Jewish and come for financial reasons, and there were forged documents.
  • Today Batei Din in Israel and beyond are far more reserved. The Rabbanut has a department to verify documents, and the organisation Shorashim is a main body doing this work.
  • Without a parents' ketubah, Soviet documents marking someone as Jewish (his understanding is this required both parents to be Jewish) are used to find a grandparent and trace down through birth certificates. Documents of secular courts are accepted as proof, as are archives linking relatives.
  • The basic halacha believes such people, but circumstances have changed.

In one line

Rav Ovadiah Yoseph accepted Soviet immigrants who said they were Jewish, but forgeries and changed circumstances have led Batei Din to require thorough documentary proof today.
