Topic: The historical setting of Purim, real and illusory kavod, the laws of mishloah manot and gifts to the poor, and Amalek as doubt Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal, on Zoom, Tuesday 15 March 2022, on the eve of Purim

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where Purim sits in history

Many people assume Purim happened after Hanukah because it comes later in the year. In fact Purim is centuries earlier. The Rabbi traced the background: the Mishkan, which stood for over four hundred years; the first Beit HaMikdash built by Shlomo HaMelekh; the split into a northern kingdom of ten tribes and a southern kingdom of Yehudah and Binyamin with the Kohanim and Leviim; the exile of the ten tribes by Assyria; and the destruction of the Mikdash and exile of the south to Babylonia, modern Iraq.

Yirmiyahu had promised a return after seventy years, but people built communities in exile and spread into Persia, and many gave up hope. When Persia overtook Babylon, Ahashverosh, by a miscalculation the Gemara describes, concluded the seventy years had passed and the Jews would never return. Convinced his was the eternal empire, he threw a feast using the vessels Nevukhadnesar had taken from the Mikdash. Mordekhai warned the people not to attend, since enjoying a party served with the vessels of the Temple we were mourning was wrong. The Gemara says that this sin led to the decree.

Who gives weight to what

The theme that runs through the whole Megillah, the Rabbi said, is a conflict over what kavod really is. We carry a divine spark and seek perfection, and we want others to recognise it. That becomes dangerous when our worth depends on others' opinions.

  • Ahashverosh calls Vashti to display her beauty to the princes of 127 provinces. On the simple reading she refuses to be objectified. His advisers warn that every wife will despise her husband, and they advise her death.
  • Haman has everything, second only to the king, but one man will not bow, and he burns with anger. He comes to the palace at night with his plan. The Rabbi's aside: if you have a plan you thought of at night, wait until morning before you send that email. After parading Mordekhai through the streets, Haman goes home mourning and complains to his wife Zeresh and his friends. Later he boasts to them about his wealth.

Mordekhai, by contrast, had a bombshell: the plot of Bigtan and Teresh. Imagine being the only person to know of a plot against a world leader; you would become famous overnight. Mordekhai tells Esther, and Esther tells the king "in the name of Mordekhai." From this Hazal learn the principle seen on every page of the Talmud: quote people accurately, and if you are not sure, say so. And Mordekhai, the leading sage of his time, follows the instructions of Esther, whom he raised.

Kavod comes from kaved, heavy: it means weight, what we give weight to. Building on a talk by Rabbi Sacks, the Rabbi explained that material goods are zero-sum. If I share political power, I have less; if my wife must be the most beautiful, everyone else must be less so. That leads to fighting. Spiritual things, love, friendship, influence and truth, grow the more they are shared.

The same applies to arguments. You can tell whether people argue to find the truth or to be right. In the study hall, sages argue fiercely and end in love, because each has helped the other see the truth. When you argue to win, every argument makes you angrier. Honour that only exists because others lack it is illusion, kavod hamedumeh. Real kavod is truth, influence and love.

A participant noted a view that the food at the feast was not kosher; the Rabbi agreed that there are two opinions.

The laws of Purim

There are four misvot: reading the Megillah, mishloah manot, the festive meal and gifts to the poor.

Megillah. It is read by night and by day, and the daytime reading is the main one, instituted by Mordekhai and the Sanhedrin. The night reading was added later by the Hakhamim. So anyone who comes at night should certainly come in the day.

Mishloah manot. The Rambam writes that one should send two portions of meat, or two kinds of cooked food, to a friend. Why meat? The Rishonim give two reasons for the misvah: to make sure everyone has food for the meal, or to increase friendship, answering Haman's claim that we are scattered and divided. The Rambam's wording shows he follows the first reason. So the Rabbi recommended that at least one parcel contain real food for the meal, not just sweets to snack on for months.

The two reasons lead to practical differences:

  • Giving to someone you fell out with: for friendship, this is the ideal, and the poskim say it is preferable.
  • Anonymous gifts: they provide food but build no relationship.
  • A door slammed in your face: you provided no food, but you tried to build the relationship.

In practice we try to satisfy both: give food usable at the meal, and do not give anonymously. Asked why some send children with the parcels, he explained that the Mishnah Berurah recommends sending one through an agent, but the recipient must still know who sent it.

The meal and the poor. He read the Rambam's words, "worth repeating year in, year out": it is better to increase gifts to the poor than to spend more on one's meal and mishloah manot. There is no greater joy than gladdening the hearts of the poor, orphans, widows and converts, and one who does so resembles the Shekhinah, who revives the spirit of the lowly.

Matanot laevyonim means one gift each to two poor people. An ani has little; an evyon has nothing at all. He recalled with sadness a poster in Israel advertising "mehudar" poor people, as if they were an etrog. Misvot between people should be done because we care, not as a way to collect a misvah; that is why there is no berakhah on charity or visiting the sick. Imagine telling someone you are visiting them because God commanded it. The poskim write that someone who struggles to make it to the end of the month is sufficient. Each gift should be worth a meal; in 2022 he suggested roughly £7.50 to £10 per meal, about £20 per person, given on the day of Purim itself, and anyone distributing on our behalf must give it out on the day.

Amalek and doubt

Why remember Amalek thousands of years later, when we do not know who Amalek is? The Mishnah in Rosh Hashanah asks whether Moshe's raised hands won the war. No: when Israel looked upward and subjected their hearts to their Father in heaven, they prevailed. The same is said of the copper serpent. Both lessons come in the context of Amalek.

The Malbim distinguishes two words for fool. The kesil knows deep down that the Torah is true but invents doubts to justify what he wants to do; there is hope for him. The evil denies that truth exists at all: everything is a question, nothing is objective, and so any behaviour can be justified. The Rabbi called this view, that there are no objective morals, very troubling. The letters of evil, alef vav yod lamed, are the letters of ulai, "perhaps."

That is Amalek. He attacked the weary stragglers at the back, and forty years later came disguised as another nation, so we would not know whom we were fighting. Amalek and safek, doubt, share the numerical value of 240. The cure in both cases was to look up: to be firm and certain. If we were truly convinced of what we believe, we would not do most of what we do wrong. Purim was won by people of total commitment, Esther and Mordekhai, who risked their lives.

He ended wishing everyone a meaningful Purim, in which we see Hashem's hand in our lives, and praying for an end to modern-day Hamans and for world peace.

Summary

  • Purim happened in the Persian exile, centuries before Hanukah.
  • The Megillah is a clash between honour that cannot be shared and honour that grows when shared.
  • Hear the Megillah by day; give mishloah manot that can be eaten at the meal and is not anonymous.
  • Gifts to the poor come first, and should be given out of care, on Purim day.
  • Amalek is doubt; we defeat it by looking up with firm commitment, as Esther and Mordekhai did.
