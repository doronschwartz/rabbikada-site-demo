These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The whole Siman is about one question: when a man divorces his wife, under what circumstances may he not remarry her? Generally he may: if he changes his mind they can marry again. But there are exceptions, which the Rabbi went through in three groups. Siman 9 (Katlanit) was finished last time, and Siman 8, which is very short, was left for later.

Seif 1: she married or was betrothed to another man

The Shulhan Arukh says that if he divorced her and she then had relations with other men, without any marriage (zenut), he may take her back. The reverse is also true: if she had a non-marital relationship with one man, then married a second and was divorced, she may return to the first. A boyfriend and girlfriend, even living together, never had anything formal.

But if she was divorced and then married, or even only betrothed, to a second man, and the second man divorced her or died, she cannot return to the first. The Torah says she may not return after she was "defiled" and the second husband was with her (vehayta leish aher). The Gemara asks whether this includes only kiddushin. Rabbi Yose ben Kipar says no, but the Shulhan Arukh and all the Rishonim rule like the Hakhamim: even kiddushin alone is enough. What matters is a formal relationship, a havayah, even only kiddushin. Relations outside any formal framework do not count.

The Rema, from the Rosh: even if the second marriage is doubtful (a safek kiddushin), she cannot return. The Helkat Mehokek adds that this applies even when the first get was only a kol (a rumour of divorce) and not a real get, since the rumour itself is enough to cause concern.

Civil marriage. The Rabbi asked about a woman who was married with kiddushin and nisuin, was divorced, and lived with a second man in a civil marriage only. This is the same question we dealt with in Siman 6 (civil marriage in the context of havayah). The same answer applies: the civil marriage is not considered a kol for this purpose, so she may return to her first husband.

In practice. The Rabbi noted that this does happen: couples who divorced and then remarried each other. But from Seif 3 to the end of the Siman there are cases where he cannot take her back even if she did not marry anyone else.

Seif 2: a deaf-mute (heresh) and a regular person

The Rambam rules that a heresh who divorced his wife cannot retake her if she married another, just as with a regular man. Hazal made the heresh's kiddushin like a Torah kiddushin, and that is a rabbinic enactment.

The Rambam's novelty: if a regular man (pikeah) divorced his wife and she married a heresh and was divorced by him, the first husband may take her back. The Ravad disagrees. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam: Hazal equated the heresh's kiddushin with a regular one, but not to the disadvantage of the pikeah. When the heresh himself divorces, he is treated like anyone else. But the heresh's kiddushin will not be used to prevent a hearing man from taking his wife back. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam, against the Ravad.

Seifim 3 to the end: divorcing because of a problem

Now a different situation: no second marriage. The Mishnah lists four reasons for a divorce: shem ra (a bad reputation, rumour), nedarim (vows she made), ailonit (she cannot bear children), and roah dam mehmat tashmish (bleeding after relations). The Rabbi asked what is the problem with taking her back, and went through the Gemara.

Shem ra and neder. The Hakhamim say he can never take her back. Rabbi Yehuda is lenient: for a neder, only if it was publicly known. The Gemara gives two reasons for the Hakhamim:

  1. Kilkul. Rashi explains: if he divorced her because of a rumour and later learns it was false, he might say "I only divorced her because of the rumour, and had I known it was false I would not have divorced her". Then people say the get was not valid, she was never divorced from the first husband, and the children of her second marriage are mamzerim. We prevent this by telling him: once you divorce, you can never take her back. So he will do his investigation beforehand.
  2. Kenas. A penalty, so that women do not bring on themselves a bad reputation or make vows constantly. On this reason, even a divorce with no words at all counts.

The reason of the Hakhamim matters: if kilkul, it applies only if he said why he was divorcing her. If kenas, it applies even if he said nothing.

The contradiction. The second Mishnah (about ailonit) seems to reverse the positions: here Rabbi Yehuda is strict and the Hakhamim lenient. Three answers in the Gemara:

  • Shemuel: swap them around.
  • Abaye: answers the contradiction in Rabbi Yehuda only.
  • Rava: asks that Abaye did not answer the Hakhamim. Rava's answer: the Hakhamim in the first Mishnah follow Rabbi Meir and a tenai kefel. There, he repeated himself: "I am divorcing you because of the shem ra, and if you did not have a shem ra I would not divorce you." In the ailonit Mishnah he only said the reason once.

The Tosafot add that this is not a real tenai kefel: if it were a real condition, there would be no get at all and she would be forbidden to others. It is a repeated statement, which is why the Rabbi called it a tenai kefel "in inverted commas".

Three shittot in the Rishonim

The Rabbi laid out three approaches. Each applies to shem ra, neder and ailonit.

Opinion

When he cannot take her back

The Rosh (following Rava, like Rabbi Meir)

Only if he repeated his words (a double statement)

The Rif and the Ramban (as the Beit Yoseph understands them, following Abaye and the Hakhamim)

If he said the reason even once; if he said nothing, he may take her back

The Rambam

Even if he said nothing at all. He rules like both reasons, kilkul and kenas

The Rambam's view is difficult: if he said nothing, there is no kilkul, and no kenas for shelo yihyu benot Yisrael perusot (a woman should not be at risk). The Maggid Mishneh explains it for ailonit: since she cannot have children, it is as if he said the reason. The Beit Shemuel reads the Rambam differently: for ailonit, if he said nothing, he may take her back.

The Shulhan Arukh (Seif 3). The Shulhan Arukh brings the Rosh's view first, then the others as yesh omerim, so it is not stated outright. The Rabbi said the Pithei Teshuvah says the halacha is not like the Rambam: a divorce without any reason stated permits her return. What is left undecided is whether we follow the Rif (said once) or the Rosh (repeated).

Seif 4: bleeding

If a man divorced his wife because she bleeds constantly after relations, everyone agrees, even the Rosh, that he cannot take her back. The reason is the kilkul concern. The Rabbi gave a striking implication: even if it were later proven medically that she had many relations without bleeding (so that the hazakah from Hilchot Niddah was broken), he still cannot take her back.

Seif 5: if he did remarry her

All four cases: if she was married to someone else, he cannot take her back (Seif 1). But if she did not, and he divorced her for one of these reasons and then remarried her, he does not have to divorce her (lo yosi). The Rabbi explained the Yerushalmi versions (a dispute of nusahot, as with Siman 8):

Ramban

Rashba

Leniency

If he did nisuin, he stays even if Beit Din warned him

If he did kiddushin, he stays

Stringency

Kiddushin alone is not enough

If Beit Din warned him, he must divorce even after nisuin

The Shulhan Arukh does not specify and treats it as a safek derabbanan, so we can be lenient on both. The Rabbi said everyone agrees that if Beit Din warned him not to remarry her and he only did kiddushin, he must divorce her. In that case he should not consummate the marriage. If he did nisuin despite a warning, or kiddushin without a warning, he can stay.

The warning. When a man divorces for any of these reasons, Beit Din tells him in advance that he is divorcing her and cannot take her back forever. This is so that he cannot later say he was only testing, or reconsidering. His mind is made up.

Seif 6: the man's own vow

All of the above concerns reasons that relate to the woman. A man's neder is different. If he divorced her because of a neder he himself made, he can take her back: the neder was that he would divorce her, and now it is fulfilled. A neder she made is the problem.

Practical summary

  • A man may take back a woman who had only non-marital relations after the divorce; if she married or was betrothed to another man, he may not.
  • Civil marriage is not a havayah, so a woman who lived with another man in a civil marriage only may return to her first husband.
  • If he divorced her because of shem ra, nedarim, ailonit or bleeding, there are three views (Rosh, Rif and Ramban, Rambam). The Pithei Teshuvah: not like the Rambam; undecided between the Rif and the Rosh.
  • For bleeding, everyone agrees he cannot remarry her.
  • If he remarried her anyway, he stays with her, except kiddushin only after a warning from Beit Din, where he should divorce.
  • A man who divorced because of his own neder may take her back.
