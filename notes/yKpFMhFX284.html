Where we are

Essay 1 was about proving that there is a Creator. Essay 2 is about God Himself, and specifically His unity. Last week the class learned the first three chapters. They gave five attributes of Kadosh Barukh Hu (one, alive, omnipotent, omniscient and incomparable), argued against dualism, and explained verses that might suggest two deities.

This week covered Chapters 4 to 7.

An idea Saadiah introduces: the attributes are one

Before the text, the Rabbi introduced a concept that Rav Saadiah is the first to state explicitly, and which the Rambam later develops at length in the Moreh Nevuchim. Academics call it negative theology. In simple terms: every attribute we use for God, including "alive", "powerful" and "wise", is only a way for our tongues to express something. In Hashem Himself these are not separate things. It is all one.

To divide God into parts is more than limiting Him. It breaks His unity. A human being can be wise or not wise, alive or dead, so there is the person and there is his intelligence and his life. In Hashem there is nothing like this. The Ramhal later used the word peshitut, simplicity: everything is combined in one. This applies to the thirteen attributes of mercy too. Hashem does not have thirteen things; there is only what appears to us as thirteen.

Chapter 4: three attributes, one reality

Rav Saadiah starts from Essay 1. We have established that there is a Creator. Logic then tells us that He must be powerful, since only the all-powerful can create; alive, since only the living has power; and knowing, since a creator must know what He is making. Our minds understand these three together, at once.

But our language has no single word for all three, so we use three words. Do not think they are three separate attributes in Hashem. It all falls under the one fact that He is the Creator. When we say "Hashem is a Creator", we are not saying there is Hashem and there is also a creator who is added to Him. God created, and that is all one thing. The same is true of "living", "omnipotent" and "omniscient".

The principle behind this is that being divisible into elements belongs only to physical matter, which science keeps finding can be split further. Something spiritual cannot be split, and the Creator of everything physical is beyond all diversity.

An analogy the Rabbi often uses: sunlight is one. Ten people at the beach wearing ten colours of sunglasses will see ten colours of light. That does not mean there is diversity in the sun. The light is the same, and it is perceived differently by different people. So too merciful and powerful describe how Hashem reaches us, not parts of Him.

A question about the soul

Someone asked: if only physical things can be divided, what about the human soul, which has several levels such as nefesh? Is that not splitting it?

The Rabbi called it an excellent question and said he does not have a perfect answer. The Ramhal himself writes that the soul is one, and elsewhere describes its five levels. So we must say something along the lines that the division is from our perspective, as the soul relates to us. This needs more analysis, but that is the direction.

Chapter 5: why the Trinity fails

Rav Saadiah now spends three chapters on the Christian doctrine of the Trinity: philosophical proofs in Chapter 5, verses in Chapter 6, and the different Christian groups in Chapter 7. The Rabbi went through it quickly, since belief in the Trinity is not our main challenge, but the book aims to be complete.

Rav Saadiah explains that Christian thinkers reason like this: a creator must be alive and wise, so His life and His wisdom must be two things separate from Him. So there are three: God the Father, His spirit or life, and His wisdom, which became manifest in the Son. He responds as follows.

  • Is it physical or not? If they mean a physical Trinity, that is clearly mistaken, as everything about God having no body applies to them. If they mean a spiritual one, but with three parts, then they are speaking of diversity, and diversity is a property of physical things. They are only using other words.
  • A beautiful proof. We know that a person and his life are separate, because we see people live and die. We know that a person and his wisdom are separate, because we see people who are clever and people who are not. But with Hashem there is never a time when He is not alive or not wise. So these are not separate from Him.
  • Why these three? Why choose life and wisdom, and not power, hearing, seeing and the rest? And if you say that being wise already implies being alive, then you need only two. They are inconsistent even by their own approach.

Chapter 6: the verses

Next are the verses that are brought to show a Trinity. First, verses about "the spirit of Hashem" and "His word". The Rabbi explained that these refer to something distinct from God that He gives to a prophet, an emanation and not God Himself. Like your name, which describes you but is not you, the word can mean a name. Often it simply means "with His will". The same applies to the "mercy" of Hashem and to the eyes and ears of Hashem: all are metaphors, explained later in the chapters on anthropomorphism.

"Let us make man"

Another verse used is "Let us make man". Rav Saadiah answers that Hebrew allows a great person to speak in the plural, and brings examples: Balak, Daniel in front of the king, and Manoah saying "we will make you food". The Rabbi added that the ibn Ezra, who came about 150 years later, argues on these examples: Balak's "we" means Balak and his army, and Manoah's "we" means himself and his wife. So the ibn Ezra does not see them as proof of a royal plural. The Rabbi then returned to the point itself: whichever way we read it, the plural certainly does not mean two or three deities. As Rashi says, Hashem speaks in the language of consultation, as an act of humility, and the Midrash says this teaches us that when we make big decisions we should consult others. The Rabbi noted that the Torah was willing to use a phrase that can so easily be misunderstood, in order to teach this.

The three visitors to Avraham

A further verse: Hashem appeared to Avraham, and then he saw three men. Some say these three are the Trinity. This is easily answered by reading on. The three men went to Sedom, while Avraham was still standing before God, so God is clearly distinct from them. Later it says that two went, not three.

So what does "Hashem appeared to him" mean? Rav Saadiah explains that Hashem first appeared to Avraham in a prophetic vision to tell him that important guests were coming, His angels. Then when Avraham says "Adonai, if I have found favour in your eyes", there is a word missing, as there are elsewhere in scripture, and it means "the angels of Hashem". Rashi reads it differently: this is a plain word meaning "my masters", with no holiness in it. The practical difference is that, according to Rashi, a scribe who made a mistake writing that word in a Sefer Torah could erase it, which he could not do with a holy name.

The Rabbi added that the Rashbam explains that angels, as God's agents, are sometimes described as God in scripture. This must be handled with care, as it can sound as if God is the three men, which is clearly not what is meant.

Chapter 7: the four Christian groups

The Rabbi pointed out that Rav Saadiah does not avoid any subject, however uncomfortable. In Chapter 7 he describes four Christian groups and answers each. Three are more ancient and one more recent.

  1. Jesus was, in body and soul, a part of God. Rav Saadiah refers back to Essay 1, where creation from God Himself was already refuted: how can something without form take a form?
  2. Jesus was born human, and God then put some of His essence into him and made him God. But God comes down in many places in scripture, and nothing there becomes God.
  3. A combination of these, with the same difficulties.
  4. Jesus is not God at all, and "son" only means beloved, like "Israel is My firstborn". Rav Saadiah has no theological argument against this group as far as God is concerned. His disagreement is about changing the law, which comes in Essay 3.

Looking ahead

From Chapter 8 the essay turns to the physical descriptions of Hashem in the Torah, a question that Rav Saadiah is the first to ask and answer. The Rabbi acknowledged that the Trinity can seem abstract, but said it is important to go through the whole book, and the rest will become more relevant to our lives.

Summary

  • All of Hashem's attributes are one. We use many words only because our language and minds cannot hold it in one go.
  • Anything with parts or diversity is physical and created, so God cannot have parts.
  • The Trinity fails philosophically: it is either physical, or it divides God, and it is inconsistent in choosing three.
  • The verses brought for it are metaphors, a plural of humility, or refer to angels.
