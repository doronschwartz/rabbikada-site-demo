Picking up from last week

The previous class covered the fasts, who is exempt, and the restrictions on joy, business, building, weddings, haircuts and laundry. This class, the last before the break, continued from Siman 551, paragraph 5.

Clothes: laundry, new clothes and weaving

Laundering for non-Jews. The Shulhan Arukh forbids washing non-Jews' clothes in the week of Tisha BeAv. There is no enjoyment in that, so why? The Mishnah Berurah explains it is marit ayin: people may think you are laundering your own clothes. The Rabbi linked this to what the class had just learnt in Hilchot Shabbat: when Hazal forbade something because of appearances, it is forbidden even in private only if onlookers would suspect a Torah prohibition. Here they would suspect only a rabbinic one, so in private it is permitted. A laundry shop must close in that week, but helping a non-Jewish neighbour whose machine has broken, at home, is permitted.

New clothes. New clothes may be worn during the Three Weeks if one avoids the Shehiyanu problem, by saying it before the 17th of Tammuz or on Shabbat. But in the week of Tisha BeAv one may not wear new clothes, just as one may not wear freshly laundered ones. Minor items that do not warrant Shehiyanu, such as underwear, are fine. New shoes count as new clothes.

Making clothes. The Yerushalmi praises women who do not begin weaving from Rosh Hodesh Av, a play on the word shetiyah (weaving, and the Temple's foundation stone). So making or finishing new clothes is avoided from Rosh Hodesh. Someone whose livelihood is making clothes for non-Jews may continue.

Meat and wine

The Shulhan Arukh lists customs: no meat or wine in the week of Tisha BeAv, or from Rosh Hodesh, or from the 17th of Tammuz. "This is a custom." By the law of the Gemara, meat and wine are forbidden only at the last meal before Tisha BeAv, about which the Gemara speaks very sharply.

  • Ashkenazim refrain for all Nine Days.
  • The Ben Ish Hai records the Baghdad custom: from after Rosh Hodesh, eating meat on Rosh Hodesh itself since it is somewhat festive. The Rabbi understood this to be the Syrian practice too.
  • Many Moroccan communities refrain only in the week of Tisha BeAv.
  • Yemenites never took on the custom, following the Rambam.

Still, the Shulhan Arukh warns that whoever breaks his community's custom "breaches the fence", language he uses only to enforce a custom. Keep your community's custom; if unsure, follow the general Sefaradi or Ashkenazi practice. How binding local customs are when communities live mixed together is "a discussion for another time".

Why meat and wine?

The Rabbi stressed that this is not a mourning practice. A mourner in the seven days may eat meat and drink wine; only an onen, before the burial, may not. And of all the restrictions, only meat and wine continue through the 10th of Av, which the Shulhan Arukh calls a proper custom. The reason: meat and wine recall the offerings of the Temple, which was set alight on the afternoon of the 9th and burned through the 10th. The extensions, such as avoiding chicken, salted meat or very new wine, are "a custom on a custom". The Mishnah Berurah adds that some avoid even soup cooked with meat, though by the Shulhan Arukh's ruling the soup alone is permitted.

Practical questions

  • Shabbat leftovers. The Hida permits eating meat left over from Shabbat during the week, until the last meal before the fast. The Shaarei Teshuvah, an Ashkenazi, says his communities do not rely on this. One may not deliberately cook extra, though a generous table for guests is normal. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul suggested freezing leftovers or giving them to children. The Rabbi's view: "there's plenty to rely on" for genuine leftovers.
  • Wine for a misvah, such as Havdalah or Birkat HaMazon over a cup, is permitted. The Rema says to give the Havdalah cup to a child. The Rabbi recalled being invited every year in Gateshead to make Havdalah for people for exactly this reason, and noted how hard it is to find a child of the right age awake late on a summer Saturday night. Sefaradim drink the Havdalah wine themselves.
  • Children were never included in this custom, as Rav Ovadia Yosef and Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul write, against the Mishnah Berurah. Children below bar or bat misvah may eat meat.
  • The unwell may eat meat; the Mishnah Berurah includes someone who simply needs strengthening. Pregnant and especially nursing women who normally eat meat may eat it, and someone with a cold may have chicken soup.

Seudat misvah and siyumim

At a brit, a siyum or another seudat misvah, meat and wine are permitted. For Sefaradim this applies throughout the Nine Days and the week of Tisha BeAv to anyone who would normally attend; the Rema limits it in that week to ten people. The Rabbi admitted he has never understood why meat is allowed at a siyum while the Havdalah cup is given to a child, and he criticised organising a meat siyum every night of the Nine Days. The Mishnah Berurah writes that a siyum counts only if one reached the end naturally, not if one timed it for the Nine Days. For a postponed brit, Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul suggests being strict in the week of Tisha BeAv, though one may be lenient.

Haircuts

By the Gemara's law, haircuts of the head, beard or body are forbidden in the week of Tisha BeAv, for women too. A moustache that interferes with eating may be trimmed. Adults may not give children haircuts or launder their clothes that week, to create an awareness of the time. But young children constantly get dirty, so their clothes may be washed; Rav Ovadia Yosef relates the age to the child, and certainly three- to five-year-olds.

Someone whose shloshim ends in that week may only shave the hair off with a razor, not cut it nicely with scissors. In practice people wait until after Tisha BeAv. On Hol HaMoed, by contrast, one whose mourning ended may have a normal haircut. The reasons differ: Tisha BeAv is about mourning, while the Hol HaMoed restriction is to make sure people cut their hair before the festival.

Bathing and swimming

Washing is forbidden by the Gemara only on Tisha BeAv itself. Not bathing beforehand is a custom: Sefaradim in the week of Tisha BeAv, Ashkenazim for the Nine Days.

  • For medical needs one may bathe; the Mishnah Berurah allows a weak person a daily hot bath.
  • Swimming for leisure should be avoided. But someone who swims regularly for physical or mental well-being may, in the Rabbi's view, continue even in that week.
  • Context matters. In the past a bath was a spa-like experience, which is why one should not go to the bathhouse just before Minhah. A five-minute shower is basic hygiene, so there is much more room for leniency: "you need to take a shower when you need to." Rav Ovadia Yosef strongly opposed applying the Rema's European custom in Israel's climate.

Shehiyanu, danger and travel

The only thing the Shulhan Arukh mentions for the whole Three Weeks is that it is good not to say Shehiyanu on new clothes or fruit, except on Shabbat. He also warns against danger, since we relive the times of the calendar; the Rabbi gave bungee jumping as an example. Travel is permitted, even on Tisha BeAv, though leisure trips are best avoided in the Nine Days.

Asked whether summer holidays should change things, the Rabbi distinguished: what the Gemara forbids cannot change, though there may be room to discuss customs. A custom is like a vow, and missing it once for a real reason is not breaking it. "That's why it's very important to me that we always understand what's the din and what's the minhag."

Practical summary

  • Sefaradim: no laundry, freshly laundered clothes or new clothes in the week of Tisha BeAv; private laundering for a non-Jew is permitted.
  • Meat and wine are a custom recalling the Temple offerings; follow your community. Genuine Shabbat leftovers may be eaten, but not at the last meal before the fast.
  • Children, the unwell, and pregnant and nursing women may eat meat; Sefaradim drink the Havdalah wine themselves.
  • Meat is permitted at a genuine seudat misvah, but a siyum should not be timed for the Nine Days.
  • Haircuts are forbidden in the week of Tisha BeAv; young children's clothes may be washed.
  • Showers for hygiene and swimming for health are permitted; avoid leisure bathing and dangerous activities.
