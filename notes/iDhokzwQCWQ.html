These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

HaMelekh HaMishpat and how a hakham thinks

The Rabbi chose to speak about parts of Yom Kippur and the Ten Days that are sometimes neglected. He announced that the following week would be a full class on the Sephardi approach to sitting in the sukkah.

During the Ten Days we say HaMelekh HaKadosh, and everyone agrees that one who forgets must go back. With HaMelekh HaMishpat the Rishonim, including the Rambam, the Rif and the Rosh, also say to go back, and so does the Shulhan Arukh. The Rema, however, follows a view that one does not go back. The Ben Ish Hai rules that since there is a doubt, and in a doubt over berakhot we are lenient, one continues without going back.

Yet in a different case the Ben Ish Hai goes the other way. On Friday night the hazan repeats a short form of the Amidah (Magen Avot), originally instituted for latecomers. Since it is not a true repetition, the poskim say that one who forgot HaMelekh HaKadosh in it does not go back. The Ben Ish Hai rules that he does go back, because according to the Arizal this blessing is a full institution in its own right.

The lesson: to understand a hakham's rulings you must understand his framework. For the Ben Ish Hai, the teachings of the Arizal and the hidden Torah carry tremendous weight, at times even over the ordinary rules of pesak.

Pat Yisrael and the "conception" of the year

The Shulhan Arukh says that in the Ten Days one should be careful to eat bread baked by a Jew, even if one is lenient the rest of the year. People ask: what is the point, if I know I will go back after Yom Kippur?

The Rabbi compared it to a pregnant woman. Doctors tell her to stop drinking or smoking for nine months, even if she will return to her habits afterwards, because she is creating something new. The Ten Days are the conception of the year. "When you're creating something, it has to be perfect at the beginning." So in these days we take on stringencies, even ones we will not keep all year, to lay pure foundations.

Kapparot

The Rashba objected to kapparot as a non-Jewish practice, and the Shulhan Arukh (Siman 605) writes plainly that the custom should be stopped. "People don't know this." The custom spread widely through the influence of the Arizal, and the Ben Ish Hai rules that we do it.

But it must not become superstition, as though waving a chicken guarantees a good year. "If you don't have the hirhur of teshuvah whilst you're doing the kapparot, it's a waste of time." It may even be worse. Its value is in recognising that what happens to the bird should really be happening to me, and turning that into teshuvah.

  • It can be done just as well with money.
  • If getting a chicken means rising at dawn and ruining your day, he would say not to.
  • Where it is done with chickens, make sure the shehitah is done properly, since at such speed it often is not.

Eating on Erev Yom Kippur

The Gemara teaches that whoever eats and drinks on the ninth is considered as if he fasted on the ninth and the tenth. The Rosh explains that out of love HaShem commanded us to eat, so that we have strength for the fast.

  • When does it begin? The Rabbi brought the Gemara about not making a wedding feast on Mosei Shabbat, and the question why Yom Kippur may fall on a Sunday night. The answer, that there is the day to prepare, led the Aharonim to conclude that the misvah is by day. The Hida questioned the proof. The Rabbi concluded that the misvah begins in the morning of Erev Yom Kippur, not the night before. Eating and drinking are both included.
  • Women are obligated too. Although it is a time-bound positive misvah, anyone included in the fast is included in the eating.
  • Someone not fasting. A man who cannot fast still has a misvah to eat on the ninth, since we do not narrow a misvah by the reason the Rosh gave. A woman who is not fasting has no particular misvah, since her obligation rests only on her fasting the next day.
  • "Easy fast" pills may be taken before Yom Kippur. Rav Ovadia Yoseph permits them; they are no different from foods that release energy slowly.

Teshuvah is not the misvah: viduy is

"I really believe what we'll say now can change our whole understanding of Yom Kippur." The Rambam begins Hilchot Teshuvah: when a person does teshuvah for any sin, he is obliged to confess. He counts viduy as the misvah, not teshuvah itself.

Teshuvah, for the Rambam, has three parts:

  1. Stop the sin in the present.
  2. Regret the past.
  3. Resolve for the future.

The common denominator is stopping: in the present, the past and the future. Someone who throws out questionable food but spends Yom Kippur fondly remembering how good it tasted has not left the sin. "You don't have a present if you don't have a past." This is why dementia is so terrible. Your memories are part of who you are.

Why no special misvah? Because stopping is what the Torah already commanded. Having done these three steps, you are "a regular Jew": what you should have been all year.

Viduy: the power of acknowledgement

Viduy and todah, thank you, share a root. Both mean acknowledgement: of good done to me, or of harm I have done.

Relationships need balance. When someone keeps giving to me and I cannot repay, the least I can do is say thank you, and somehow that restores the balance. When I have hurt someone, the instinct is revenge, which puts us back on the same level. Sincere acknowledgement achieves the same thing without a further wrong. But only after teshuvah: an apology from someone still planning the next insult is "probably another insult".

The first three steps are within myself; viduy is the only part that involves the other. The Rambam calls viduy without teshuvah one who immerses holding a dead rat in his hand. Yom Kippur is like a mikveh, but come out still holding the same habits and nothing has changed.

Asking forgiveness. Very often we ask mehilah from the people it is easiest to ask, not the ones we really need to face. "I'll send them a text... that's useless."

Asked about deep disputes, he said to separate the money from the relationship. You may claim every penny you are owed, as in a business break-up or a divorce. But between yourself and yourself you can forgive and move on, even if this is not someone you can stay close to. And be honest: very often neither side is entirely in the right.

God forgives once we forgive

The first time the Torah records someone forgiving is at the end of Bereshit, a book full of people not getting along: Kayin and Hevel, Yishmael and Yishak, Yaakov and Esav, Yoseph and his brothers. After Yaakov's death, the brothers fear revenge, and Yoseph "comforted them and spoke to their hearts". Only after that do we find HaShem explicitly forgiving, in the Golden Calf and the spies. HaShem does not forgive until human beings have learned to forgive one another.

The Gemara in Rosh Hashanah says the Thirteen Attributes never return empty. Why does it not always seem so? Rav Moshe Cordovero explains yaasu lefanai: not "say them" but "do them". Am I slow to anger when I have waited half an hour on the phone? Saying every piyut with the heart elsewhere is to honour HaShem "with the lips" alone.

He ended with the Ramhal: HaShem usually governs by justice, cause and effect, like a parent who must discipline a child. But sometimes, like a parent melted by a child's smile, He sets justice aside. We reach that by living the same way: forgoing, not living with others by strict justice. "The best segulah" for forgiveness is to forgive. "I'm speaking to myself... It's very hard."

Practical summary

  • If you forget HaMelekh HaMishpat, Sephardim following the Ben Ish Hai do not go back.
  • Be particular about Pat Yisrael in the Ten Days, even if lenient the rest of the year.
  • Kapparot must come with teshuvah; money is fine; insist on proper shehitah.
  • The misvah to eat on Erev Yom Kippur begins in the morning; it applies to women who are fasting.
  • Viduy only counts after stopping, regretting and resolving; ask forgiveness from those you really need to.
