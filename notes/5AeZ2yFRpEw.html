Topic: Rabbi Sacks' book The Great Partnership: God, Science and the Search for Meaning Shiur: A conversation between Dayan Daniel Kada and Rabbi Joseph Dweck on Zoom, Tuesday 2 February 2021, the first evening in their series on the works of Rabbi Sacks

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why this book

The Rabbi opened the series by explaining that the idea of discussing Rabbi Sacks' major works together had been planned for some time, and that his passing had made its importance clearer. Rabbi Dweck spoke of what stood out in Rabbi Sacks: he was head and shoulders above others in presenting Torah to the Jewish people and to the world, and he never sounded apologetic. In a post-religious world he spoke with complete confidence as a religious leader.

He stressed that the book does not reconcile science with Torah. It says they should not be forced together, because they are two different things. The key is its subtitle: the search for meaning. The Rabbi added a line from a review: "I'm not entirely convinced by Sacks' arguments, but I am convinced by his passion."

The Rabbi's synopsis

The Rabbi gave an overview of the book's three parts. Rabbi Sacks only reveals at the end what prompted it: in 2009 he saw a bus advert, "There's probably no God. Now stop worrying and enjoy your life." The opening pages have the "new atheists" in view, who see religion as only destructive.

The thesis in one sentence: "Science takes things apart to see how they work. Religion puts things together to see what they mean." The Rabbi noted a parallel in the Maharal of Prague: science tells you mah, what; religion tells you lamah, why. And Einstein: science without religion is lame, religion without science is blind.

Part one. Science and religion cannot be at war, because they ask different questions using different parts of the mind. A central theme is that the meaning of a system lies outside the system. Someone from another planet watching football must first learn the rules, but even then he will ask what the game is for, and that answer is not in the rulebook. So too, science can describe the laws of the universe, but its meaning lies outside it. That, says Rabbi Sacks, is the revolution of Avraham. Polytheism places its gods inside nature, a god of the sun, a god of rain. Monotheism places God and meaning outside the universe.

Part two. Without faith we stand to lose five things: - Human dignity, including free will. The Rabbi asked how a criminal justice system can be justified if we are told everything we do is determined by chemical impulses. - The politics of covenant, a brit between governor and governed rather than citizens serving the state. - Morality. Rabbi Sacks quotes Voltaire, who wanted his lawyer, his servants and his wife to believe in God so he would be robbed less often. Rabbi Sacks compares society without religion to an orchestra that keeps playing for a while after the conductor leaves; morality is the last thing to go. - Relationships and the family, which Bereshit is all about. - The meaning of life. Tolstoy searched philosophy and science and found an answer only in faith.

Part three. Four common questions: evolution, why there is evil, why people do evil in God's name (the subject of the following week), and why God is so important.

Two ways of thinking

Rabbi Dweck developed the opening chapters. Rabbi Sacks goes deeper than "two different subjects": these are two fundamentally different ways of seeing the world, connected with the left and right sides of the brain, analysis and detail on one side, wholeness and relationship on the other. He links this to Greece and Israel. A Hebrew root such as samekh-peh-resh gives sipur, story, mispar, number, and tisporet, haircut: you understand the words through their relationships. The Torah teaches through stories and laws set in real life scenarios, not abstract theories. He also argued that the Rambam, who read Aristotle as an Eastern thinker writing in Arabic and Hebrew, drew on him without becoming an Aristotelian.

The Rabbi offered an idea from the Gemara in Yoma: when Alexander the Great came to destroy Jerusalem, Shimon HaSaddik went out to meet him in the priestly garments, and Alexander bowed down to him. The commentators ask how the kohen gadol could wear those garments outside the Temple. Perhaps, the Rabbi suggested, he was showing the Greek that we too have beauty, that Torah is not only abstract ideas but something real and tangible, integrating both sides. "I think that's the whole Hanukkah story."

Rabbi Dweck added that it is a great mistake to read the Torah for its science. The Rishonim and the Hakhamim of the Talmud teach that the account of creation is not to be read at face value; its depths are of meaning, not scientific description.

Not proofs, but softeners of the heart

From chapter 14, Rabbi Dweck spoke about the evidence Rabbi Sacks brings: the improbability of the universe, of life, of the survival of the Jewish people. These are not proofs, and anyone looking for proofs of God will be disappointed. What they do is open the heart to the possibility of God, softening the barriers people build around their souls.

The Rabbi found it striking that after quoting so many philosophers, Rabbi Sacks comes back at the end to simple emunah: we live it through relationship, through practising Torah, and through the courage to take the risk of faith. "He modelled it."

Questions from the audience

Asked whether halacha changes when science changes, the Rabbi gave the example of kinim, lice, which the Gemara and the Shulhan Arukh allow to be killed on Shabbat because they were thought not to reproduce. Some say the halacha must now change; others that nature has changed; and others that halacha has its own framework and, as the Pahad Yishak puts it, they do not reproduce to the naked eye. Rabbi Dweck added that the Rambam would see such rulings as established by the Beit Din, while science is certainly a consideration in defining things such as fire.

Asked how evolution fits with "a verse does not leave its plain meaning," Rabbi Dweck answered that peshat means the simple meaning, not the literal one, and that a reading which allows for evolution is within the peshat.

Summary

  • Science asks what and how; religion asks why. Both are needed and neither replaces the other.
  • The meaning of a system lies outside it, and that is the heart of Avraham's monotheism.
  • Without faith we risk losing dignity, covenant, morality, family and meaning.
  • The book's evidence does not prove God but opens the heart to Him.
  • In the end, Rabbi Sacks returns to simple faith, lived through Torah.
