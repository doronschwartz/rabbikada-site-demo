Topic: Why we pray if God cannot be changed, Rav Yoseph Albo's answer, and praying for others Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Monday 1 March 2021, in an afternoon talk on the concept of prayer

The recording begins partway through the talk, during a discussion with those present.

The problem

The recording opens as someone suggests that prayer is really about reflection: coping with what happens to us and acknowledging the good we have. The Rabbi agreed that self-scrutiny is an important part of prayer and promised to come back to it. But to say prayer is only psychological, he said, does not fit the words we say. We are directly asking for things.

So the question stands. "Does this not trouble anyone? We've been praying all our lives. What are we doing in prayer?" If my circumstances come from God, and He knows what is best for me, why ask Him to change them? And how can we ask to move a God whose perfection means He cannot be moved?

Someone offered the answer that sometimes God simply says no. That may be, he said, and why bad things happen is a subject for another time. But it does not explain why we try to get Him to say yes in the first place.

Why were the matriarchs barren?

The Rabbi brought the Gemara: why were our matriarchs barren? Because HaKadosh Barukh Hu desires the prayers of the righteous. Read on a simple level, he said, this "beggars belief." God makes people ill, poor and childless so that they will pray to Him more? "What kind of God is that?"

Rav Yoseph Albo: prayer is transformative

The answer, given by Rav Yoseph Albo and many medieval thinkers, is for the Rabbi "the only satisfactory understanding that we have of prayer": prayer is transformative.

He gave a parable. A doctor tells a patient that after a lifetime of bad diet his legs must be amputated within a week. The patient asks for that week and completely changes his life. The doctor examines him again: no amputation now, but some harsh medicine. Two more weeks of change, and only tablets are needed. Two more, and he needs nothing at all. Did the doctor ever change his mind? No. He was right each time, for the patient in front of him. The patient changed.

So too with prayer, following Rav Yoseph Albo: what we can do is change ourselves. The verdict was right for the person you were. Through prayer you become a different person, and a different verdict can apply. That is why HaShem desires the prayers of the righteous: He wanted Sarah and Rahel to become who they became through their prayers. He gave them the chance to grow because He saw their potential.

He compared it to studying: someone who has not done GCSE maths cannot follow calculus. A person on a lower spiritual level may not be fit for a certain blessing; through prayer he rises to a level where he is. The same, he said, explains HaShem telling Moshe "leave Me alone": in their current state the people deserved destruction, but if they prayed and changed, the decree would no longer fit them.

Someone asked why Leah had children. That, he answered, belongs to the hardest question in theology, why good and bad things happen to whom they do. We can only speak of general principles. Someone else noted that Leah had her own pain: her husband did not love her fully. "Everyone has their own package in life."

How prayer changes us

The Rabbi gave three ways: - Self-scrutiny. As someone present had pointed out, tefillah comes from a root connected to judging. The verb almost always appears as lehitpalel, the reflexive form, something you do to yourself. If I pray for wisdom, am I doing all I can to become wise? If I ask for mercy, do I show mercy to others? - Relationship. A child who asks his uncle for money instead of his father makes the father feel estranged. Relying on God builds closeness. He brought the serpent, cursed to eat dust: it never has to worry about its food again, but God is saying, I want nothing to do with you. With human beings He wants a relationship. He wants us to come to Him and recognise Him as the source of all. - Surrender. In sincere prayer a person says: I am inadequate, I cannot do this without You. Thinking in those terms changes his whole attitude to HaShem.

Praying for someone else

The Rabbi left a final question to think about: if prayer works by changing the one who prays, how can I ask the rabbi to pray for a sick person, or read out a name of someone who is not praying at all?

Several answers were offered. One was that God hears more when a minyan prays together; true, he said, but it still does not explain it. Another, that one who cannot pray for himself is like a baby who must be fed. He could not accept that theologically, because the sick person deserves what he deserves in his current state. Appealing to God's mercy? We do not change God's mercy either; we attach ourselves to it.

If both people pray, the Rabbi said, then of course a rabbi's prayers can join yours. For the rest, his explanation comes from our being a nation: kol Yisrael arevim zeh lazeh, we are responsible for one another. Just as one person can make kiddush for the whole family, we are interlocked. The person praying transforms himself, and that transformation has a knock-on effect on those connected to him. The closer the connection, the stronger the effect. Praying for someone in your own community or family will be more effective than reading out the name of a stranger far away, though in truth we should feel connected to every Jew.

He added that we do not come to shul to "worship" in the sense of begging on our knees for mercy, a term he called Christian in origin. God cannot be changed by human whims. We come to work on ourselves.

Someone shared that her late father, a Torah scholar, used to tell couples who asked him to pray for children: you have to pray, and I can be the messenger. "That story is the whole lecture," the Rabbi said.

Summary

  • We cannot change God's mind; through prayer we change ourselves and become fit for a different verdict.
  • The matriarchs were barren so that, through prayer, they could become who they were meant to be.
  • Prayer transforms us through self-scrutiny, relationship with God, and surrender.
  • Praying for others works through our bond as one people, and is strongest where the bond is close.
  • No one can pray for us in our place; at most they can pray with us.
