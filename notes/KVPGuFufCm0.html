These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi said we finished Siman 6 last week. In that Siman we saw the gerushah and then the zonah. Now we move to the third woman forbidden to a Kohen, the halalah, which he said is much simpler. He planned to go through the whole of Siman 7 and then, if there was time, return to a few points from Siman 6. Siman 7 is about the halal and the halalah, a person whose status comes from a forbidden union of a Kohen.

How a halalah is made

The Shulhan Arukh begins with a woman who becomes a halalah through a Kohen's forbidden relation. The Rabbi explained several principles.

The status starts after the first relation. When a Kohen has relations with a woman he may not marry, she becomes a halalah only once that first relation is complete. The consequences begin with the second relation, whether with the same Kohen or another. The Beit Shemuel asks why we do not say that the haarah (the beginning) already makes her a halalah, so that the rest of the same act would be a second transgression. The Rabbi said we do not hold this, because the status takes effect only at the end of the act. If he withdrew and returned, that would be a second relation and then the status applies.

Daughters too. When a Kohen marries a zonah, for example, the child becomes a halal, and a daughter becomes a halalah. Both carry the status. The Rabbi said we will see what this means for a halalah's later marriages.

Does the chuppah alone make a halalah?

The Shulhan Arukh says that if a Kohen married a woman through nisuin, even if there was no relation, she becomes a halalah. The reason is that every marriage is presumed to include relations (hezkat beulah), even if the woman may in fact still be a virgin. The question is what happens when we know for certain that they never had relations. Perhaps the couple were never alone together, or they were always accompanied by witnesses.

The Rabbi presented the views:

Authority

Position

Tosafot

The nisuin itself, the chuppah, makes a halalah, even without a relation

The Rambam

His wording can be read two ways. He may hold like Tosafot, or he may rely on the presumption that there was a relation

The Helkat Mehokek

Reads the Rambam as relying on the presumption. If we know for certain there were no relations, she is not a halalah

The Taz

We must be strict both ways, so she is a doubtful halalah

The Bah

The chuppah itself makes a halalah, and the children are definite halalim

The Pithei Teshuvah

Follows the Taz and rules strictly

The Rabbi said the Rambam's wording is hard. If he held that the nisuin itself is the problem, he would simply say so. Instead he speaks of a presumption that she is beulah. That suggests the Rambam does not hold like Tosafot. The Taz wants to rule strictly in both directions, and the Pithei Teshuvah agrees. So what we know for certain is that eirusin and kiddushin alone do not make a halalah. We know Tosafot holds that the chuppah does. The practical question is a doubt about the Rambam, and so the Taz and the Pithei Teshuvah say to treat the children as doubtful halalim.

The general rule is that a halalah must come from a prohibition that is specific to a Kohen (pesulei kehunah).

Seifim: what happens to the child

The first relation. The Shulhan Arukh says that if a Kohen had relations with one of the arayot or with a yevamah who is forbidden to him, and she became pregnant from that first relation, the child is not a halal. The reason is the same. She only becomes a zonah once the first relation is finished. So the child from the first relation is kosher, and only later children are halalim. The case is different for a convert. A convert already has the status of a zonah, so a child from the first relation is a halal.

The Rabbi asked why the first-relation rule matters, since the child of a Kohen and a forbidden woman such as a married woman is a mamzer anyway. The only practical difference is for a daughter: she is forbidden to a Kohen as a mamzeret, not as a halalah. He also pointed out that the Rambam names a yevamah leshuk, who falls into the zonah category only through a special derivation, and the child of that first relation is kosher.

A niddah. A Kohen's child from a niddah is completely kosher and is not a halal, because a niddah does not make a zonah. The Rambam says "the child is kosher", but also calls him pagum. The Rabbi explained that pagum here means not a halal or a mamzer but a spiritual blemish, a weaker character, because of the way he was conceived. The Bah notes that the expression pegumah lekehunah is a blemish and not a disqualification, so the advice is to avoid such a marriage at the outset, but if it happened they may stay together.

He told of a rabbinic authority who was asked about a young man who wanted to marry a girl who was born to a mother during the days of niddah. She was a fine person, and he only worried about this pagum. The answer was that such a child may have a greater propensity for flaws, but if she has shown through her life that she overcame it, there is no problem. Everyone is born with flaws, and the question is whether we sink into them or overcome them. The Rabbi said he found this beautiful.

The status passes through the father

A halal who marries a regular Jewish woman has children who are halalim. The status continues from father to son. But if a Kohen marries a gerushah and has a daughter, she is a halalah and may not marry a Kohen. Her own children, if she marries an Israelite, are regular Israelites. There are two ways to be a halalah: by being born one, or by being a woman who had a forbidden relation with a Kohen. A Kohen may not marry either kind. The Rabbi stressed that many people do not know that the daughter of a Kohen and a gerushah can never marry a Kohen. He said you have to work these cases through carefully, because the status does not pass the same way in each generation.

The convert and the child

A halalah must be created through conception. If a Kohen marries a gerushah who is already pregnant by another man, she becomes a halalah from the marriage, but the child she is carrying is not a halal. Only children conceived afterwards are.

For a female convert, the Gemara says that a girl born to two converts should not marry a Kohen, but if she did, she may stay. The Rabbi said this is less known. It is common that a convert marries a convert, and the boy is a regular child, but the girl cannot marry into the Kohanim at the outset.

He asked what happens if an Israelite makes a non-Jewish woman pregnant, and she converts during the pregnancy. Is the child a regular Jewish girl, or does she have the status of a convert? The Beit Shemuel says the leniency that allows a married couple to stay together applies only where the girl herself was conceived in holiness. If she was conceived before her mother's conversion, then even if she married a Kohen she may not stay. The Rabbi said this is important.

A halalah by the Rabbis

The Shulhan Arukh next says that a Kohen who has relations with a halusah makes her a halalah midirabanan, because a halusah is forbidden to a Kohen by the Rabbis. Children born afterwards, from the same or another Kohen, are halalim by the Rabbis. A prohibition that applies to everyone, such as a relative, does not make a halalah, because it is not specific to a Kohen.

He noted further situations that make a halalah midirabanan which the Shulhan Arukh does not mention, such as a divorce where there was reiah haget, or a get given to a woman who was forced (meunnes), both seen in Siman 6.

A doubtful halusah. The Rema adds safek halusah. The Rabbi asked how this can be right, if a Kohen who marries a doubtful halusah is allowed to stay with her. A doubtful halusah is forbidden to a Kohen at the outset, but if he married her, he may stay. So how can the child be a doubtful halal? The Bah raises this and distinguishes: if the Kohen married her, the child is regular, and if it was relations without marriage, the child is a doubtful halal. The Rabbi found this very difficult to accept. His conclusion is that the consensus of later authorities does not make a doubtful halal from a doubtful halusah, since we allow him to stay with her.

The last seifim

If a Kohen is forbidden to marry her, such as a gerushah, both of them share the transgression. In the other direction, if she is a Kohenet and he is a halal, she is allowed to marry him.

Practical summary

  • A halalah is made by a relation forbidden to a Kohen specifically. The status takes effect after the first relation, so the first child is kosher and later children are halalim.
  • The child of a convert's first relation with a Kohen is a halal, because she is already a zonah.
  • Whether the chuppah alone makes a halalah is disputed. The Taz and the Pithei Teshuvah treat the children as doubtful halalim.
  • A Kohen's child from a niddah is kosher but has a pegam, and we advise care in marriage.
  • The status of halal passes through the father. A halalah's children from an Israelite are regular.
  • A halusah makes a halalah by the Rabbis. A doubtful halusah does not.
