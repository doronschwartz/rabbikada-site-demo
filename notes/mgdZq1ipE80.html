Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 298, seifim 1-15 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 11 April 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seifim 1-2: The candle and the double flame

Siman 298 deals with the candle of Havdalah. The Rabbi began by reviewing the end of the previous class. On an ordinary Mosei Shabbat one does not need to go looking for a flame; the berakhah recalls Adam HaRishon discovering fire on the first Saturday night, which is a fine idea but not an obligation to search. After Yom Kippur it is different, since one may not use fire on Yom Kippur, and so there one should look for a flame. "Meorei" is plural because a flame has several colours. The Rema adds that without wine or the local beverage one may still say the berakhot on the spices or on the fire alone, because Havdalah, the spices and the fire are three separate berakhot that we join together.

Seif 2: the good practice is to say the berakhah on an avukah, a flame of two wicks together, since it is a stronger flame. This is only misvah min hamuvhar, the finest way of doing the misvah, not a requirement. "You don't have to drive yourself mad" to find the right candle; one wick is fine. There is also an opinion that if you have no double flame you should light a new candle rather than use a light already burning in the house, so that it is clear it is lit for Havdalah. That is where the dedicated Havdalah candle comes from. Three or four wicks are also fine.

Seifim 3-4: You must benefit from the light first

The Rabbi took seif 4 before seif 3. One may not say the berakhah until one can benefit from the flame. So one may not say the berakhah first and then strike a match: the flame must already be burning. The test of benefit in the Gemara is being close enough to tell the coin of one country from the coin of another, for example a penny from a cent.

The key word is that one would be able to tell them apart. "People think there's a halachic necessity to switch off the lights. There's no necessity to switch off the lights." In a normal room a small flame is enough even with the lights on. Only if you are at the back of a large hall with a hundred people and a small candle should you check, and then switching the lights off may help.

Why the fingernails? On Mosei Shabbat nobody has coins in his pocket, so a simpler test developed using what is closest at hand: can I tell my nail from my flesh, and can I see the lines on my palm? The Shulhan Arukh records the custom of looking at the nails and the palm, and the Rema explains that one bends the fingers into the right palm while holding the cup in the left, so that the tips of the nails and the palm are seen together. One looks at the tip of the nail, not its face, since the point is to distinguish nail from flesh. One hand is enough.

The Rabbi stressed that this custom is about 500 years old and is simply a practical test. Later ideas were added (nails keep growing, a sign of blessing; the lines of the palm), but "you always have to understand the source of every custom", and here the source is very rational. Someone asked why people did not look at a siddur instead; he answered that 500 years ago most people did not have a siddur, and your hands are always with you.

Why see the flame before the berakhah here, when women light Shabbat candles and then say the berakhah? Friday night candles are a birkat hamisvah, which should come before the act; this is why Rav Ovadia Yosef questioned the custom of lighting first, though most women had that custom. Borei Meorei HaEsh is a berakhah of praise or of benefit, and one must see what one is praising, just as one says the berakhah on blossoms in Nisan while looking at them.

The person making Havdalah holds the cup throughout: in the right hand for Borei Peri HaGefen, moved to the left for the spices and the fire, and back to the right for HaMavdil.

Seifim 5-12: Which flames are valid

The Rabbi moved through these quickly. - A flame lit on Shabbat. One may not use a flame lit on Shabbat in a forbidden way, whether by a Jew or by a non-Jew. A flame lit on Shabbat for a sick person, which was permitted, may be used, since on Mosei Shabbat (unlike Mosei Yom Kippur) the fire need not have rested all Shabbat. - Avodah Zarah. No berakhah on a flame lit for idolatry, or on a fire passed on from a non-Jew's flame. A bonfire seen from the road depends on whether most of the town is Jewish. - New fire. Fire struck from stones on Mosei Shabbat is fine, which helps someone spending Shabbat in the countryside. After Yom Kippur this does not work. - Glowing coals are enough if they would reignite a splinter put into them. - It must be lit for light. A brick kiln, a fire for cooking, a candle lit only for honour (for example beside a hatan walking to the huppah), or the candle by a coffin is not valid. Practically, a gas flame lit to make a cup of tea is not valid for Havdalah.

Rabbi Akiva Eiger's ruling. If someone said Borei Meorei HaEsh on a flame lit for cooking, the berakhah was in vain. He must say it again on a proper flame, and Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds that he must repeat the whole Havdalah including Borei Peri HaGefen, because the wasted berakhah was an interruption. Someone in the class objected that if it was an innocent mistake, such as a candle that had gone out unnoticed, it should not count as an interruption. The Rabbi liked the point and said others indeed disagree, while Rav Ovadia Yosef rules like Rabbi Akiva Eiger; he said he would need to check whether Rav Ovadia distinguishes between the cases.

A synagogue candle (seif 11): the Gemara says it depends whether an important person is present, and it is read both ways. The principle is that a flame lit only for honour, not for light, is not used.

Seifim 13-14: A blind person, and doing it together

A blind person does not say Borei Meorei HaEsh, since he cannot benefit from the light. He does say Pokeah Ivrim in the morning, because the morning berakhot praise the general order of the world and because he depends on those who see. Here the berakhah is on what I myself see now.

Seif 14: if a group learning in the beit midrash on Mosei Shabbat is brought a flame, the Gemara rules that everyone should stop and one person says the berakhah for all, because of berov am hadrat melekh, rather than passing the flame around. The Rabbi said this is explicit in the Gemara.

Asked about the spices in shul, he said ideally one should wait for the spices to go round so everyone hears the berakhah. In practice people often start talking while waiting, which spoils the Havdalah, so the person making Havdalah may judge it better to continue and let people smell afterwards.

Seif 15: Electricity and glass

A flame must be both seen and used. If it is covered, so you can see light but not the flame, or you can see the flame but not benefit from it, there is no berakhah. The Shulhan Arukh adds a flame inside a glass or crystal enclosure (aspaklaria). Mishnah Berurah 37: even though the fire is visible through the glass, it is not enough, since one needs a naked flame. The Magen Avraham allowed glass, but many poskim disagree, and the Rabbi said that this is what we hold: no barrier between your eyes and the flame. So one cannot say the berakhah through a glass door.

This is also why electricity does not work for Havdalah, even a filament bulb. On Friday night a filament bulb has the status of fire, and Rav Ovadia Yosef allows a woman in a hotel to say the berakhah on a lamp she keeps on in her room (not the bathroom light). But for Havdalah the bulb is glass between you and the fire.

Glasses? Prescription glasses are considered part of you, just as wearing them on Shabbat is not carrying; the same logic applies to a walking stick for someone who cannot walk without it. A discussion followed about sunglasses worn as fashion compared with a tie, and the Rabbi said the laws of carrying would come in Siman 301.

Practical summary

  • Use a dedicated Havdalah candle, ideally with two wicks, but one wick is fine.
  • Light it first and only then say Borei Meorei HaEsh; never say the berakhah before the flame is lit.
  • Look at your nails and palm to check you are close enough to benefit; there is no need to switch off the lights in a normal room.
  • Do not use a flame lit for cooking, for honour, or by a coffin, nor an electric bulb or a flame behind glass.
  • In a group, one person says the berakhah for everyone.
