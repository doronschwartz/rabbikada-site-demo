Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 290, seifim 1-2; 291, seifim 1-2 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 22 February 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

One hundred blessings on Shabbat

Last week (289) covered the morning Kiddush. Siman 290 is short, with a few interesting points, before Siman 291 on the three meals.

Seif 1: on Shabbat one should add fruit, delicacies and fragrant spices to complete the hundred berakhot a day. The Gemara in Menahot says King David instituted the hundred blessings at a time of plague. The Rabbi understood the point as bringing Hashem into one's awareness throughout the day. On a weekday, praying three times, eating and going to the bathroom easily makes a hundred. On Shabbat each Amidah has seven blessings instead of nineteen, so about 36 are missing, and one needs to make a conscious effort. Listening to the Haftarah blessings and answering amen also counts. "You don't have to drive yourself mad counting"; just add more.

The hundred are counted from nightfall to nightfall, as the Shulhan Arukh in Siman 46 begins the count at night. On a long summer Friday when Shabbat is brought in early, the Rabbi's opinion is that the blessings from then count as Shabbat, though contemporary poskim discuss it.

An unnecessary blessing, and when Shabbat allows it

The Rabbi distinguished two concepts:

  • Berakhah levatalah, a blessing in vain: for example, a second blessing on the next grape. The Rambam, followed by the Shulhan Arukh, sees this as a violation of the third commandment; Tosafot, the Hinukh and others see it as rabbinic. This is why Sefaradim are stricter about it than Ashkenazim.
  • Berakhah sheeinah serikhah, an unnecessary blessing: saying an after-blessing when you plan to continue eating, causing a new blessing later. The second blessing is not in vain, but it was avoidable.

On Shabbat, to reach a hundred, one may cause unnecessary blessings when it is not obvious from what one is doing that it is only to add blessings. Eating dessert after Birkat HaMazon and then saying its own blessings is normal behaviour, so it is allowed. So is the Moroccan and Gibraltarian custom of saying blessings on various foods after Kiddush before HaMosi. But spending Shabbat afternoon eating a grape, saying the after-blessing, and eating another is plainly done for the blessings and is not allowed, even on Shabbat.

Asked whether one may say a new blessing on a more important fruit after eating an apple: "No, we don't hold that."

Sleeping on Shabbat, and preparing for after Shabbat

The Rema: if you normally nap in the afternoon, do not stop on Shabbat; it is part of Oneg. But there is no misvah to sleep on Shabbat. The saying that "Shabbat" stands for "sheinah beShabbat taanug" is nice, but "don't make yourself go to sleep." If you are not tired, use the time well.

Mishnah Berurah 4: one may not say "let's sleep so we can stay up after Shabbat." The Rabbi asked the class: may someone who never naps sleep on Shabbat afternoon to have energy for a long drive on Saturday night, or before a Seder that falls on Mosei Shabbat? He explained a principle of hakhanah, preparing on Shabbat for a weekday: it is forbidden only when the preparation is evident from the action or from what one says. A person may walk to a bus stop on Shabbat afternoon to catch the first bus after Shabbat, because walking does not show preparation. If he announces that is why he is walking, it is forbidden.

So:

  • Sleeping on Shabbat afternoon so as to have energy at night is fine, as long as it stays in your mind.
  • Do not say it out loud. Do not tell children "go to sleep so you have energy for tonight"; say "have a rest, it's Shabbat."

Learning on Shabbat. The Shaarei Teshuvah brings an opinion of the Yaavets, Rav Yaakov Emden, that one should not learn in depth on Shabbat. The response is that one should, on the contrary, find new Torah insights on Shabbat, as the Zohar says one is asked after Shabbat what he learned. The Rabbi noted that the Yaavets would not be persuaded by the Zohar, since he wrote that it was a forgery, and explained his strong opposition to anything Sabbatean. He quoted the Arizal that new insights on Shabbat are like honouring one's parents.

The Hida records that the Arizal slept two or three hours on Shabbat afternoon, adding that many keep this custom, and "if only they kept all his other practices too." The Rabbi's lesson: people pick the comfortable parts of Kabbalistic practice.

Seif 2: after the morning meal people gather to study, and it is forbidden to hold a public meal at that time. The Gemara speaks sharply against hosting a banquet while the hakham gives his class. Today, the Rabbi said, if your community has a Shabbat afternoon class and you invite people from that community to a meal at the same time, that violates this halacha. The Rema: working people should study more on Shabbat than scholars who learn all week, who may relax more on Shabbat.

Seudah Shelishit: the misvah and how much to eat

Siman 291 has six seifim. The Rabbi noted that the next siman, 292, is Minhah. Since the Shulhan Arukh goes in order, he seems to have eaten Seudah Shelishit before Minhah.

Seif 1: take great care to eat the third meal, even if full, with at least a keveisah. If it is truly painful to eat, you need not, but "a wise man's eyes are in his head": do not overfill at the morning meal. The Gemara learns three meals from the three times "hayom". Some Rishonim say this is from the Torah; most say it is rabbinic.

  • In the summer "there's absolutely no excuse" not to eat Seudah Shelishit. In the winter it is harder.
  • He criticised the practice of not washing for Seudah Shelishit and fulfilling it with Torah alone, as "completely against everything."
  • The Shulhan Arukh and the Rambam require a keveisah, about 56 grams; some poskim allow a kezayit. Aim for a keveisah; if you cannot, a kezayit is still good.

When to eat it, and before or after Minhah

Seif 2: the time is from Minhah time, six and a half hours, about 12.45 in winter. Before that one has not fulfilled it. The Rambam holds the three meals can be at any time, and we rely on him on Erev Pesah that falls on Shabbat.

Some say one may not drink water between Minhah and Arvit on Shabbat because the souls are returning to Gehinnom. From this midrash, Tosafot and the Rosh say to eat Seudah Shelishit before Minhah, calling it a small meal that does not fall under the rule against eating before Minhah. The Rema prefers praying Minhah first, simply because once Minhah time arrives one should pray before a meal.

The Ben Ish Hai goes further and says Seudah Shelishit before Minhah does not fulfil the misvah at all. "No one agrees with that." The Rabbi's ruling for Sefaradim:

  • You may eat Seudah Shelishit before or after Minhah, as you wish.
  • If you eat first, either have a fixed Minhah time at shul or appoint someone to remind you.

Asked about making the morning meal small and the third meal the main one in winter, he said the morning meal is the main meal of Shabbat and deserves the best food. He admitted he does it himself because of his shul's times. If someone genuinely prefers dairy at nine in the morning and meat later, that is his Oneg. If it is equal to you, the main meal should be in the morning.

Practical summary

  • Make an effort to reach a hundred blessings on Shabbat with fruit, spices and listening to blessings in shul.
  • Do not cause extra blessings in a way that is obviously only for the count.
  • Nap on Shabbat afternoon if you like, but do not say you are sleeping for Saturday night.
  • Do not host a meal at the time of your community's Shabbat afternoon class.
  • Eat Seudah Shelishit with at least a keveisah of bread, from midday on, before or after Minhah.
