Sefer: Pirkei Avot with the Rambam's commentary Chapter: Chapter 2 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series on the Rambam's commentary to Pirkei Avot, Tuesday 18 June 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Recap

Before Shavuot the class had covered Chapter 1: the five categories of speech and the Rambam's demanding idea of a true friend. In Chapter 2 the Rambam refers back to his Shemonah Perakim again and again, and the Rabbi focused on those points.

Tiferet: the middle path

Mishnah 1. Rabbi (Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi) said: which is the right path a person should choose? One that is tiferet, splendour, for the one who does it, and brings him tiferet from others.

Does that mean we should act in order to be praised? No. The Rambam says the right path is the one taught in Chapter 4 of the eight chapters: the middle path. Someone who keeps away from the extremes of every trait acquires good character for himself, and others will also get on with him and like him. That is splendour from within and from others.

The Rabbi added an observation he called fascinating. The Rambam never mentions Kabbalah, and he did not believe the Rambam intended this, yet in Kabbalah tiferet is exactly the middle path:

  • Avraham is hesed, kindness, sometimes too much of it. HaShem had to tell him to listen to Sarah and send away Hagar and Yishmael, though as a man of hesed he felt it was wrong. That is why in the Amidah we speak of hasadim tovim, good kindness: there is also undiluted kindness that is not good.
  • Yishak is gevurah, strength and strictness.
  • Yaakov is tiferet, the blend of the two, which is why in Kabbalah hesed is on the right, gevurah on the left and tiferet in the centre.

Light and severe misvot

The Mishnah continues: be as careful with a light misvah as with a severe one, for you do not know the reward of misvot; and weigh the loss of a misvah against its reward, and the gain of a sin against its loss.

The Rambam explains that the Torah gives a hierarchy only for prohibitions. For positive misvot it does not reveal the reward, so that people will not pick and choose. His examples of misvot people treat as light are rejoicing on the festival and learning the Hebrew language, and of weighty ones, milah, the Pesah offering and sisit.

Learning Hebrew. The Rabbi noted it is unclear where the Rambam gets a misvah to learn Hebrew. The suggested source is the Sifrei on the Shema: when a child begins to speak, his father speaks with him in the holy tongue and teaches him Torah. Since the Rambam does not count it separately among the misvot, one view is that it belongs to the misvah of Talmud Torah: Torah should be learned in its own language. He was less sure about modern Hebrew, but learning the original language of the Torah is, for the Rambam, a misvah.

Sisit. The Rambam lists sisit among the great misvot alongside milah and the Pesah offering. Generally, the Rabbi said, positive misvot are opportunities: if you miss one, it is your loss. But the Gemara says sisit is equal to all the misvot, because it envelops a person and reminds him of all of them.

The eight levels of prohibition

For negative commandments the Torah does give us a scale, from most to least severe:

  1. Stoning (sekilah): for example, certain forbidden relationships and breaking Shabbat.
  2. Burning (serefah): for example, a kohen's daughter who commits adultery. The Rabbi stressed that Hazal understood this as molten lead, not burning alive.
  3. The sword (hereg): a murderer and the idolatrous city.
  4. Strangulation (henek): ordinary adultery, striking a parent, kidnapping.
  5. Karet: for example, eating on Yom Kippur. So, he noted, breaking Shabbat is more severe than eating on Yom Kippur, not less.
  6. Death at the hands of heaven: for example, a non-kohen eating terumah.
  7. Lashes: for example, eating non-kosher food.
  8. A prohibition without lashes: for example, lashon hara.

A case. A sick person on Shabbat must eat meat. Should we give him non-kosher meat already available, or break Shabbat to obtain kosher meat? By the scale, non-kosher food is the lighter prohibition, and some Rishonim say so. But the ruling of the Shulhan Arukh is to break Shabbat for kosher meat. Not because Shabbat is lighter, but because a patient may recoil from non-kosher food and not eat it. If he could be given it without knowing, the Rabbi said, there would be no such concern.

Why the reward is hidden. Because we are not told the rewards, we treat every misvah as precious. Hence the principles that one engaged in a misvah is exempt from another, and that we do not pass over a misvah in front of us to wait for a greater one.

Weighing loss and reward. We can still learn something. Where neglecting a positive misvah carries a heavy penalty, as with milah, the Pesah offering, Shabbat and a parapet on the roof, we know its reward is great. And when a person has the chance to sin and holds back, his reward is measured by how severe that sin is.

Torah, the community and character

  • Torah with work (Mishnah 2). Torah without an occupation will not last and leads to sin. The Rabbi deferred this to Chapter 4, where the Rambam writes at greatest length. In answer to a question, he promised to cover it there in full.
  • Working for the community. If communal work means missing a minyan or other misvot, HaShem says: I count it as if you did them.
  • Do not separate from the community. Living alone is only for extreme circumstances. People who live alone, the Rambam says later, fall into melancholy.
  • Do not trust yourself until the day you die. Even a trait you have mastered over years must be kept up with constant action.
  • Do not say something that cannot be understood. Rashi reads this about secrets. The Rambam reads it as advice to teachers: be clear and concise, not cryptic. That, the Rabbi said, is the beauty of the Rambam's own writing.
  • The boor, the hasid and "where there are no men". Recalling Chapter 7 of the eight chapters, the two requirements for prophecy were intellectual perfection and most of the good character traits. A boor has neither; an am haares may have good character but not wisdom. Where there are no people of virtue around you, work on yourself anyway.

What goes around, five students and the good heart

Harm returns. The Mishnah teaches that whoever harms others is harmed in return, and the Rambam says this is visible at all times and places. Someone asked how this fits with Moshe's question of why the wicked prosper. The Rabbi answered that the Rambam, as he understood him, means harm done to society: a wicked person may prosper in many ways, but the specific harm he does to others comes back on him.

Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai's students. Rabbi Eliezer ben Horkenos never forgot anything. For the Rambam, a hasid is someone who has perfected both character and intellect. That is why Hazal say whoever wants to be a hasid should fulfil the words of Avot. The best path is a good heart: all virtues lie in the faculty of desire, the fourth of the soul's five faculties, and the whole Torah addresses it.

Warm yourself by the fire of the hakhamim but beware of their coals. Spend time with them but do not become over-familiar, and do not assume you can easily apologise later. The Rambam cites Gehazi, who was brazen before Elisha and was struck with saraat.

Ayin hara, the evil inclination and hatred of people drive a person from the world. The Rambam understands the last as melancholy. "We are social mammals," the Rabbi said; someone who cannot get on with anyone must ask himself why.

Know what to answer an apikoros. Learn other disciplines to answer challenges from outside, as Rav Saadiah Gaon did. The Rambam says not to engage a Jewish heretic. The Rabbi read this with the Mishneh Torah, which says those raised in mistaken beliefs are not to blame. The Rambam means someone who knew the truth and rejected it.

A final question. Asked whether "Talmud Torah is equal to them all" means that study comes before practice, the Rabbi said yes: the Rambam explains it is equal to all because without study you cannot keep the misvot.

Summary

  • Tiferet is the middle path; the Rabbi noted it matches Kabbalah, though the Rambam surely did not intend that.
  • The rewards of positive misvot are hidden so that we treat each one as precious, even learning Hebrew.
  • Prohibitions are ranked in eight levels, which help us weigh one against another.
  • Most of the Mishnayot are read as lessons in character, built on the Shemonah Perakim.
  • Torah study is supreme because it makes the rest of the misvot possible.
