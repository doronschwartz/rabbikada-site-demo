Topic: Tiltul min hasad, moving mukseh indirectly on Shabbat (Sefaradi and Ashkenazi sources) Source: Shabbat 141a, 123 and 43; Rashi, Ritva, Meiri, Tosafot, Rosh, Ran, Taz, Hazon Ish, Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Safra Kollel, Monday 29 December 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

What does tiltul min hasad mean?

This was a be'iyun shiur, tracing the topic from the Gemara through the Rishonim and Aharonim to the poskim. The Rabbi said the term is often used without clarity. He set out three possibilities and, by the end of the class, refined them into four kinds.

  1. Moving mukseh with a part of the body other than the hands, such as pushing it off the bed with an elbow or a foot (today usually called a shinui).
  2. Pushing mukseh with something else, for example pushing a phone off a table with a stick.
  3. Moving something permitted so that mukseh moves with it, for example lifting ice cream that sits on top of frozen food that cannot be defrosted on Shabbat, so the frozen food moves too. This has two versions, discussed below.

Sugya 1: the Mishnah and Rav Nahman's radish (Shabbat 141a)

The Mishnah says that if money lies on your bed, you may shake it off with your body. The Gemara brings Rav Nahman: a radish stuck in the ground may be pulled out if the earth will not move, but if pulling it will certainly move the earth, it is forbidden, even though the earth is only moved indirectly. The Gemara challenges Rav Nahman from our Mishnah: if one may move mukseh with the body, one should be allowed to pull the radish too. The conclusion is that tiltul min hasad is not a problem.

Two things come out. First, the Gemara ruling is that tiltul min hasad is permitted. Second, the Gemara treats moving mukseh with the body and moving it indirectly (by lifting something permitted) as the same concept, although they are two different things.

Sugya 2: the fig in the straw (Shabbat 123)

A fig covered by straw may be taken out if the straw is partly open. Rashi explains that this is tiltul min hasad, and it is allowed because the straw moves only after the fig is lifted, unlike a radish, where the earth moves as the radish is pulled. The Ritva disagrees: if the fig is completely covered, it is nullified to the straw and the whole becomes mukseh. The Rabbi said he is not fully clear on the details here and that this is not the main point.

The Gemara continues that even a fully covered fig may be taken out with a stick. The Meiri understands this in two ways. On the first, the stick is an extension of the hand, so you may not push through the earth or straw to reach the fig, only through an opening. On the second, the Gemara permits it, which makes this a third kind of tiltul min hasad: moving mukseh through an object rather than touching it directly.

Sugya 3: moving a body on Shabbat (Shabbat 43)

A body that is lying in the sun may be moved on Shabbat for the sake of honour, so it is moved along with the bed, which is moving the body indirectly. The Gemara concludes that this is permitted even though it would normally be forbidden, and the reason given is a special allowance for a body, lest one otherwise end up moving it directly. This is where Tosafot's distinction comes from:

  • When I move something permitted for my own need, and mukseh moves with it, it is allowed.
  • When I do the movement for the sake of the mukseh itself, it is generally forbidden.

The Rabbi's example: I move a table so that it will cover the phone and keep it from being stolen. That is forbidden, even though the phone moves indirectly, because the entire action is for the phone's sake.

The Rosh and the Ran

If the distinction is for the need of the permitted object, how can the Mishnah allow shaking money off a bed? The Rosh, in the way most poskim read him, says that moving with the body is a lower level of tiltul altogether, and permitted in all circumstances, even for the mukseh's own sake, unlike indirect moving of mukseh, which is permitted only when it is for the need of something permitted. The Rabbi noted a difficulty: the Gemara itself equates the two.

The Ran disagrees. For him there is no difference: moving with the body is permitted only for the need of something permitted. He understands "need" broadly. Moving money off a bed to lie down is for the need of the bed.

The practical difference is clear. On the Ran's view, you could not kick a phone off the floor just to stop someone taking it. On the Rosh's plain reading you can, since you move it with your foot. The Rabbi added that even the Ran would permit clearing the phone off a table if you want the table to look clean, since that is a need of the table.

The Hazon Ish

The Hazon Ish says the Rosh was misunderstood and that the Rosh holds like the Ran. In the Rabbi's words, the Hazon Ish understands the Rosh like the Ran, so for him moving mukseh with the body is permitted only for the need of something permitted. The Rabbi said this is not the accepted view and that most poskim follow the plain reading of the Rosh. He mentioned the Mishnah Berurah in this connection, though that part of the recording is unclear. Part of the passage around the Hazon Ish is garbled in the transcript, so the rest is left out.

The four kinds, summarised

  1. With the body (elbow, leg): permitted in all cases according to the Rosh, only for a permitted need according to the Ran.
  2. With an extension of the hand, such as pushing peels off a table with a knife: depends on the two approaches of the Meiri. The Taz permits it, for example to clear peels in order to eat. The Rabbi added that a different place in the Taz seems to add a limit, but that part is unclear.
  3. Indirect movement, like the ice cream and the frozen food, divided into (a) for the need of the permitted object, and (b) for the need of the mukseh.

The recording ends in the middle of the discussion of the third kind, on the extension of the hand.

Practical summary

  • Moving mukseh with a foot or elbow: the Rabbi follows the plain Rosh, so it is permitted even for the mukseh's own sake (for example, kicking a phone out of the way to protect it). The Hazon Ish is stricter.
  • Lifting something permitted that moves mukseh as a side effect: permitted if you need the permitted item. The Rabbi said this is clear in the Shulhan Arukh.
  • Moving something permitted in order to move the mukseh itself (a table to cover the phone): forbidden.
  • Pushing mukseh with a stick or knife: depends on the Meiri's two approaches. The Taz permits it, at least for the sake of eating.
  • The recording stops before the Rabbi finished the extension-of-the-hand case, so that point is left open here.
