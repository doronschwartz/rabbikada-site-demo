Topic: The blue thread on the sisit: what it is, how it was lost, and whether to wear it now Sources: Bemidbar 15:38; Menahot 38a, 43b, 44a; the Rambam, Hilchot Sisit; Rav Yishak HaLevi Herzog Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 28 June 2022, the first of five shiurim on the weekly parashah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The misvah of sisit and tekhelet

This was the first of a five-week series linked to the parashah, opening with the end of Parashat Shelah, read the Shabbat before. The Rabbi warned at the start that when you research a topic properly you sometimes reach surprising conclusions, and that he would leave his listeners to draw their own.

The Torah commands that a four-cornered garment have sisit on its corners "for their generations", and that on the fringe of each corner there be a petil tekhelet, a blue thread. Today most clothes do not have four corners, yet we make ourselves obligated by wearing a tallit katan. Rashi gives two meanings for the word sisit: the fringes themselves, or "gazing", as in the verse in Shir HaShirim, "peering through the lattice".

Why blue? Rabbi Meir in the Gemara (Menahot 43b) explains that the blue resembles the sea, the sea resembles the sky, and the sky brings a person to think of God. The white threads remind us of the misvot; the tekhelet reminds us of God Himself. God is no more in heaven than on earth, he added, but we naturally think of Him as above.

One does not hold back the other

The Gemara (Menahot 38a) asks what happens if you have only white or only blue. Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi held that each is essential to the other. The Hakhamim held that you still fulfil the misvah of sisit, and the halacha follows them, as the Rambam rules. The Rabbi stressed that the Gemara discusses this as a theoretical case, like many others; it never hints at a shortage of tekhelet.

How many threads should be blue? The verse says one petil, and the Rishonim read it three ways:

Opinion

Blue threads out of the eight

The Rambam

One (half of one of the four original threads)

The Raavad

Two (one full thread, doubled over)

Rashi and Tosafot

Four (half blue, half white)

He then made a point that would matter later. The Shulhan Arukh writes that the non-blue threads should ideally match the colour of the garment, so a red garment would have red sisit. The Rema says our custom is always white. Either way, coloured threads are not a problem. So the strongest argument of those who wear tekhelet today is: even if the identification is wrong, "so what?" You have dyed some threads a colour, and the misvah of sisit is not harmed at all.

What colour is tekhelet? Rashi calls it yarok, usually "green". The Rambam defines it as the colour of a clear sky in daylight. So Rashi means something between green and blue. The Rabbi suggested something like turquoise, while noting that others read the sources as a darker blue.

The hilazon, and how it was lost

The Gemara (Menahot 44a) says tekhelet comes from the hilazon, whose body resembles the sea, whose form is like a fish, and which comes up only once in seventy years, which is why it was expensive. It was found in the territory of Zevulun, on the northern coast, had a shell, and its colour was like kala ilan, indigo. The Gemara also warns that God will punish those who wear a cheap vegetable dye and pretend it is tekhelet.

All through the Mishnah and Gemara, until about 500 CE, people seem to have worn tekhelet. Then the Midrash (Bemidbar Rabbah), from around the eighth century, says: today we have only white, because the tekhelet has been nignaz. That can mean hidden away, or simply that it is no longer available. Rav Herzog suggested that the Arab conquest of the Land of Israel in the seventh century ended the dye industry. So tekhelet has been missing for some 1,300 to 1,400 years.

Two later voices explained the loss. The Or HaHayim noticed that "for their generations" comes before the blue thread in the verse: sisit would last for all generations, tekhelet would not. The Arizal taught that we are no longer on the spiritual level to wear tekhelet, and the Rabbi suspected this view helped the matter fade from discussion until the 1880s.

The Radziner Rebbe and Rav Herzog

Then came the Rebbe of Radzin in Poland, a Hasidic leader who was also learned in chemistry, medicine and engineering. He wrote three books arguing that anyone able to search for the hilazon must do so. He visited the great aquarium in Naples, concluded that the hilazon was the cuttlefish, learned from an Italian chemist how to make a dye from its ink, and within a year thousands of his Hasidim wore blue threads. Most authorities of the time rejected it, chiefly the Beit HaLevi, who argued that without an unbroken tradition we cannot reintroduce it, and the Arukh HaShulhan, who read nignaz as "hidden until Mashiah". His dye was also very dark, almost black. Still, the Rabbi credited him with starting the whole conversation, and the Radzin Hasidim and some other groups still wear this dye today.

Rav Yishak HaLevi Herzog, later Chief Rabbi of Ireland and then of the Land of Israel, wrote a doctorate at University College London on "Hebrew porphyrology", the study of the purple and blue dyes. He had the Radzin dye analysed, and chemists found its colour came from a reaction with iron sulphate, not from the creature itself. He turned instead to the snail Murex trunculus, which met nearly all the criteria. He had two doubts: its dye looked purple, and its shell was white and brown, not like the sea.

Both doubts were later answered. In its habitat the snail is coated and camouflaged so that it looks just like the seabed. And researchers found that sunlight turns the purple dye a light blue. In the 1980s rabbis and scientists produced a lasting blue wool from the snail, and that is the tekhelet most people wear today, in the last thirty-odd years.

Arguments for and against

The Rabbi went through the objections:

  • No tradition. Rav Hershel Schachter, who wears tekhelet himself, answers that having no tradition because you cannot know is one thing; refusing to look when you can find out is another. Our Hakhamim looked at the world: Rabbi Eliezer saw the high priest's sis in Rome, and the Ramban changed his view on the weight of the shekel after seeing coins in the Land of Israel.
  • Yuhara, appearing more pious than one's elders. Hakham Ben Sion Abba Shaul advised against it for this reason. The Rabbi found this hard. It makes sense when choosing one side of a halachic debate, but not for a misvah we simply could not do before. Jews in the Soviet Union could not perform brit milah for years; that never made it a tradition not to.
  • Standing out. Some advise wearing it only on the tallit katan, under one's clothes.
  • Pretending to wear tekhelet. The Gemara's warning, as the Ritva explains, is about deliberately deceiving people. It does not apply to someone who has done all he can to check and believes it is real.
  • Nignaz. The word does not have to mean "until Mashiah".

Generally, he noted, it has not been Sefaradi practice to wear tekhelet, and that does not make wearing it wrong. His own view: "I personally think it's good, there's nothing really to lose." He does not yet wear it himself but was strongly considering it. He would not "go mad" over it either: it is not our custom and it is not certain. All in all he thought it a decent practice and that the benefits may outweigh the drawbacks.

In questions, he guessed that Rav Schachter dyes one thread of the eight, like the Rambam, and said he had not seen any view from Rav Uziel in the literature.

Summary

  • The Torah commands a blue thread on the sisit, a reminder of God; without it the misvah of sisit is still fulfilled.
  • The Rishonim differ on one, two or four blue threads of the eight, and the colour seems to be light sky blue.
  • Tekhelet was lost around the seventh century; the Radziner Rebbe revived the search, and Rav Herzog's Murex snail is the source of today's tekhelet.
  • Since coloured threads do no harm, the Rabbi saw no substantial argument against wearing it, while noting it is not the Sefaradi custom.
