Topic: The laws of the shofar blasts, and Yihud Hashem as the key to Rosh Hashanah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Shasha synagogue, Monday 19 September 2022, less than a week before Rosh Hashanah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Thirty blasts from the Torah

The Rabbi opened by saying that the idea he would share had changed his own whole perspective on Rosh Hashanah when it settled in him some years ago. First, the laws of the shofar.

The Torah never calls the day Rosh Hashanah; that name comes from the Gemara. The Torah calls it a day of teruah. The word teruah appears three times (in Emor, Pinehas and Behar), so the Torah requires three teruot. "You shall sound a teruah" teaches that each teruah is preceded and followed by a long blast, a tekiah. That makes nine blasts.

But already in the time of the Gemara it was unclear what a teruah is: the sighing sound we now call shevarim, the short staccato blasts we call teruah, or both together. Rabbi Abbahu therefore instituted all three. Tekiah, shevarim, tekiah, three times, is nine. Tekiah, teruah, tekiah, three times, is another nine. Tekiah, shevarim-teruah, tekiah, three times, is twelve, since the combination counts as two sounds. Altogether thirty, which is what the Torah now requires of us.

Someone asked why we do not simply blow the combination, which includes both. The answer is that if teruah means only one of them, the other sound would be an interruption between the teruah and its surrounding tekiot.

Blasts in Musaf, and the hundred

Separately, the Hakhamim instituted blowing during Musaf, in the context of a sibbur, so that the blasts accompany the Musaf prayer. Old mahzorim show different numbers; over time it became accepted to blow a set after each of the three special blessings, Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot, adding thirty. That makes sixty.

This has practical consequences. When we blow for someone ill or elderly at home, thirty blasts suffice, because the extra ones belong only to Musaf with a congregation. And someone praying Musaf alone at home who knows how to blow should not blow during Musaf; he has no permission to interrupt his prayer that way. If he wants more blasts, he can blow after Musaf.

The hundred blasts appear nowhere in the Shulhan Arukh or the Rema. The Rema records the Ashkenazi custom of his time as thirty before Musaf and some during the repetition. The hundred is a custom from the Arizal, accepted almost everywhere, though Yemenite communities do not do it.

When may one speak?

Once the berakhah is said, one may not speak until all thirty blasts are finished, since until then we may not have fulfilled the misvah. Because the berakhah also covers the blasts in Musaf, one should not speak until those are done too. If there is a real need, for example a child asking something, one may speak after the Musaf blasts. Strictly, the final blasts of the hundred are custom, but it is good to wait for them.

He also raised the practice of saying vidui aloud between the sets of the first thirty, which the Ben Ish Hai writes. He found it very difficult to understand, since the berakhah covers all thirty and speaking may be an interruption before the misvah is complete. His view was that the vidui between the sets should be thought, not said aloud. A participant asked about the person who calls out each blast; the Rabbi answered that this is one person, accepted for the sake of the congregation.

Finally, the misvah is to hear the shofar, not to blow it. The berakhah is "lishmoa kol shofar." The Mishnah says one who blows into a pit and hears only the echo has not fulfilled it. And the strongest proof: a misvah performed with the body, like tefillin, sukkah or lulav, cannot be done by an agent. Since we can fulfil shofar through someone else's blowing, as with hearing the Megillah, the misvah must be the hearing.

Dogs barking at their master

The Zohar says that those who pray on Rosh Hashanah for children, health and livelihood are like dogs barking at their master. From a young age we learn that everything is decided on Rosh Hashanah, so we try to pray for everything under the sun. The Zohar says that misses the point. "Rosh Hashanah is not a time for praying for our needs."

To understand why, the Rabbi turned to the Ramhal, whose whole philosophy rests on one principle, Yihud Hashem, the oneness of God. This is not just that there are not two gods. We cannot truly comprehend any positive attribute of Hashem; when we say He is compassionate, we can only mean that He does not act in a way we would call uncompassionate. The one thing we can grasp fully is a negative: that there is no other power or force in the universe besides the will of Hashem. Our business, our health, our children, nothing happens outside it. Believing this in theory is one thing; living it is another, which is why we have the mezuzah and say Shema morning and evening. In the end of days, "Hashem will be One": now there is so much illusion of other powers.

Two ways Hashem runs the world

The Gemara says that whoever claims Hashem simply lets things go will have his own life let go. Yet these days are all about mercy and forgiveness. The Ramhal explains that there are two modes: hanhagat hamishpat, the conduct of justice, and hanhagat hayihud, the conduct of oneness.

Think of mishpat as consequences, not reward and punishment. Pour water and the table gets wet. Hashem created a world in which actions have consequences, and most of the time He is, as it were, bound by that system. But after the golden calf Hashem told Moshe there would be times when He would show favour even when it is not deserved. In hanhagat hayihud He sets the system aside.

The Rabbi's analogy: a child has done something wrong and the rules of the home say there are consequences. If the parent ignored them every day, there would be no rules. But on a rare special day, out of love, the parent says: today there are no rules. "I'm the one who sets the rules in this home." That is the parent's yihud.

That is Rosh Hashanah. When we say before the shofar that Hashem rises from the throne of judgment to the throne of mercy, we are asking Him to rule today not by mishpat but by yihud. And if He does, what is there to ask for? Requests belong to mishpat, where we plead that we deserve this or did not mean that. That is Yom Kippur, the day of judgment, when we go through our sins one by one and ask for everything. That is why we do not mention sins on Rosh Hashanah. Rosh Hashanah is about one thing: Hashem is King, and when we truly proclaim it, everything else falls into place.

Questions from the class

Asked why our Rosh Hashanah prayers do include requests such as "remember us for life," he said these additions come from the later Geonim, not from Hazal. The original prayer is pure: Malkhuyot, Zikhronot, Shofarot. Most people find the lofty idea hard to grasp, so additions were made that speak to them, but the heart of the day is the third blessing, which ends "the holy King."

Referring to that week's royal funeral, he noted that a monarch is heralded with trumpets: the shofar announces that there is a King. And when the King is present, the regular rules can be set aside, like a pardon. In America, he said, the President can pardon. Asked whether crying is right on Rosh Hashanah, he said it depends: tears of awe, like those of the people filing past the Queen's coffin, are the right kind.

Summary

  • The Torah requires nine blasts; doubt about the teruah makes thirty. The Musaf blasts are rabbinic, for a congregation, and the hundred is a custom from the Arizal.
  • The misvah is to hear the shofar; avoid speaking until the Musaf blasts are complete.
  • Yihud Hashem means there is no power in the world other than the will of Hashem.
  • Hashem normally runs the world by mishpat; on Rosh Hashanah we ask Him as King to rule by yihud.
  • So Rosh Hashanah is for proclaiming Hashem King, not for lists of requests; the details belong to Yom Kippur.
