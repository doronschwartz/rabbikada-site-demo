Why must we earn the good?

A participant found it strange: if Hashem is so good, why create a world to test whether we will become good, and only afterwards give us the good? The Rabbi answered in the voice of Hashem: "I am a loving person. I'm a perfect person. I want everything around me to be perfect and good," so I created as many beings as possible to have the perfect goodness. "Now, I want you to work for it. I want you to earn it."

Like a speed camera that makes you slow down, Hashem's guidance is there to help us acquire the good, "because I want you to have the best goodness." He asked the group to let go of childish conceptions of the world as a battle in which everything is there to trip you up.

Punishment in the afterlife

The discussion turned to what happens to those who choose badly. The Rabbi explained that what we call "punishment" in the afterlife is a borrowed term. It is really a consequence.

Karet. The most severe consequence in the Torah is karet, being cut off, which applies to 36 of the misvot, such as intentionally breaking Shabbat or eating on Yom Kippur. Karet means that after death nothing happens to you: you have no identity, you are simply cut off. "It just ends up with nothing."

Gehinnom. Gehinnom lasts a maximum of twelve months, and it is essentially a cleansing process. His analogy: an apple that is bruised all over, with nothing good left, you simply throw away. That is the person who is cut off. But a decent apple with quite a few bruises still has something to it, so you cut away the bruises and keep the rest. That is Gehinnom: "cutting the bruises off the apple." The good you did remains; the bad is removed. It is painful, but the pain is not physical fire; it is more like the burning of embarrassment, a subject the book returns to.

This is unlike school punishment, such as writing lines, where there is no connection between the misdeed and the penalty.

A participant noted that some find this uncomfortable: could the most wicked person, such as Hitler, simply cease to exist? The Rabbi mentioned a view that one ceases to exist yet retains some awareness of it, though he called this quite speculative, and noted that other schools hold that ceasing to exist is not enough of a deterrent. How Hashem weighs these things, he said, "that's not for me to answer."

Suffering in this world. Another asked about the passages in the Gemara where Sages suffered illness so as to receive their punishment in this world. The Rabbi said that is also a cleansing process: painful, but its purpose is to cleanse.

After death. Asked how a person can benefit from merits after death, if they can no longer change, the Rabbi explained that when someone learns or does good in their memory, the person who died has caused that good, and in that sense has elevated their own status. Even more directly, when we learn the Torah of Rashi or the Rambam, "Rashi is still alive. Rambam is still alive."

What is a test?

The Hebrew for a test, nisayon, shares its root with nes, a banner raised high, like the pole on which Moshe raised the copper serpent. The purpose of a test is to raise you up, not to trip you up.

Compare a university exam. The lecturer is not trying to catch you out; he wants to make sure you have learned properly before you practise as a midwife or whatever it may be. That is different from someone "waiting behind the corner" to see if you fall. Hashem does not do the second kind. The Rabbi rejected the idea that Hashem placed dinosaur bones in the ground to test whether we would still believe: "that's the kind of test which I think should be rejected."

Do we give anything to Hashem?

A participant asked: when we choose good, we fulfil Hashem's will, so are we not, in a sense, giving to Him? The Rabbi answered that Hashem does not "want" in the way we do. Good simply bestows goodness; the benefit is ours.

Rav Dessler's point of choice

When we choose the good, we acquire goodness within ourselves; that is what free will is about. The Rabbi brought Rav Dessler's well-known idea that each person's free will is at a different point.

Imagine a boy growing up in a family that kills and loots. He decides he does not want to kill, only to steal, and spends his life stealing. Another grows up with everything handed to him in a religious home, and drops a level, for example no longer praying three times a day, while still keeping Shabbat and kashrut. Who has acquired more goodness? Rav Dessler says the first: he rose from his starting point, while the second went down.

A participant raised the opposite worry: that the expectation to keep growing can make people give up. The Rabbi agreed that growth is clearest in the school and university years, and less visible later, and accepted the point that maintaining what you do year after year is itself special.

Another asked: if so, why not raise children without religion and let them grow? The Rabbi gave two answers. Parents have a responsibility to guide their children on the right path. And the risk is far greater: a child given direction is far more likely to succeed, whereas one given no shaping may rise, but is more likely to end up much worse. Once children reach adulthood, they become responsible for their own growth.

Summary

  • We are meant to earn and own the good Hashem gives; His guidance is there to help us acquire it.
  • What we call punishment in the afterlife is a consequence: karet is being cut off, and Gehinnom, at most twelve months, is a cleansing, like cutting the bruises off a good apple.
  • A nisayon, from nes (a banner), is meant to raise us up, not to trick us.
  • Following Rav Dessler, free will is measured from each person's own starting point.
