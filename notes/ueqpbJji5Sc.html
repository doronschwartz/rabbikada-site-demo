These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A principle with two versions

The class, the first after the winter break, was dedicated to the memory of the mother of two regular participants, who had passed away a month earlier.

The Rabbi reviewed the first three principles: God's existence, His unique unity, and that He has no body. The fourth, he said, is the only one of the 13 whose meaning is not certain, because the Rambam's text exists in two versions. The main text says:

"The fourth principle is God's priority: the One we have described is first in the absolute sense, and nothing that exists outside Him is prior to Him." The source is "the eternal God is a refuge" (Devarim 33:27).

Some editions add in brackets: "And know that the great foundation of the Torah of Moshe is that the world was created from nothing."

Without the brackets, the principle says that God is first and everything depends on Him. With them, it also says He created the world yesh meayin, something from nothing. Which did the Rambam mean? Most of the class came from the Moreh Nevuchim (II:13-25), where he sets out three views held by those who believe in God. (He does not even discuss Epicurus, who denied God, and from whose name we get apikoros.)

1. Aristotle: an eternal universe

For Aristotle, God and the universe have always existed together. There is no beginning; "Bereshit" makes no sense. Creation from nothing is impossible, and nature can never change, so there are no miracles. The Rambam brings eight proofs for this view, four from Aristotle and four from his followers, and refutes them all. The Rabbi explained two:

The heavens never change. Everything material moves and decays, yet the heavenly spheres move in perfect circles without change, so they must be eternal. The Rambam's parable: imagine a child raised on an island seeing only adults. Tell him that every person began inside his mother, and he will call it impossible. You cannot work back from how something exists now to how it came to be.

God's will cannot change. If the world had a beginning, God must once not have wanted it and then wanted it; change implies imperfection. The Rambam answers that time itself was created. Before the universe there is no "before", so the question cannot be asked.

The Rambam concludes that Aristotle's view cannot be reconciled with the Torah.

2. Plato: creation from eternal matter

Plato held that the world had a beginning, but God formed it from matter that has always existed, "like iron in the hands of a smith". The Rishonim call this matter hyle.

Surprisingly, some Rishonim accepted this. The Ibn Ezra, on the first verse of the Torah, rejects the view that "bara" means creating from nothing, since the Torah says God "created" man, who was formed from the earth. "Bara," he writes, means to cut or set a boundary. The Ralbag also held that creation from nothing is impossible, so there must have been matter.

Does this not compete with God? The Rabbi's analogy was a piano that has always played, and music that has always sounded. Both are eternal, yet the music depends on the piano, and the piano is primary. So too, eternal matter would still depend on God.

Someone asked how this fits with God's oneness. The Rabbi admitted, "It's very difficult... I have this problem as well." But we exist, and that does not detract from God's unity, because we depend on Him. The same would be true of such matter.

The Kuzari, who himself believes in creation from nothing, writes that a believer who felt compelled to accept eternal matter, or even earlier worlds, would not damage his belief that this world was created at a particular time. The mainstream view, stated clearly by the Ramban at the start of Bereshit, is creation from nothing. But those who held otherwise were not called heretics.

3. The Torah of Moshe: creation from nothing

The third view, held by most of our Sages, is that God created the world from absolutely nothing. The Rambam says that the verses could be read in Plato's way, but there is no need, since Plato's view has not been proved. Elsewhere he writes that only superficial people think Plato agrees with us.

So what did the Rambam hold? The Rabbi's conclusion: he himself believed in creation from nothing, and probably wrote the bracketed line. But like the Kuzari, he did not count the Platonic view as heresy. In the Mishneh Torah his list of heretics includes "one who says that He alone is not the First and the Rock of all", but says nothing about creation from nothing. The essence of the principle is that all matter depends on God, who is its author and gave it form.

Why "Bereshit"?

The Rabbi then turned to the practical lesson. On 10 Tevet, just commemorated, we also remember the translation of the Torah into Greek. King Talmai (Ptolemy) placed 72 sages in separate houses to translate it, and all made the same changes in the same places, to prevent misunderstanding. One was "let us make man", which they rendered "I will make man". Another was the very first verse. A reader without tradition might take "Bereshit bara Elohim" to mean that something called "Bereshit" created God, so they wrote "God created in the beginning".

So why does the Torah open with "Bereshit" and not "Elohim"? Because that first word holds the whole purpose of creation. Purpose only exists with free will. If nature and God simply coexist and neither can change the other, as Aristotle held, there are no miracles and no purpose. But if there was a beginning, God is in control of matter. And we, created in His image, can also rise above our matter: our instincts, our physical nature. The Vilna Gaon said the whole Torah is contained in the word Bereshit.

Whether from nothing or from something, there was a beginning, God formed the universe, and "we mustn't go through our lives thinking that there is a whole universe out there... living of its own accord".

Shifra and Puah

He closed with a thought on the week's parashah, Shemot, in memory of the mother to whom the class was dedicated. The parashah is full of heroic women: Yokheved, Sipporah, and the midwives Shifra and Puah, who risked their lives to save the Jewish babies. Rashi explains their names. Shifra "beautified" the newborn, arranging its limbs. Puah "cooed" and soothed it.

Why are the saviours of the Jewish people named for such small things, and not for rescue and salvation? Because how a deed is done matters as much as the deed. The Talmud says that one who gives charity receives six blessings, and one who adds kind words receives eleven more, though a hungry man would choose the meal over the smile. Shifra and Puah saved lives, and remembered the little things too. So, he said, we remember a person not only for what she did, but for how she did it: with a constant smile and "Barukh Hashem".

Summary

  • The fourth principle certainly means that God is first and all existence depends on Him; one version adds creation from nothing.
  • The Rambam rejects Aristotle's eternal universe, refuting his proofs, partly by showing that time itself was created.
  • Plato's creation from eternal matter was held by the Ibn Ezra and the Ralbag; the Kuzari and the Rambam reject it, but do not call it heresy.
  • The Rambam himself held creation from nothing, the mainstream view.
  • "Bereshit" teaches that God is master of matter, and so we too can master our own nature.
