Sefer: Derech Hashem by Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto (the Ramhal) Section: Part 1, Chapter 4, paragraph 5 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Derech Hashem series at Tiferet Eyal, Wednesday 17 February 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

This was a small evening group on Zoom, and after the paragraph itself most of the class became an open discussion. The discussion continues in the next recording.

Recap: body and soul in balance

A participant summarised the previous class. Man is constantly involved with the physical, and the material is not simply negative: we need it to reach perfection. We have two parts to our identity, body and soul, and we cannot neglect either. You cannot neglect the soul by living a purely hedonistic life, nor the body by living a purely spiritual one.

The Rabbi added the example of the nazir, who separates himself from wine for a period and at the end must bring a sin offering. The Gemara asks why, and answers: because he denied himself what the Torah permitted. It is easier to cut oneself off from the world than to engage with it in an elevated way. The nazir's process may be necessary for him, as a means to an end, but it is not the ideal. To let us engage with the physical world without losing our spirituality, Hashem gave us a framework with boundaries.

The question: why not my own way?

People often ask: I accept that I need a balance between physical and spiritual, and that spirituality matters, but why can't I find my own way to connect to God? Why must I keep the intricacies of the misvot and halacha?

The Ramhal answers in paragraph 5. The framework is the misvot, positive and negative. Every one of them is there to refine an element of the body and remove its darkness, whether by doing a positive misvah or refraining from a prohibition. This is the idea of the 248 positive and 365 negative misvot corresponding to the parts of the body. The Rabbi noted that the point is the idea behind it, not whether the anatomical numbers are exact.

All the misvot, and all the details of each misvah, are built on the true nature of man and on what he needs for his perfection. Hashem's great wisdom knows the essence of every human being, paid attention to all of it, and commanded us all the misvot for our good.

The instruction manual

So other forms of spirituality are not the path to complete perfection. A person may feel that meditating on a mountain gives him a special connection with God, "that's fine. And that's good." But if Hashem created human beings, as we believe, and gave us an instruction manual for perfecting ourselves, it makes most sense to connect through the misvot He gave.

The Rabbi acknowledged honestly that halacha can sometimes become "fossilized," and people can lose the bigger picture and get stuck in the details, something the Ramhal addresses in the next paragraph. But generally, the misvot are designed to bring us to the highest perfection.

What about women?

The Rabbi then raised a question for the group. Many of the 248 positive and 365 negative misvot do not apply to women, including some that women eagerly do, such as shofar and lulav, which are not halachically required of them. If the purpose of the misvot is to perfect us, why do women have a different set of obligations? Surely women need the same perfection.

Participants suggested that women can do these misvot voluntarily, or that the exemption reflected the demands of raising children. The Rabbi pressed the point: "It's impossible to say that God has created a system where you're not obligated to do something and only if you choose to do it, then that brings you perfection." If it is part of perfection, Hashem would have commanded it. This question was taken up again in the next recording.

Halacha without a Sanhedrin

A participant suggested that the exemptions reflect interpretations shaped by the society of the time. The Rabbi described two approaches. Some hold that everything Hazal said was handed down letter by letter from Sinai, and will never change. Others, and he said this is how the Rambam approached it, hold that these rulings were the interpretation of the verses by the Sanhedrin of their day, codified at that time.

"This is the real problem of exile": we have no Sanhedrin, so rulings dating back 1,500 to 2,000 years cannot be updated. A beit din may not annul the ruling of an earlier one unless it is greater in wisdom and number, and we have no such body.

He gave examples:

  • Two days of Yom Tov. Every year people ask why we still keep two days outside Israel, when the calendar has been fixed since the fourth century. His first answer is that they are right that the original reason no longer applies. But the Gemara states explicitly that we keep it as a custom from the time when the dates were uncertain, and that cannot be reinterpreted.
  • Washing before bread. This has nothing to do with hygiene, despite claims during Covid that Hazal anticipated it. Holy food had to be eaten with clean hands; this was extended from the kohanim to everyone. It is no longer practical, yet we do it because it was instituted in the time of the Gemara. "Whoever's advocating to cancel two days Yom Tov should equally be advocating to cancel washing hands before eating bread."
  • Mayim aharonim. This was instituted because of a dangerous "salt of Sedom" when people ate with their hands. Tosafot already wrote, around 800 years ago, that this no longer applies. Those who follow Kabbalah give other reasons, but by the strict letter of the law, the reason given was a health reason.
  • Meat and fish. The Gemara treats eating them together as a danger, something we do not see today, yet the practice remains. "This is what I mean by fossilization of halacha... This is part of our sadness of Tisha BeAv."

Asked about women learning Gemara, he explained that no Orthodox institution goes against the Gemara, but some reinterpret it. Rav Yosef Dov Soloveitchik read the Gemara in Sotah differently and famously taught Talmud to women. Some things, though, like the second day of Yom Tov, cannot be reinterpreted.

Asked what to say to someone who calls Judaism archaic, he said that rabbinic Judaism is stuck because we are in exile. "That's why we're crying out every single day, three times a day: Hashivah shofetenu kevarishonah." Unlike Parliament, which can change laws today, "we can't change any laws."

Other questions

  • The year 6000 and the environment. A question had been posted that day in a rabbinic group: if the world as we know it ends by the year 6000, why be environmentally friendly? The Rabbi answered that your house will not be around in a thousand years either, yet you look after it. A participant compared it to looking after our bodies, which will not last forever, and the Rabbi agreed: Hashem gave us a world and a body for this life, and we must look after both.
  • Why not establish a Sanhedrin now? There have been attempts, supported by Rabbi Adin Steinsaltz, but the vast majority hold that it cannot be done before the messianic era, since it depends on full sovereignty.
  • Messianic times. The Rabbi said the State of Israel shows we are in messianic times, which is a process, not an overnight change: 1948, 1967, 1973, and the recent ties with Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates.
  • Is each generation worse? A participant felt so. The Rabbi disagreed: in sexual ethics the world has gone down, but in many things, such as care for women and children, it is a far better place than fifty years ago.

Summary

  • Body and soul must both be engaged; withdrawing from the world, like the nazir, is not the ideal.
  • The misvot and all their details are designed by the One who knows human nature, so they are the surest path to perfection, more than any self-made spirituality.
  • The Rabbi raised the question of why women's obligations differ if the misvot perfect us, continued in the next recording.
  • Without a Sanhedrin, some rulings whose reasons no longer apply cannot be changed; this is a tragedy of exile and a reason we pray for its restoration.
