Topic: The concept of gilgul (reincarnation): its sources, the Rishonim who rejected it, and common questions about it Source: The Zohar; the Ramban on Vayeshev and Iyov; Rav Hayim Vital, Shaar HaGilgulim; the Ramhal; Sefer Haredim Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Torah Way's "Start Your Day with Torah", North West London, Thursday 25 February 2016 (16 Adar I)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

What is a gilgul?

For his first talk in the series, the Rabbi chose a subject most people have wondered about. A person's main identity is the neshamah, the soul. The body is clothing for the soul while it is in this world, like the spacesuit an astronaut wears on the moon. Gilgul means that after the body dies, the neshamah can come down again in another body, a second or even a third time.

Where does the idea come from?

Gilgul is not mentioned anywhere in Tanakh. Nor, he pointed out, is it found in the Bavli, the Yerushalmi or the Midrashim. Its source is the Zohar and the Kabbalah. For that reason some of the Rishonim did not accept it. Rav Saadiah Gaon rejects it sharply in Emunot VeDeot, the Rambam never mentions it, and Rav Yoseph Albo in Sefer HaIkkarim also rejects it.

On the other hand, the Ramban explains several passages through gilgul. Onan refused to have a child with Tamar because the child would really carry the soul of his brother Er, and the Ramban explains the whole of Sefer Iyov in these terms. Among the Aharonim the idea became central. Rav Menashe ben Yisrael, the rabbi in Amsterdam who petitioned Oliver Cromwell to readmit the Jews to England, wrote about it at length in Nishmat Hayim, and the Ramhal and many others discuss it.

The Rashash, on the Gemara in Berakhot that a person should leave the world as free of sin as he entered it, notes that this is difficult according to gilgul, since a soul enters the world to repair earlier sins. And one of the Aharonim writes that had the Rambam and Rav Saadiah seen the Kabbalah, they would have accepted it.

The Rabbi once asked Rav Yaakov Hillel, the mekubal from Jerusalem, whether one must believe in gilgul. He answered that since the Zohar has become so central (the Beit Yoseph quotes it some 800 times), gilgul is now part of our emunah.

Which body will rise at Tehiyat HaMetim?

If the same neshamah lived in three bodies, which one is revived? The Rabbi set out three views:

View

Who is revived

All the bodies

Each body rises, with the neshamah divided between them

The last body

Only the body that completed the soul's repair (the Selah on Berakhot 58, among others)

A combination

The final body is made up of the parts each life repaired

The Rif's reading of the blessing over a cemetery, "He knows the number of all of you and will revive you", may reflect the first two views. The last-body view fits the principle that a misvah is credited to the one who completes it.

Why is Gehinnom not enough?

If Gehinnom cleanses a person, why come back at all? The Reshit Hokhmah, quoting the Ramak, and the Ramhal give three reasons:

  1. Gehinnom can purge a sin, the way salt draws blood out of meat, but it cannot supply a misvah a person failed to do. For that he must come back.
  2. Sometimes the cleansing is like an amputation: the person is saved but left "blemished", and needs another life to make himself whole.
  3. Wrongs between people, such as an unpaid debt, can only be put right in this world. The Gra said that both the debtor and the lender may need to return.

How many times, and does one know?

Rav Hayim Vital writes that a person who does not improve is given up to three more chances. One who keeps improving is given as many as he needs.

The Sefer Haredim writes that a person who comes back as a human does not know he is a gilgul. But the Gra says that everyone has an intuition about his purpose in the world. An area where someone keeps stumbling may be exactly what he came back to repair.

Someone might think: if I get more chances, why not enjoy this life? First, you do not know whether this is your last chance. And the Ramak compares a person who arrives in the next world with only physical achievements to a merchant who comes home after five years abroad carrying coal, to a town full of coal. The disappointment would be enormous.

Gilgul and suffering

Many Aharonim use gilgul to explain why the righteous suffer, a question asked by Avraham, Moshe and Yirmeyahu. The Ramban reads Iyov this way, and Nishmat Hayim uses it to explain why children are born with disabilities or die young. The Rabbi mentioned that the Hazon Ish would stand up for severely disabled children, saying they had almost completed what they came into the world to do.

Summary

  • Gilgul means a neshamah can return in another body to complete its task.
  • It is not in Tanakh or the Talmud and comes from the Zohar; Rav Saadiah Gaon, the Rambam and the Ikkarim did not accept it, but the Aharonim and the Kabbalists did, and it is now part of our belief.
  • There are three views about which body rises at Tehiyat HaMetim.
  • Gilgul does what Gehinnom cannot: it lets a person do misvot he missed and put right wrongs to others.
  • The lesson is not to rely on second chances, but to make the most of this life.
