Melakhah 17: Meisekh, Preparing the Warp

Every woven cloth has two sets of threads. The hutei shti (the warp) run along the length of the cloth. The hutei erev (the weft) run across it. Meisekh means preparing the warp threads so that the weft can be woven into them. It takes two forms, and each one is a melakhah in its own right:

  • Disentangling and straightening the warp. After dyeing and spinning, the threads are frizzy and stuck together. They are beaten apart, sometimes with a stick (shovet), straightened and stretched, so that each thread will be in its place when the weaving begins. This is like Menapes (beating out the raw wool), but it comes after the spinning.
  • Fixing the warp to the loom. Each thread is tied to pegs at either end of the loom. Alternatively, one very long thread is wound up and down between the pegs until the cloth is wide enough. Simply putting the threads onto the loom is already the melakhah.

The Mishnah Berurah says the same: stretching the threads along the length and width of the loom is the av melakhah of Meisekh, and beating the threads apart is its toladah. Separating threads during the weaving belongs to Oreg.

The book adds some applications:

  • The first stage of darning a hole, laying threads across it so that others can be woven into them, is Meisekh. The interlacing itself is Oreg.
  • Putting wool onto knitting needles or a knitting machine is probably Meisekh, even before a single stitch is made.
  • No loom is needed. Laying out the canes, raffia or thin wood that will be the warp of a wicker basket, mat or fence is Meisekh.

Why is a preparation a melakhah? Because that is how it was in the Mishkan. Every separate stage of a process was its own melakhah. "Just like Borer": taking the seeds out only prepares them for grinding, kneading and baking the dye, and yet it is a melakhah.

Straightening threads that are not for weaving

It is permitted to disentangle threads for any purpose other than weaving: the fringe of a scarf, a tassel, pom-pom strands. For the same reason a sheitel may be brushed and straightened on Shabbat, even when it is quite tangled. The Shemirat Shabbat KeHilkhatah is stricter about a sheitel that is completely tangled and cannot be worn, and treats it as Makeh BePatish.

The same applies to sisit. Separating the strings is a problem only when they are first made, if they come tangled. Strings that became mixed up afterwards may be separated on Shabbat, and doing so is a good thing: the Mishnah Berurah says the strings should always be kept separate.

In short: Meisekh is preparing the warp for weaving, by straightening and stretching it or by attaching it to the loom (or to any frame, as in basket-weaving). It is forbidden by the Torah.

Melakhah 18: Oseh Batei Nirin, the Harness

Large-scale weaving is not done by hand, in and out. Every second warp thread is tied to a harness above the loom: one harness holds threads 1, 3, 5 and so on, the other holds 2, 4, 6. Lifting one harness raises half the threads and opens a tunnel. A shuttle carrying the weft is thrown through it. Then that harness is lowered and the other raised, which traps the weft and opens a new tunnel, and the shuttle goes back. The weaving moves fast, with no fingers working in and out.

According to most Rishonim, Oseh Batei Nirin is attaching the warp threads to the rings of the harnesses. Nothing has been woven yet: throwing the shuttle is Oreg. The harness is the last preparation, and it "finally sets the stage" for the weaving.

The Rambam (Hilchot Shabbat chapter 9) explains it differently, perhaps because tying on a harness seemed too small to be its own melakhah. For him, Oseh Batei Nirin is making netting: a weave with open, box-like holes, as opposed to ordinary tight weaving. It is a different craft that makes a different material, so it is a melakhah of its own. In the Mishkan this was the netting of the hangings of the courtyard, and the nets for catching the hilazon.

The Mishnah Berurah adds: whoever makes a sieve, a basket, or a bed strung with rope, and lays the strands down crosswise before the weaving, has done this melakhah. This applies to any material, even straw.

The Twin Melakhot

Oreg and Posea are the first of the "twin" melakhot, where a positive melakhah has a negative partner:

The rule for every pair: the undoing can never be more severe than the doing. If the writing is only derabbanan (letters drawn on a misted window in winter), rubbing them out is only derabbanan. Building a temporary tent is derabbanan, so taking it down is derabbanan. Taking down a makeshift door is derabbanan even when it is done in order to rebuild.

Melakhah 19: Oreg, Weaving

Oreg is interlacing threads, whether by throwing the shuttle on a loom, knitting by hand, or plaiting a basket. Every material counts: wool, linen, cotton, silk, wicker, and even detached hair. The amount for liability is two threads.

Examples:

  • The woven holders that some people put on the lulav (the Moroccans use them too) are "classical Oreg".
  • Drawing a pulled thread back into a cloth, such as the thread that has come out of a jacket or a tablecloth, is Oreg. "I think this is the most practical application."
  • Plaiting a sheitel is Oreg, because the hair is detached. Usually the plait is not permanent, so it is derabbanan; a plait meant to stay is from the Torah.
  • Plaiting hair on the head has nothing to do with weaving. It is forbidden as Boneh (derabbanan, since it is not permanent), and undoing a plait is Soter.

Melakhah 20: Posea, Unravelling

The Rambam explains Posea as the opposite of Oreg: unravelling woven material. In the Mishkan, a finished piece was taken off the loom and its ends were unravelled into a fringe, so that it could be joined to the next piece. Posea must have a constructive purpose: to weave further, to make the garment look better, or to use the thread. Pulling out a thread out of boredom, destructively, is not the melakhah.

The Raavad explains Posea as cutting the finished cloth off the loom. On that view it is not really Oreg's twin; it is closer to Meisekh, which is putting the threads on.

The Mishnah Berurah: unravelling two threads makes one liable. Doctors must take care with bandages: separating out threads from a gauze bandage is Posea.

Posea or Koreah? Koreah is tearing through the whole material. Posea is working on the individual threads, pulling them out of the weave. So drawing a pulled thread back into a tablecloth is Oreg, and pulling it further out to remove it is Posea.

Undoing a plait in a sheitel is Posea, the reverse of the Oreg of plaiting it. Undoing a plait in one's own hair is Soter.

A last example: when the winding at the end of the sisit strings comes loose, unravelling the strings is also Posea.

Summary

  • Meisekh: preparing the warp, by disentangling it or by attaching it to the loom or any frame. Brushing a sheitel or separating sisit strings is permitted.
  • Oseh Batei Nirin: attaching the warp to the harness (most Rishonim), or making netting (the Rambam).
  • Oreg: any weaving, with any material, including drawing a thread back into cloth and plaiting a sheitel.
  • Posea: unravelling for a constructive purpose (the Rambam), or cutting the cloth off the loom (the Raavad).
  • In every twin melakhah, the undoing is never more severe than the doing.
