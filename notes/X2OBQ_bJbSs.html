Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 288, seifim 1-10 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 1 February 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Three kinds of fasting on Shabbat

Last week's class covered Musaf (286) and visiting the sick and comforting mourners on Shabbat (287). Siman 288 has three parts: fasting on Shabbat in general, including diets; fasting for a bad dream; and praying and crying out on Shabbat.

Seif 1: it is forbidden to fast on Shabbat until six hours, midday. The Rema adds: even if one is busy learning Torah.

The background is Oneg Shabbat, "vekarata laShabbat oneg", which is from the Prophets. Some Rishonim hold that the three meals are from the Torah, learned from the three times "hayom" in the account of the man in Parashat Beshalah. Either way, fasting on Shabbat is a serious problem.

The Rabbi set out three separate levels:

  • Fasting the whole day: a full fast means not eating until sunset. This violates at least the words of the Prophets, and possibly a Torah misvah.
  • Holding back on purpose for any length of time: deciding to skip your usual Shabbat afternoon tea for an hour to afflict yourself or atone is a "taanit shaot", a fast of hours. That is forbidden, at least rabbinically.
  • Not getting round to eating until midday: even with no intention to fast, the Sages forbade reaching midday without eating or drinking. Being in shul or learning Torah does not excuse it.

A fast means no food or drink at all, so a drink of water is enough to avoid it; there is no minimum amount except on Yom Kippur. Asked about accepting a fast the afternoon before, he explained that a private fast must normally be accepted during the previous afternoon, "and a lot of people who fast just don't know" this. A whole day without food is still a violation even if it was not accepted.

In the winter midday in London can be 11.40, so one could easily reach it in shul. Before Shaharit one may drink water, tea or coffee, since the obligation of Kiddush only begins after prayer (for Sefaradim, after Musaf). Eating cake before Shaharit is not allowed as on any weekday. If a long service would leave someone hungry and unable to concentrate, there is room to eat a little first, but the Rabbi did not approve of a whole breakfast before prayers.

When not eating is itself Oneg

Seif 2: if eating is harmful to someone, he should not eat, since this is his Oneg. The Mishnah Berurah: if he can manage a kezayit of bread, he should, to fulfil the meal and say Birkat HaMazon.

His example: someone with a medical test on Saturday night who must not eat for a few hours beforehand. Looking after your health is enjoyment, so it is permitted. If you have a choice and it is not urgent, better to book the test for another time so as not to fast on Shabbat. If you have no choice, take the test.

The Rema adds that if crying relieves someone's distress, it is permitted on Shabbat. The Biur Halacha says a very emotional person who will be upset by it should not visit the sick on Shabbat. The Rabbi said it is hard to know when crying is sadness and when it is relief.

Seif 3: someone who fasts every day, for whom eating on Shabbat upsets his routine, may fast. Rabbi Yehudah HeHasid was known for this. "I don't think we're on this level."

Diets on Shabbat

"Shabbat always ruins my diet" is a common question. The Rabbi quoted Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul's distinction: a diet for health, such as a doctor's instruction or before an operation, is like seif 2, and one need not eat; a diet only for appearance does not justify missing the meals.

He added two comments:

  • Today we know how harmful excess weight is, so there is an argument that almost any diet for someone overweight is for health, even if the intention is to look slimmer. He excluded anorexia.
  • A diet is no excuse to skip bread and eat everything else. Have a kezayit of bread (about 27 grams, one slice) at each of the three meals, and cut down on the meat and chicken instead.

If the whole week is fruit and vegetables only, or no carbohydrates, then for health it is right not to eat bread on Shabbat. For appearance alone some would forbid it, but he thinks there is room to argue it is also for health.

Fasting for a bad dream

Seif 4: the Gemara permits fasting on Shabbat after a bad dream on Friday night. It is the only private fast that need not be accepted the afternoon before, since the dream came at night. The reason is not atonement but distress: someone who is anxious all day is allowed to fast to relieve it, like the crying. But he must then fast another day to atone for fasting on Shabbat. If Sunday is too hard or a festive day, he fasts later in the week. The Rema adds a case of a dream during a Shabbat afternoon sleep: fast until nightfall, then fast on Sunday too.

The Rabbi understands the Gemara to mean this is not the right course, only a concession: "the best thing to do is to enjoy Shabbat. That's the best kaparah." A true servant of Hashem who has a frightening dream would take it as a message and increase his Oneg Shabbat.

Seif 5: the Rishonim limited it. Some say only if the dream came three times. Some say we no longer know which dreams are good or bad, so it no longer applies. The Beit Yosef brings three dreams for which one fasts on Shabbat: a Sefer Torah burning, Yom Kippur at Neilah, and the beams of one's house or one's teeth falling. Others add more. The Mishnah Berurah quotes the Shelah that even for these it is better to spend the day in Torah and prayer and not fast on Shabbat.

His own approach, which he said he gives when people call him about dreams:

  • Most dreams we cannot interpret, and many are triggered by something we saw that day.
  • A fast is meant to bring you close to Hashem; it is not "hocus pocus."
  • Do not ignore a dream completely, as Hashem sends messages. "Do teshuvah, give sedakah, think of something in your life you can make better, and move on." Interpret it for the good.
  • Unless someone truly cannot cope, do not fast on Shabbat for a dream: "It's wrong to do so."

Crying out and Mi Sheberakh on Shabbat

Seif 7: one may eat the day meal earlier or later as suits one's digestion, though ideally before midday.

Seifim 8-10: we do not fast on Shabbat for public troubles, do not cry out, and do not blow the shofar, except for famine, a city besieged, a ship in a storm, or one person pursued. Then one may cry out and pray, but not blow, unless blowing is to call for help. Shabbat is about the present; requests are about the future. Seif 10 extends this to anyone dangerously ill. The Rabbi mentioned the Misallim prayers for the gravely ill in Spanish and Portuguese siddurim.

This is why a Mi Sheberakh on Shabbat for someone who is ill but not in danger, with flu or a cold, must add "Shabbat hi milizok", "Shabbat prevents us from crying out." It shows we are not crying out, only praying. He always adds it, and on Yom Tov too. For someone seriously ill, no addition is needed.

Practical summary

  • Have a drink before or during the service so that you do not reach midday on Shabbat without eating or drinking.
  • Do not skip a meal or a drink on purpose on Shabbat; if eating is harmful to you, you need not eat.
  • On a diet, still eat a kezayit of bread at each meal and cut back elsewhere.
  • For a bad dream, enjoy Shabbat rather than fast; do teshuvah and give sedakah.
  • In a Shabbat Mi Sheberakh for someone not dangerously ill, add "Shabbat hi milizok."
