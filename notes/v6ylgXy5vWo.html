Topic: Rain during a meal or sleep, forcing oneself to stay in the sukkah, and why we say Leshev BaSukkah only when eating Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 639 (Seifim 6-8), with the Rema, the Mishnah Berurah and the Biur Halacha; the opening of Siman 640 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Thursday 26 September 2024, a week before Rosh Hashanah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

When the rain stops

The previous class had covered the first night, learning in the sukkah, and how much rain exempts you. This class picked up at Seif 6: what happens when things change in the middle of a meal or a night's sleep.

During a meal. If rain drives you indoors and then stops, you need not go back until the meal is over. This is from the Gemara and accepted by all: it is too much bother (tirhah). Think of a summer picnic: once it rains and you carry everything inside, you finish indoors. The Biur Halacha brings a debate if you have brought everything in but not yet started eating. The Rabbi's suggestion: it depends on the bother. A table for fifteen already set in the dining room need not be moved back; for a couple, where it is easy, go back. Once the meal is over, if the weather is good again, return to the sukkah for tea and dessert. In England, he added, the weather changes every five minutes, so by the time you carry everything out it may be raining again.

During sleep (Seif 7). If you went inside to sleep because of rain, you may stay inside until dawn or until you wake. Getting up at 2am does not oblige you to go back out. If you wake after dawn and want to sleep more, there is a strong argument that you should go back out, unless setting up again is so much bother that you would not get back to sleep. If you were ready for bed indoors and the rain stopped before you fell asleep, the Mishnah Berurah brings both views; again it depends on how much bother it is. "A lot of it is up to common sense."

Drizzle. For eating, the measure of rain is enough to spoil a simple dish. For sleeping, even a light drizzle is enough, as anyone who has tried to sleep in a sukkah knows.

"Ignorant practice"

The Rema rules that one who is exempt and forces himself to stay in the sukkah is called a hedyot, an ignorant person. The Mishnah compares rain on Sukkot to a servant who pours a drink for his master, and the master throws it in his face: HaShem is saying He does not want the misvah now. The Rabbi noted that the "bad sign" fits the climate of Israel and Babylonia, where this is not the season for rain, not Europe. But the halachic point stands: you get no extra reward for pushing yourself.

The Biur Halacha qualifies this. It applies only to bodily discomfort. Carrying your drink from the kitchen into the sukkah is just a little extra effort, and there the more you sit in the sukkah the more praiseworthy it is. Some Hasidic poskim held that it is praiseworthy to stay in the rain, but the plain halacha is as stated.

And when you do go in because of rain, do not cheer. Go in humbly, recognising a lost opportunity: our misvah of sukkah was not accepted.

Two views of the misvah

Seif 8 states that the custom is to say Leshev BaSukkah only when eating. Both the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema record it. The Rabbi traced it to Rabbenu Tam, the great French Rishon of about 900 years ago, who held that the main misvah of the sukkah is eating; the berakhah on the meal covers everything else. The Rambam and the Sefaradi Rishonim held the misvah is dwelling, with eating only one part of it, so one says the berakhah each time one enters the sukkah, even without eating. "When we understand this difference, so many things fall in place."

He then showed how this one difference explains a series of disputes.

The first night. Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh require eating a kazayit in the sukkah even in the rain, but no one says you must then sleep or stay there. For them that is possible: eating is the main misvah. For the Rambam it is impossible, since eating is only one expression of dwelling.

The order of berakhot. When asked, someone in the class worked it out: if dwelling is primary, Leshev comes first; if eating, Hamosi first. The Shulhan Arukh says Leshev first, the wider berakhah. The Rema, following the Rosh, says Hamosi first.

Kiddush standing. Normally we sit when one person says a berakhah for others, as on Shabbat morning (and, according to the Shulhan Arukh, at Havdalah). On Sukkot our poskim say to stand for Kiddush, say Leshev, and then sit, so that the berakhah comes before the dwelling. Strictly, the Rambam would say the berakhah before entering the sukkah, like every misvah. For the Ashkenazim it makes no difference, since the misvah only begins with the eating.

A kazayit on Shabbat. Normally up to a kebeisah of bread may be eaten outside the sukkah. With maaser, though, the Mishnah teaches that Shabbat itself makes even a small meal "fixed". Rabbenu Avigdor of Vienna in the thirteenth century applied this to the sukkah. The Hida rejects it. If the misvah is eating, Shabbat fixes the eating; if it is dwelling, a fixed meal is still not a fixed dwelling. That is why we hold one may eat a kazayit outside the sukkah even on Shabbat.

The wording of the berakhah. Some berakhot say "al" (al akhilat masah, al hatevilah) and some say "le" (lehitatef basisit, lehaniah tefillin). Tosafot concede there is no fixed rule, but suggest that "al" is for a single act and "le" for something ongoing. Leshev BaSukkah, "to dwell", fits an ongoing misvah, as the Rambam understands it.

The Rabbi offered a thought, only as a possibility: perhaps climate shaped the two views. In France, living in a sukkah for seven days and nights in October was impossible, so the misvah was read as eating. In Spain, the sukkah could simply be one's home.

When to say the berakhah without eating

The custom has spread to almost all Sefaradim, apart from groups such as the Yemenites. But the Mishnah Berurah says that if you will not eat at all, for example if you are fasting, even Rabbenu Tam would agree you say the berakhah on sitting in the sukkah. So too if you said it at breakfast, went out, and come back to spend two hours there before going out again for Minhah; the evening meal's berakhah cannot cover that separate stay.

Some Sefaradi poskim disagreed, but in the Rabbi's opinion a person who will not be eating bread should say Leshev BaSukkah. This is relevant today, when many people avoid bread through the week. "I'm dwelling in the sukkah. It's perfect weather." The Shulhan Arukh took this custom from an Ashkenazi source, and it became more entrenched among Sefaradim, who are very strict about berakhot.

The start of Siman 640

The class ended with 640:1. Women, servants and children are exempt, as sukkah is a positive misvah bound to time. There is still a misvah for women to sit there. Ashkenazi women say the berakhah; the Sefaradi practice is not to, since HaShem did not command them. The same applies to the lulav. There were Sefaradi communities, in Morocco and Iraq, where women did say these berakhot, and a woman who knows that custom may continue it. The class would resume after Rosh Hashanah.

Practical summary

  • If rain sends you in during a meal, finish the meal indoors; if you had not started, it depends on how much bother returning would be.
  • If rain sends you in to sleep, you may stay until dawn or until you wake.
  • Do not force yourself to sit in the sukkah in real discomfort; go in humbly, as a lost opportunity.
  • Say Leshev BaSukkah before Hamosi, and stand for Kiddush on Sukkot until after Leshev BaSukkah.
  • A kazayit of bread may be eaten outside the sukkah even on Shabbat.
  • In the Rabbi's view, someone who will not eat bread in the sukkah, or who spends a separate stretch of time there, should say Leshev BaSukkah.
