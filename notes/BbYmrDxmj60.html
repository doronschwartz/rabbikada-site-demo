From God to revelation

The first five principles, now complete, were about God Himself. Principles 6 to 9 are about revelation: 6 and 7 about prophecy, 8 and 9 about the Torah. Then come 10 to 13, reward and punishment.

"There is a big misconception when it comes to prophecy," the Rabbi began. People think it means God sending messages through people. "Prophecy has got nothing to do with God sending people on a mission. That's not the definition."

The Rambam writes that among human beings there are people of great intellectual perfection whose souls are ready to receive, whose intellect joins with the Active Intellect, and on whom an exalted emanation is shed: "These are the prophets, and this is prophecy." The principle is simply that prophecy exists. It is not that every word of the prophets is true; that belongs to principle 8. So the Ani Maamin's "all the words of the prophets are true" misreads the principle, while the Yigdal is precise: "The rich gift of His prophecy He gave to the men of His treasured people."

Why does it matter? Because a God who created the world and left it is not enough. The Ramban, at the end of this week's parashah, Bo, says this was Pharaoh's mistake: he believed in a deity, but one disconnected from the world. Prophecy means God is open to us and communicates His will.

1. Dreams, Ruah HaKodesh and prophecy

All three are communications from the spiritual world to the material one.

Dreams. Sleep is one sixtieth of death. During sleep the neshamah, our consciousness, leaves the body, which is why we wash our hands on waking, as a void brings impurity. The freed soul can interact with spiritual agents, not always good ones, and a dream is "a fuzzy transmission" of that encounter. So we never fully trust a dream, and a bad dream can be annulled. A dream about something you thought about during the day can be dismissed. Someone asked about prayers said to have been dictated in a dream by someone who had died. That, the Rabbi said, is a dream: the lowest level.

Ruah HaKodesh, divine inspiration or deep intuition, comes while awake. The Ramhal describes it as an understanding that "falls into a person's heart", often without his knowing where it came from. The Rabbi distinguished it from a "gut feeling", which is often wrong. It is the moment the last piece of a jigsaw falls in and the whole picture is clear. The 72 sages who all translated the Torah the same way had Ruah HaKodesh, not prophecy.

Prophecy has two marks: cleaving to God and clarity. The Rambam: "his soul joins the level of the angels and he becomes a different man." The Ramhal: he knows clearly, without doubt, that he is cleaving to his Creator. The Rabbi's analogy: hold a book with your eyes closed and you can feel its corners and count its pages; then open your eyes. Or walk into a room full of people with your eyes shut, and then open them. The Rambam, who prized philosophy so highly, said comparing it to prophecy is like comparing a jug to all the water in the world. "If you've had prophecy in your life, you would know about it."

Someone asked why Shemuel did not recognise God's call until the third time. Prophecy, the Rabbi answered, involves training. There were "sons of the prophets", schools where people practised to reach it.

2. Who can become a prophet?

The Rambam (Moreh Nevuchim II:32) brings three views: 1. God chooses whom He wishes, wise or ignorant. The Rambam calls it the view of the masses, though the Kuzari and Rabbi Yosef Albo held it. 2. Aristotle: prophecy is human perfection, and a perfected person will necessarily become a prophet. 3. The Rambam: like Aristotle, only a perfected person can prophesy, but God may still withhold it. "The gift isn't who God gives the prophecy to. The gift is who He doesn't give it to."

3. What does a prophet need?

The Mishneh Torah (Yesodei HaTorah 7:1) requires "a very wise sage of strong character, never overcome by his natural inclinations, with a very broad and accurate mind", who constantly directs his thoughts upward, understanding all of creation "from the highest spiritual form to the navel of the earth". The Moreh Nevuchim (II:36) speaks of perfection in the theoretical sciences, refined moral qualities and a perfect imaginative faculty.

Together these give three requirements: - Intellectual perfection, including the sciences. "Whoever would argue that the Rambam didn't believe in learning science is absolutely blind." - Moral perfection: refining anger, pride and every bad trait to the utmost. - Imaginative perfection: seeing a boat and a bird and imagining a flying ship.

The Talmud (Megillah 14a) says Israel had twice as many prophets as those who left Egypt. The Rabbi admitted this is hard to take literally on any view. It also shows that prophecy did not mean carrying messages: there could not have been so many messages. Many, like the sons of the prophets, were training, not necessarily attaining.

Must a prophet be Jewish? For the Rambam, no: it is human perfection. For the Kuzari, it is a gift reserved for Jews, and given only in the Land of Israel. Bilam, all agree, was an exception. Why aren't prophets always prophesying? Because people fluctuate; one can reach the level for a moment and come down.

At Sinai, for the Rambam, the people heard the first two commandments, the existence and unity of God, through philosophical illumination, since these can be reached by reason. The other eight needed revelation, through Moshe.

4. The prophetic experience, and false prophets

The Ramhal describes it: the prophet is overwhelmed, his limbs tremble, he feels turned inside out, and his senses cease. You would know.

False prophets either faked the experience, or reached a real spiritual "high" from the wrong, impure sources, as a kind of shortcut. They knew what they were doing.

5. Is there prophecy today?

The Talmud says that with the death of Haggai, Zekharyah and Malakhi, prophecy departed from Israel. For the Kuzari that is simple: the gift came only in times of closeness. But for the Rambam, if it is human development, why not today? The Rambam's answer: we can still attain intellectual and moral perfection, but the imaginative faculty needs joy. Yaakov lost prophecy while grieving for Yosef. In exile, after the destruction, our souls are constrained.

The Rabbi suggested the Talmud means prophecy became extremely hard, not impossible. "Who knows who's a prophet out there, and who knows if maybe one day we'll achieve that."

Is Mashiah a prophet? That is not one of his requirements, and the Rambam says: when he comes, we will see.

Summary

  • The sixth principle is that prophecy exists, God's open relationship with humanity, not that every prophetic text is true.
  • Dreams are fuzzy and unreliable, Ruah HaKodesh is a deep, waking intuition, and prophecy is a clear cleaving to God.
  • For the Rambam, prophecy needs intellectual, moral and imaginative perfection, and even then God may withhold it.
  • Prophecy does not mean carrying a message; most prophets' experiences were never recorded.
  • Prophecy departed with the last prophets, but for the Rambam the barrier is the sadness of exile, not an absolute bar.
