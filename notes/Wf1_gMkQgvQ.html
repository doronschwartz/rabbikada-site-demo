Topic: Exchanging rings under the huppah: what kiddushin requires, Rav Moshe Feinstein's rulings, Reform weddings, and practical options for a bride Sources: Kiddushin 5b; Rav Moshe Feinstein, Iggerot Moshe, Even HaEzer 3:18; Rav Yoseph Eliyahu Henkin Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Raleigh Close, Wednesday 5 March 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The question

The Rabbi called the topic controversial, not because the halacha is unclear, but because more and more couples are asking about it. Can a bride give her groom a ring under the huppah after he gives her one? If not, can she do it afterwards? And if it did happen, what is the result?

He did not go into whether egalitarianism is a right value from the Torah's point of view, except to say in one sentence: "Difference is not discrimination." But he took the question seriously. He once had a couple who said that if the bride could not take part, they would marry under another organisation. "If people feel that they cannot be accommodated in an orthodox framework... then we lose generations of Jews."

What makes kiddushin

A Jewish wedding has two stages: erusin (or kiddushin), betrothal, and nisuin, marriage. In the past they could be a year apart. Erusin is not an engagement. Calling off an engagement has no halachic effect, but after erusin the woman needs a full get to marry anyone else.

Kiddushin is made, as everyone agrees, by a man giving a woman something worth at least a perutah, with her consent, for the purpose of betrothal, in the presence of two kosher witnesses. A ten-pound note would work; the custom for the last seven or eight hundred years has been a ring.

There must also be some sign that the gift is for marriage, which is why the groom says "Harei at mekudeshet li", you are betrothed to me with this ring according to the law of Moshe and Israel. If the setting already makes it clear, as when the couple were discussing their wedding, it works even without words. The bride says nothing. Holding out her finger is her consent. So in most orthodox weddings, the Rabbi observed, that is the bride's only active part, and some find this hard.

"I am betrothed to you" does not work

The Gemara in Kiddushin discusses a man who reverses the formula and says, "I am betrothed to you." It has no effect at all, because the verse says "when a man takes a woman": he must bring her into his domain, not bring himself into hers. The man must initiate.

The Rabbi digressed briefly to explain the idea, which he said comes from Rabbi Tatz. The man begins with the spark; the woman takes it and makes it real. A child begins with a father, but needs nine months of a mother to become a person. Men carry the greater obligation to study Torah, yet the verse says "do not forsake the Torah of your mother": the mother turns abstract learning into a home of Torah. He found the same pattern in the names of God. The names that start with aleph or are pronounced with an aleph sound are masculine names of a vision being set. The names that begin with a letter from the end of the alphabet, Shekhinah, Shabbat and Shalom, are feminine: the vision fulfilled, God dwelling among us.

Rav Moshe Feinstein: if it happened, and should it happen

As far as the Rabbi knew, the first to discuss the exchange of rings explicitly was Rav Moshe Feinstein in the early 1970s.

If it happened. The moment the groom places the ring and says the words, the kiddushin takes effect. If the bride then gives him a ring and says "you are betrothed to me", it is "empty words". It does not undo what has happened, even if the rabbi and witnesses knew in advance.

Should it happen? No, for two reasons:

  1. Hukkot HaGoy. The Torah forbids following the practices of the nations. The Rabbi explained that most Rishonim and poskim, following the Maharik, limit this to religious practices, which this is not. Others include any non-Jewish practice without a rational reason, and Rav Moshe takes this stricter view into account.
  2. Forgetting what kiddushin is. In fifty or a hundred years people would think a Jewish wedding needs both rings and both formulas, and that without the bride's part there is no marriage.

Someone asked whether it also adds to a misvah (bal tosif). The Rabbi said no: that applies to adding within the misvah itself, like a fifth species on Sukkot. Here the misvah is complete before she gives anything.

Reform weddings: is a get needed?

The second part of the class asked something that comes up often. A couple married in a Reform synagogue, lived together for years, and divorced civilly. Does she need a get to remarry? Rav Moshe says not, for three reasons:

  1. No kosher witnesses.
  2. An exchange of gifts. Even if two observant witnesses happened to be present, a ceremony in which he must give her a ring and she must give him one is, he says, not kiddushin but an exchange of gifts.
  3. Living together. Rav Henkin held that a couple known to live together as husband and wife may be considered married through relations, since everyone witnesses it. Rav Moshe disagrees, and adds that even Rav Henkin would say this only where there was no religious ceremony at all. A couple who had a ceremony in a temple put their religion there, and nothing suggests their later life together was intended as kiddushin.

How can that fit with the first ruling, where the bride's ring did not cancel the kiddushin? Rav Moshe answers that the setting makes all the difference. In an orthodox wedding, the intention is clearly kiddushin under halacha. In a Reform ceremony, where the double exchange is part of the procedure, it is read as an exchange of gifts.

In practice, he said, the policy of batei din is to try to obtain a get, but if that is impossible, the woman may remarry relying on Rav Moshe. A get given by a Reform body is not a get at all. He drew a lesson for people choosing where to marry: even someone who is not orthodox in practice may be "orthodox in ideology", and it can make a great difference to the marriage and to future generations to marry within an orthodox framework.

Practical options

What can a bride who wants to take part be told?

  • In the yihud room. The easiest and most halachically acceptable option is to give her husband a ring in private, after the ceremony, with whatever words she likes. Neither of Rav Moshe's concerns applies. A man wearing a ring is also fine.
  • The kinyan on the ketubah. In Israel the ketubah is often read between kiddushin and nisuin, and the groom makes a kinyan with an object belonging to someone else to accept its terms. It need not be the rabbi's pen; the bride could hand him the object. The Rabbi stressed this is more controversial, needs greater authorities than him, and he was not saying it is correct.

Questions from the class

Asked about a wedding with only one rabbi, he said there must be a second kosher witness, and he often brings someone with him when no one else present is observant. Asked about a Kohen marrying a divorcee, he said it is forbidden and no orthodox rabbi will officiate, but if it happened before two kosher witnesses the marriage takes effect and would need a get. "You don't need a rabbi for a wedding... you need two kosher witnesses."

Practical summary

  • Kiddushin is made by the groom giving the ring with the formula, before two kosher witnesses, with the bride's consent; "I am betrothed to you" from either side has no effect.
  • If the bride gives a ring back under the huppah, the marriage is valid, but Rav Moshe Feinstein rules it should not be done.
  • After a Reform wedding, Rav Moshe holds that no get is needed; batei din still try to obtain one where possible.
  • A bride may give her husband a ring in the yihud room.
  • Any role in the kinyan on the ketubah is more controversial and needs a greater authority's approval.
