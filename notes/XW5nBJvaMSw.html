Introduction: The Words of the Giving

Siman 136 is "quite contained", and the class went through it in one shiur. It deals with what the husband must say when he gives the get, two couples in one town with the same names, and giving a get on Shabbat. Much of the shiur was a discussion, with students presenting the sugyot and the Rabbi pressing and adding.

המגרש צריך שיאמר כשיתן לה הגט הרי זה גיטך וכיוצא בזה, ואם נתן בידה ולא אמר לה כלום הרי זה גט פסול The one who divorces must say, when he gives her the get, "This is your get", or the like. If he put it in her hand and said nothing, it is a get pasul.

The Rema gives the full wording lekhathilah: harei zeh gittekh, harei at megureshet mimeni, harei at muteret lekhol adam. The husband folds the get like a letter and gives it to her, so that she can hold all of it in her hand. And the husband must know what he is saying: that is why it is translated and explained to him clearly.

Part 1: Why Must He Speak? (Seif 1)

Three views

Opinion

Why the husband must speak

Tosafot

So that she knows she is being divorced. It is for her: a matter of her daat (awareness)

The Rambam

The giving must be betorat gerushin, in a setting that defines it as a divorce

The Baal HaMaor

The speech is part of the divorce itself: a get is done in writing and orally together, so he has to say it

These views come from the sugyot in the Gemara. In one, if he told the edim beforehand that this is a get and then gave it to her saying nothing, it works. And even if he said to her "this is a shtar hov" (a promissory note), it works: you might think calling it a shtar hov cancels it, but no, because he told the edim beforehand.

The nafka minot

1. He told the edim it is a get, and told her it is a shtar hov. - The Rambam: absolutely fine. He gave it as a get before the edim. - Tosafot: not good enough. She does not know she is divorced. Of course a woman can be divorced against her will, but she has to know that she is being divorced. If she thinks it is a shtar hov, it does not work, unless he tells her afterwards that it was a get. - The Baal HaMaor: a student first suggested it would not help, but the Rabbi said it would: there was an amirah, to the edim. "It's not a din in her knowing anything. It's a din in me orally saying what the get is about."

2. He gave it and said nothing at all. - Tosafot: definitely no good, since she does not know she is divorced: batel. - The Baal HaMaor: also batel, since the speech is missing. - The Rambam: only pasul derabbanan. Why only rabbinic? As the Beit Yoseph explains, when a man gives his wife a get, the plain assumption is that he gives it as a divorce. So midoraita the requirement of sefer keritut is fulfilled even without words; Hazal required him to say it out loud.

3. He told her it is a shtar hov, and told no one beforehand. - The Rambam: batel midoraita. His words "this is a shtar hov" cancel the plain assumption that it was given as a divorce, and there is no way to fix it. - Tosafot: it can be saved by telling her afterwards that it was a get. - The Baal HaMaor: that does not help.

How does the Shulhan Arukh rule?

The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam: if he gave it and said nothing, it is pasul.

The Rema, in Seif 5, adds that she must know she was divorced, or the edim must tell her afterwards. The Rabbi pointed out that this is really Tosafot's view. The Rema's wording reads as if it were just an addition to the Shulhan Arukh, when it is a different shitah; you might have expected "yesh omrim". The Beit Shmuel explains that the Rema requires both: the Rambam's and Tosafot's.

A sleeping wife

The same Gemara says that a get given to a woman while she is asleep is not a get. That is easy for Tosafot: she has no daat. The Baal HaMaor gives a new reason: the get must be in her keeping (shemirah), and a sleeping woman cannot guard it. For the Rambam it is harder: if the point is to give it as a divorce, why should her being asleep matter? One must say that someone without consciousness cannot be "cut off". "It's a bit difficult."

Part 2: When They Were Already Discussing the Get (Seif 2)

במה דברים אמורים, בשלא היה מדבר עמה על עסקי גיטה, אבל אם היה מדבר עמה על עסקי גיטה ונטל הגט ונתן בידה ולא אמר כלום, הרי זה גט

If they were talking about her get, and he took it and gave it to her saying nothing, it works.

This parallels the laws of kiddushin, where asukin beoto inyan (being engaged in the matter) also works without words. Then there is a further question: what if they were talking about a related matter? Not "I am divorcing you", but something like what would happen to the children if they separated, or living their lives apart. That is another mahloket in Kiddushin.

  • The Rif and the Rambam rule that a related subject does not work, and so does the Shulhan Arukh. He is consistent: in Kiddushin she is not mekudeshet, and here only talking about her get itself works.
  • The Rema in Kiddushin rules stringently, like the Rashba, that she is safek mekudeshet. Here he says nothing, which is unusual. You would expect him to say safek megureshet here too.

Part 3: Words That Help and Words That Spoil (Seifim 3-4)

Seif 3: "I am not your husband"

The proper words are "you are divorced from me with this get, and permitted to any man", or "you are sent away from me". A shaliah says "you are divorced from so-and-so".

But if he said "I am not your husband", "I am not your baal":

  • Some say it is not a get, even if they were discussing her get. The Torah says veshilhah, he must send her away, not send himself away.
  • Others say that if they were discussing her get, she is a safek megureshet.

"This is important to note": even for the Rambam, for whom saying nothing makes the get only pasul, here it is no get at all. His words have made it worse, because they contradict the divorce.

The Rosh and Rabbenu Yeruham

What if they were discussing the get and he used these words?

  • The Rosh: not a get.
  • Rabbenu Yeruham brings three views: she is divorced (since they were discussing it); she is a safek megureshet; or, like the Rosh, she is not divorced at all, since his words take away from the discussion.

The Beit Shmuel's question

Here the Shulhan Arukh brings two views. But in Hilchot Kiddushin, in the parallel case, he rules straight out like the Rosh: it is nothing. Why the difference?

The Beit Shmuel explains: here there is a get. As he says in several places, the words of the get "speak" for themselves, so they can cover his poor wording. That is why the Shulhan Arukh mentions the safek view here. But the Beit Shmuel himself rules like the Rosh: it is not a get.

Seif 4: "You are your own"

אמר לה הרי את לעצמך, הרי זה גט, אבל אם אמר לה הרי את בת חורין, לא אמר כלום

"You are your own" works; "you are a free woman" (bat horin) says nothing. Why?

Because a woman always remains a bat horin, a free person, even after marriage. "It's important to understand this." People speak of kiddushin as if the husband acquires her. That is the way the Torah said to do it, "but it's not that you're taking her, and she now belongs to you, and she's no longer free". As the Rambam explains it, what he acquires is her exclusivity. So telling her she is free does not end the marriage.

Seifim 1-5 together

These five seifim go together, with three main mahlokot:

  1. Tosafot, the Rambam and the Baal HaMaor on why he must speak. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam; the Rema adds Tosafot.
  2. Bad wording while discussing the get: the Shulhan Arukh brings two views here, though not in Kiddushin.
  3. A related subject (meinyan leinyan): the Shulhan Arukh is consistent that it is nothing, while the Rema, stringent in Kiddushin, is silent here.

Part 4: Two Couples With the Same Names (Seif 6)

אם יש אחר בעיר ששמו כשמו ושם אשתו כשם אשתו, אינו יכול לגרש אלא בפני האחר

"It's not so relevant nowadays, but let's see it." If another man in the town has the same name as the husband, and his wife the same name as the wife, he may divorce only in the presence of the other. The source is a Gemara in Bava Batra.

Who must be present?

  • The Rambam: just the other man.
  • A view brought in the Beit Yoseph: the other couple.

The concern is that the get could be used to divorce the wrong man's wife, so you would think the other husband is enough. The Shulhan Arukh's singular "bifnei haaher" suggests he rules like the Rambam.

The Rema adds: even if the get comes out from under one woman's hand, she is not divorced until she brings proof that he divorced her in the other's presence, or edei mesirah who testify that this man divorced his wife.

Is bringing the other man enough?

The underlying question: is the Gemara's solution enough according to Rabbi Elazar, or does the get need to prove itself from within (mukhah mitokho)?

Opinion

View

Tosafot (as presented in the class)

Divorcing in the other's presence is not enough. A sign must be written in the get itself

The Ramban and others

This is not a flaw in the get. It is an external issue: there happens to be another man with the same name, so bring him along. The sugya holds for everyone

The Shulhan Arukh

Simply bring the other man. The Beit Shmuel explains that he did not bring the extra requirement, since according to the Ramban it is not needed

The Rema: two disagreements

The Rema differs from the Shulhan Arukh on two points:

  1. Even if only the husbands' names are the same, and the wives' names are different, something must be done.
  2. A sign must be written in the get itself: a kinui of the husband, or that one is a Kohen and the other not.

"And therefore the practice is to give the get in public (barabbim)", in case there are two men in the town with the same name.

With a sign in the get, is the other man still needed?

  • Some hold the Gemara's requirement is a comprehensive takanah: even with signs in the get, the giving must always be in the other's presence.
  • The Beit Shmuel holds that once there is a sign, that is enough. But then he asks himself: why did the practice of giving in public arise? He leaves it as a question.
  • The Noda BiYehudah agrees with the Beit Shmuel and answers his question: the public giving is not for the known other man. It is an extra stringency in case there is another man we do not know about who shares the name, and even the same sign.

The conclusion is a neat compromise:

Case

What is needed

Both husbands' and wives' names identical

A sign in the get, and giving in the other's presence

Only the husbands' names identical

A sign in the get is enough (the Rema's stringency anyway)

Only the wives' names identical

No concern

Two more points from the class

  • Only the wives share a name: why is there no concern? The class suggested that it is the husband who does the divorcing. The Rabbi pushed back: perhaps the other wife is behind a scheme. In the end, we are more concerned about men getting up to such schemes than women.
  • The other man is a Kohen: if two husbands share a name, one a Kohen, and the Yisrael's get has no sign, does it help that the Kohen's get would have to say "Kohen"? The class saw this as implied by the Rema. "That's a hiddush": your get is clarified by what the other person would have to write in his.

Nowadays

Today this seif is practically obsolete. Beit Din certificates include identifying details such as passport numbers, and we do not know people by their fathers' names, so it is very hard to know positively of another couple with the same names. "But if you were to positively know this, then you'd have to go through" with it. In Israel, a photo is put on the certificate as an extra safeguard.

Part 5: A Get on Shabbat (Seif 7)

אין מגרשין בשבת, ואם השעה צריכה לכך, כגון שכיב מרע שתקף עליו החולי ורוצה לגרשה כדי שלא תזקק ליבם, אם הגט ברשותו יקנה לה אותו ותזכה בו ובגט שבתוכו

A get is not given on Shabbat; this is rabbinic, lest he come to write. But where it is urgent, for example a dying man who wants to divorce his wife so that she will not need yibbum: if the get is in his property, he transfers that place to her, and she acquires it with the get inside it. If that is impossible without moving the get, he may take it in his hand and give it to her.

What is she acquiring?

  • Rashi: she simply needs to acquire the get. Venatan beyadah does not require her property.
  • The Ran: by acquiring the place, it becomes her haser (her property), so it is like giving it into her property. The get must be in her domain.

The Beit Shmuel rules like the Ran.

Why a kinyan and not just moving the get?

A kinyan on Shabbat is itself rabbinic. Why allow the kinyan and not simply allow giving the get?

  • The Rashbam: the case in the Gemara involved carrying through a public domain.
  • The Rosh asks: if so, the husband could appoint a shaliah to go with her. So he prefers Rashi: the problem is that the get is mukseh.

But mukseh too is rabbinic, so why prefer the kinyan? The Rosh gives two answers:

  1. Hazal already permitted a kinyan on Shabbat in other cases, so this is an extension of an existing heter, not a new one.
  2. A kinyan is less noticeable: you are just closing the doors. Handling a mukseh document is more visible.

The Rema: a get is no longer mukseh

The Rema brings the Mordekhai and the Semag: nowadays, since we write down the Oral Torah, a get may be moved on Shabbat, because one can learn halachot from it. So it can be given directly in any case.

The Beit Yoseph asks the simple question: the story in the Gemara took place after Rebbi had already written the Mishnah. The Rabbi answered: it did not happen at once. When Rebbi wrote the Mishnah, texts did not automatically stop being mukseh. It was an evolution: we began reading and learning from written texts, and a get became "another kind of text, a kind of book". "It's connected to the reality."

Because of his question, the Shulhan Arukh rules that you do the kinyan. According to the Rema, it is better to give the get directly.

Would this ever apply today?

If a man had a get written on Friday, did not manage to give it, and on Shabbat it looked as if he would die, with yibbum involved, then according to the Rema they would bring the woman to him and he would give it to her directly.

A student asked about a party who had been refusing, and on Shabbat suddenly says they will give or receive the get. You do not want to miss the chance. The Rabbi questioned whether that fits this seif. Hazal made many heterim because of yibbum, and their concern about yibbum is itself because of the risk of her becoming an agunah, so the student's point has some strength. But the Rabbi left it open.

Is it not pre-dated?

A final question: if the get was written and signed before Shabbat and given on Shabbat, why is it not a get mukdam (a pre-dated get)? The Beit Shmuel discusses several answers. The one the Rabbi liked: as learned at the end of Siman 127, a get dated by the week, rather than the exact day, can work.

Summary of the Halachot

  1. The husband must say "harei zeh gittekh" (Seif 1), and must understand what he says. Lekhathilah he adds "you are divorced from me" and "you are permitted to any man".
  2. Why: for her knowledge (Tosafot), for the setting of a divorce (Rambam), or because speech is part of the divorce (Baal HaMaor). Given in silence: pasul derabbanan for the Rambam, batel for the others. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam, and the Rema adds that she must know (Tosafot).
  3. If they were discussing her get (Seif 2), no words are needed. A merely related subject is not enough (Shulhan Arukh); the Rema is stringent in Kiddushin but silent here.
  4. "I am not your husband" (Seif 3) is not a get, or at most a doubt; the Beit Shmuel rules like the Rosh that it is not. "You are your own" works; "you are free" does not (Seif 4), because a married woman always remains free.
  5. Two couples with the same names (Seif 6): divorce in the other man's presence (Shulhan Arukh). The Rema requires a sign in the get even when only the husbands' names match, hence the practice of giving the get in public. Today certificates and identity details make this almost obsolete.
  6. No get on Shabbat (Seif 7), except for a dying man to spare his wife yibbum: he transfers the place to her with the get in it. The Rema says that nowadays the get is not mukseh and may simply be given.
