These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why we read Ruth on Shavuot

There are many reasons for reading Megillat Ruth on Shavuot, but the main one, the Rabbi said, is that on Shavuot we became a nation by accepting the Torah, and Ruth did the same when she entered the covenant of HaShem and His people. Reading her story, we recommit ourselves to that covenant.

The puzzle of the book

Elimelekh and his sons Mahlon and Kilyon die in Moav. Naomi sets out for home and three times tells her daughters-in-law to go back: "Shovnah, my daughters." Orpah turns back; Ruth clings to her. The Midrash learns from these three times that we push away a would-be convert three times, and only if he persists do we accept him. That reading assumes that Ruth and Orpah were not yet Jewish.

But at the end of the book, Boaz arranges halisah before he marries Ruth. Yibum and halisah apply only after a valid marriage between two Jews. If Mahlon married a non-Jewish woman, there was no marriage, and no halisah would be needed. And converting later would not help, because at the time of the marriage she was not Jewish. Chapter 1 teaches us the laws of converts; chapter 4 teaches us the laws of halisah. Seemingly, both cannot be true.

The process in the Gemara and the Rambam

The main source is Yevamot 47a. When someone comes to convert, we ask: why? Do you not know that Israel is afflicted and persecuted? If he says, "I know, and I am not worthy," we accept him at once. We tell him some lenient misvot, such as sukkah, and some severe ones, such as Shabbat, the laws of charity, and the punishments, and also the reward in the World to Come. We do not overwhelm him: "push away with the left hand and bring near with the right."

Why "at once"? Because one does not delay a misvah. There is a misvah in helping a sincere person convert. One Rishon derives it from the command to love the ger; the Rambam from "you shall love HaShem your God," which means behaving in a way that makes HaShem beloved to others.

Why push away at all? Tosafot gives several reasons. The Rabbi singled out one from a Baal HaTosafot called Rabbi Avraham HaGer, himself a convert: converts are more careful in misvot than born Jews, and that becomes an accusation against the rest of us.

Then he showed something striking. The Gemara speaks only of teaching misvot and reward and punishment. The Rambam adds his own words: we teach him the fundamentals of the faith, the unity of God and the prohibition of idolatry, and we spend a long time on this; we teach him some misvot, and do not elaborate. For the Rambam, the heart of joining the faith is understanding it, and the Shulhan Arukh quotes him word for word.

Motives, and the answer to the puzzle

The Gemara says converts were not accepted in the days of David and Shlomo, nor will they be in the days of Mashiah, because then people may join for power or gain. Hence the principle, codified from the Rambam in the Shulhan Arukh: the Beit Din checks whether the convert seeks a position, money or a Jewish partner. Conversion must be for the sake of Heaven.

Yet Hillel accepted the man who wanted to become Kohen Gadol, and the Gemara in Menahot tells of a woman who converted in order to marry. Tosafot answers that Hillel saw that the motive, though warped, came from a real recognition of HaShem that would grow. The Beit Yosef concludes that all depends on the judgement of the Beit Din. Conversion is unusual in this: rather than looking up a ruling, the dayanim must enter the person's mind. Someone in a relationship who truly wants to discover Torah is not automatically disqualified, but one who says "if the marriage ends, I drop it all" is converting for marriage alone. And after the fact, says the Shulhan Arukh, even a conversion for the wrong motive is valid.

This, the commentators explain, solves the book of Ruth. Both women converted when they married. Naomi held the view that a conversion for the wrong motive is not a conversion, and she was never sure of their motives. When Orpah turned back, it showed her conversion had been only for marriage, so it never took effect. When Ruth stayed even after her husband's death, it proved hers was sincere from the start. She had always been Jewish, and so halisah was needed.

Milah, mikveh and kabbalat misvot

Conversion requires milah for a man, immersion in a mikveh, and acceptance of the misvot. One already circumcised needs only hatafat dam berit. Rav Kook was asked about a man whose doctors said circumcision would endanger his life. The principle, the Rabbi said, applies to all of life: when you cannot do something, no one blames you, but you cannot claim the benefits as if you had done it.

Kabbalat misvot does not mean knowing all 613. The Gemara tells of a convert who was never taught Shabbat. It means a willingness to accept everything one has been taught and will learn. What about a woman who says she will accept everything except covering her hair? Rav Moshe Feinstein suggested that she may not realise it is required, seeing so many who do not keep it. And Rav Hayim Ozer Grodzinski in the Ahiezer draws the vital distinction: rejecting a misvah on principle invalidates the acceptance, but knowing what is right and not yet being there does not. "Every one of us here has something which we know we're not doing correctly." On the same basis, he said, a conversion that rejects misvot on principle is not valid.

Can a conversion be undone? A woman who later left told Rav Moshe Feinstein she never meant to accept the misvot. He rejected her claim: words in the heart are not words. Only clear evidence from the time of the conversion itself, an umdena, can overturn it, as in a case the Rabbi heard about in Israel, where a woman had already booked a flight for the coming Shabbat when she went to the mikveh. Such cases, he stressed, are rare.

Finally, a non-Jew may not keep Shabbat fully. For someone in the process, the general advice, he said, is to make a small break at the very end of Shabbat, in the doubtful time after sunset.

Questions from the class

  • Conversion during pregnancy: status follows conception. If the mother conceived before converting, the child needs conversion; the mother's immersion counts for the fetus, and a boy then needs milah. Such a daughter may not marry a Kohen.
  • Converting a woman one has made pregnant: in theory possible, he said, but it depends entirely on whether the motives are sincere.

Summary

  • Ruth was read on Shavuot because she, like Israel, entered HaShem's covenant.
  • The Rambam puts the fundamentals of faith at the centre of preparing a convert.
  • Sincere motive is essential, but the Beit Din judges the whole person, and after the fact a conversion stands.
  • Naomi tested her daughters-in-law to discover whether their conversions had been real; Ruth's was.
  • Kabbalat misvot means accepting in principle, not yet keeping everything.
