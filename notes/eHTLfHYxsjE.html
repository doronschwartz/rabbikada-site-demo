These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seif 1: two whole loaves (Lehem Mishneh)

After Kiddush comes HaMosi. The Shulhan Arukh: break bread on two whole loaves, hold both while saying the berakhah, and cut the lower one.

Why two? To remember the double portion of manna that fell before Shabbat. We do the same on Yom Tov, because collecting the manna on Yom Tov would also have been work and bother, so a double portion fell before it too. Women are equally obligated.

Every meal. Every bread meal on Shabbat needs two loaves, even a fourth meal on a long summer evening. At Seudah Shelishit, where people often have one roll each, they are relying on the one making HaMosi.

What is "whole"? The Mishnah Berurah, based on the laws of hallah, says that if up to one forty-eighth is missing (about 2%) it still counts as whole. Two rolls baked stuck together, with a bit torn off when you separate them, are fine. A crown picked off by a child is a problem.

Cut one or both? The Shulhan Arukh cuts only one, since the Torah speaks of a double portion being gathered. Some Rishonim, following the Rashba, and the Vilna Gaon held you must cut both; the Gaon suggested baking small loaves to make it affordable. The Kaf HaHayim also writes that one should cut both. But the Mishnah Berurah writes that most people cut one, and that is the universal custom.

Which loaf: bottom, top, or right?

Why cut the lower loaf? There is a general rule that one does not pass over a misvah (ein maavirin al hamisvot). That is why the tallit goes on top of the tefillin in your bag, and the shel yad sits on the right side. Reaching under the top loaf to cut the bottom one seems to pass over a misvah.

Practice

Ashkenazim (the Taz)

Hold one above the other, with the lower loaf drawn slightly towards you so it is reached first. Cut the lower one on Friday night. The Rema says that on Shabbat day and on Yom Tov night the upper one is cut, for a Kabbalistic reason.

Sefaradim (the Arizal)

Place the two side by side, touching. Cut the right-hand loaf at every meal, Friday night and Shabbat day alike.

The Rabbi said the Arizal's way is the custom of all the Middle Eastern Sefaradi communities. Although he normally says we follow the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh, this custom is ingrained, and he advised a Sefaradi who had learned the top-and-bottom method to change, unless it was his father's custom.

A burnt loaf. You always make the berakhah on the better item, and that comes from the Gemara, so if the right-hand one is burnt, cut the other. You may cut the smaller loaf on Friday night to keep the larger one for lunch; just place it in the right position.

Timing. Do not cut until the berakhah is finished and most of those present have answered amen, because amen is part of the berakhah. The Mishnah Berurah says careful people score the loaf before the berakhah, so they can cut straight away without a delay. Only a mark: if you cut it through, it is no longer whole.

Seifim 2-3: a big piece, and who eats first

Seif 2. On a weekday you should not cut a huge piece, more than a kazayit, because you look greedy, nor a tiny one, because you look mean. The way a person eats shows who they are. On Shabbat it is a misvah to cut yourself a big piece, enough for the whole meal, in honour of Shabbat. Everyone else gets a normal piece. At the Rabbi's home there are four hallot, and each person simply gets a roll.

Seif 3. Those at the table do not eat until the one who said HaMosi has tasted. If each person has their own two loaves, they do not depend on him and may eat. At Seudah Shelishit, where people have one roll each, they should wait for him.

Should he eat before handing out bread? The Shulhan Arukh does not discuss it. Ideally he eats first and hands out while chewing, which is the Sefaradi custom. The counter-argument is that serving guests first is good manners and part of the meal, just as feeding your animals before you eat is not an interruption. The Rabbi has seen knowledgeable people do it that way and said not to change.

Honouring a guest. You may honour an important guest with Kiddush. HaMosi is always done by the host, "however important the person is." A guest does not know how much bread there is or how big to cut, and it puts him in an awkward position. If you are offered the cup for Kiddush, Havdalah, Birkat HaMazon or Sheva Berakhot, never refuse it.

Order of serving: the host eats, then his wife. To honour an important guest, cut a piece for your wife and set it aside, give the guest the next piece, then pass your wife hers.

Seif 4: bread at the night and morning meals

The Friday night and Shabbat morning meals must include bread. The implication is that Seudah Shelishit need not, but when we reach that siman we will see the Shulhan Arukh's main opinion does require bread.

If you have no whole loaves, for example on holiday with only sliced bread from the shop, you still eat a bread meal. Lehem Mishneh is a separate misvah. Two untouched pittas count as whole loaves.

Siman 275: reading by Shabbat candlelight

The siman is numbered ayin-resh-hey rather than resh-ayin-hey, because the usual order spells "ra," bad.

Seif 1. One may not read by the light of a Shabbat candle, even silently, in case one tilts the lamp to improve the flame. The Gemara tells how Rabbi Yishmael said he would not tilt it, did so while absorbed in his learning, and regretted it all his life. The ban applies even to a lamp ten floors up, a lamp under glass, or a wax candle. The Hakhamim made no exceptions.

Seif 2. Two people reading the same subject together may read, because each will remind the other.

Seif 3. If someone else is present and you specifically ask them to watch you, you may read.

When this matters today. The timer switches off the lights in the middle of the Friday night meal. You may say Birkat HaMazon, sing, and read texts you know well. You may not study new material by candlelight, unless enough light comes in from outside, such as a street lamp, that you do not need the candles.

He told what was well known in the Mir: the late Rosh Yeshivah, Rav Finkel, used only generator electricity on Shabbat, which often ran out at night. To be able to keep learning late by candlelight, his wife would stay up beside him on Friday nights. "It shows you how Hakhamim would learn Torah, and also how their wife supported them."

Later seifim. Children may read before their teacher, since they are in awe of him. Bameh Madlikin may be studied by candlelight, since its subject is this very prohibition. When Pesah falls on Shabbat, everyone may read the Haggadah, since everyone knows its outline.

Practical summary

  • Use two whole loaves at every Shabbat bread meal; a small piece missing is fine.
  • Sefaradim: two loaves side by side, cut the right one at every meal. Ashkenazim: one above the other, lower one nearer.
  • Wait until the berakhah and amen are finished before cutting; scoring the loaf beforehand is good.
  • On Shabbat cut yourself a generous piece. The host, not the guest, says HaMosi.
  • No bread rolls at all? Still have a bread meal on any bread.
  • Do not study new material by candlelight alone; familiar texts and Birkat HaMazon are fine.
