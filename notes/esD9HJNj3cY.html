Why start with principles

The Rabbi thanked Sina and Davi, whose initiative made the series possible, and Jack, who helped with the source sheets. There are eight shiurim. The first two are about the principles behind how we reach a halachic decision on Shabbat; only the last six deal with practical halacha.

Nobody can know all the endless details of Shabbat, but one can know the klalim, the principles. Whoever understands these two shiurim properly can answer about three quarters of the questions that come up.

Melekhet mahshevet and its six conditions

The root of everything is the phrase melekhet mahshevet, from the verses about Besalel building the Mishkan. The 39 melakhot are learned from the Mishkan, and the Mishkan was skilled, designed work. So the Torah forbids only creative, professional work, as Rashi explains in Besah. Anything less may be forbidden by the Rabbis, but not by the Torah.

For a melakhah to be a Torah prohibition, six things must be present:

  1. Mitkaven: you intend the act.
  2. Kedarkah: you do it in the normal way.
  3. Serikhah legufah: you do it for the purpose it served in the Mishkan. Trapping an animal only so that it will not bite you is not the Mishkan's trapping.
  4. Metaken: it is constructive. Demolishing for no building purpose is not the melakhah.
  5. Permanent: not temporary.
  6. Mitasek: you know what you are doing. Someone who wakes on Friday night and switches on the light, not realising it is Shabbat, has not committed a Torah violation.

Without all six there is no Torah prohibition, though there may be a rabbinic one. This shiur covers only the first. The rest come next week.

Davar sheeino mitkaven

Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon disagree on an unintended result. The classic example is dragging a bench or chair across the earth: it may make a groove, which would be plowing, but you have no intention of that. Rabbi Yehudah forbids it, Rabbi Shimon permits it. Rashi and Tosafot say Rabbi Yehudah forbids it only rabbinically.

Generally the halacha follows Rabbi Yehudah against Rabbi Shimon, but in Hilchot Shabbat it follows Rabbi Shimon. The Ramban and the Ran apply this throughout Shabbat, while the Meiri limits it to the laws of mukseh. The Rambam rules like Rabbi Shimon (Hilchot Shabbat 1:6), as does the Shulhan Arukh. So you may drag the bench and walk on grass, as long as you have no intention of making a groove or uprooting. If you do have it in mind, even vaguely, it is forbidden.

Pesik reisha

Rabbi Shimon's leniency has a limit, from the Gemara (Shabbat 75): if the result is inevitable, a pesik reisha, it is forbidden even according to Rabbi Shimon. The name comes from the example of cutting off a chicken's head: "will it not die?" You cannot say you did not mean to kill it. The Rambam codifies this in the next halachah: if a definite result follows from your act, you are liable.

Deep down you must have intended a certain result, or it is simply an extension of your action. The Shulhan Arukh's example: you may walk on grass, but you may not hold a picnic on the lawn, because you will certainly be watering it, a toladah of planting.

This is why a common Friday afternoon question is hard. You arrive at a holiday home ten minutes before Shabbat and want to open the fridge, but the light comes on. If you leave the shiur now, the answer is that you cannot, because the light is an inevitable result. He promised to return to this.

Karov lepesik reisha

The Shulhan Arukh says one may not open a door behind which a candle stands, "perhaps the wind will extinguish it." The source is the Gemara (Shabbat 120), where Rav was very upset by this, and the Gemara explains that it is a case of pesik reisha. The Rambam brings the case as one where extinguishing is certain. But the Shulhan Arukh writes "perhaps", following the Rosh quoting Rashi, which does not fit the principle.

To explain this, the Biur Halakha brings the Maharsha's idea of karov lepesik reisha, "close to inevitable": something 99 percent certain counts as a pesik reisha. The Mishnah Berurah uses the same idea for stuffing a rag into a jug, where the Gemara said only "perhaps" it will squeeze.

The Rabbi's view is that the Rambam did not learn this way, and the Sefaradi poskim never took such a concept into account. The Rambam says "vaday", definite. One could dissect any act and calculate, from the weight of the table and the ground, when it will make a hole, but we look at the action as a whole (as the Rivash says of brushing hair, where a hair or two inevitably comes out). For us there is no karov lepesik reisha: if it is definite it is forbidden; if not, and you have no intention, it is permitted.

Pesik reisha delo nihah lei

What if the inevitable result is of no benefit to you? The Gemara on uprooting endives in a friend's field says you are not liable, because the pruning is a pesik reisha you have no interest in. The Aruch deduces that it is permitted. Tosafot answer that the Gemara says only "not liable", so it may still be forbidden rabbinically: Shabbat has three levels, hayav, asur and mutar. Delo nihah lei means you have no positive interest, not that the result is bad for you.

On removing the stopper from a barrel, the Shulhan Arukh brings the Aruch's lenient view as an anonymous "yesh matir" and notes that others disagree. Rav Ovadia Yoseph observes that hardly anyone else held like the Aruch; most Rishonim, Tosafot and the Rosh among them, forbid it. So the halacha is that a pesik reisha delo nihah lei is not a Torah liability, but is still forbidden rabbinically.

When the prohibition is rabbinic

The Shulhan Arukh (Siman 314) permits taking in and out a knife that was wedged in a barrel before Shabbat, as long as you do not intend to widen the hole. The Rema adds that it must have been taken out once before Shabbat. The Shulhan Arukh is lenient because a pesik reisha of a rabbinic prohibition is permitted: a hole not made both to take in and take out is only rabbinic.

But the Shulhan Arukh elsewhere seems to forbid it, for example closing a box with flies in it. The Rabbi's summary: when you can easily avoid it, as with flies, do. When there is no other way, as with getting your wine from the barrel, it is permitted.

The fridge light. Assume electricity is rabbinic. If the light goes on and off with the door and you have no interest in it, this is a pesik reisha delo nihah lei of a rabbinic prohibition. There is a double doubt (sefek sefeka): whether a rabbinic pesik reisha is permitted, and whether a pesik reisha you do not benefit from is permitted even at Torah level. Rav Ovadia Yoseph in Yabia Omer uses this principle for opening a fridge (where the temperature will change), cutting a cake with letters on it, and walking in shoes that leave imprints. So one may open and close a fridge with a light, as long as one does not need the light. If it is pitch dark and the light helps you, it is nihah lei, and there is a dispute.

Questions from the class

Intent and inevitability. Someone described a German court that convicted two street racers of murder because death was an inevitable result of their driving, and asked how the principles apply. The Rabbi answered that lack of knowledge is never an excuse: one must find out what is inevitable. But if a person honestly did not think it a pesik reisha, he is not held liable.

A light that helps but is not needed. On a stairway with a motion sensor the light certainly helps, even if not strictly necessary. The Rabbi agreed: nihah lei means you are glad of it, not that you need it. Watering a friend's garden is the opposite, since you have no interest in the garden.

Doubtful pesik reisha. What of closing a box that may contain a butterfly? You do not intend it and do not know whether it will happen. The Turei Zahav permits it, the Biur Halakha is stringent, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph treats it as a davar sheeino mitkaven, which is permitted.

Practical summary

  • A Torah prohibition on Shabbat needs six conditions; the first is that you intend the act.
  • A davar sheeino mitkaven, an unintended result that may or may not happen, is permitted, since the halacha follows Rabbi Shimon on Shabbat. It is forbidden if you actually have the result in mind.
  • A pesik reisha, a certain result, is forbidden even without intention.
  • For Sefaradim there is no karov lepesik reisha: only a definite result counts.
  • A pesik reisha delo nihah lei is not a Torah liability but is forbidden rabbinically. If the prohibition is itself rabbinic, it is permitted; this is the basis for opening a fridge with a light you do not need.
  • A doubtful pesik reisha, such as closing a box that may hold an insect, is permitted following the Turei Zahav.
