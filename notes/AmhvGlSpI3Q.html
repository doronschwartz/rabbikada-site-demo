Topic: The fifth principle: to serve God alone, without any intermediary, and what prayer is for Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Avodah Zarah 1 and Hilchot Teshuvah 3:7; Rabbi Yosef Albo, Sefer HaIkkarim Shiur: Sixth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series at Tiferet Eyal on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Tuesday 21 January 2020

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Completing the first group

The first four principles describe God: He exists, He is uniquely one, He has no body, and everything depends on Him. "If you just stop there," the Rabbi said, you might have a deep philosophical idea of God but feel He is remote: "What's it got to do with me?" The fifth principle completes the group by saying that this God is One we can relate to and serve.

The Rambam writes that it is fitting to serve and praise God alone, and not anything below Him, not the stars, the spheres or the elements, even as intermediaries. A great part of the Torah, he notes, is about the prohibition of idolatry. The Rambam even explains misvot like not shaving the corners of the head as distancing us from the practices of idolatrous priests. And the prophets' two great themes, the Rabbi observed, are idolatry and social justice.

The participants found the three parts: 1. Serve God. 2. Do not serve anything else. 3. Do not serve God through anything else, even with the best intentions.

The Rambam treats the third as severely as the second. The Rabbi suggested it is really the heart of the principle, since serving God is an action, and not serving idols already follows from His unity. The Ani Maamin has all three: "to Him alone it is fitting to pray, and not to pray to anyone else." The Yigdal's line, "He is Master of the world, showing His greatness to every creature", is vaguer.

Part one: what is prayer for?

"Worship" in English suggests a great being who needs people to bow before Him. So why pray at all? If God is all-powerful and all-good, we already have what is best for us. And we cannot change God's mind, which would make Him limited. The Rabbi gave three approaches:

1. A system (the Kabbalists and the Ramhal). Every human act has cosmic significance. In Daat Tevunot the Ramhal explains that God created a system in which, as it were, He needs our prayers. The good is waiting, but it only flows down when we pray. This is no imperfection, since God made the system and can change it. A participant compared it to science: to move a ball you have to kick it. The Rabbi accepted that as a good description.

2. Meditation (the Rambam). The Rambam never discusses whether prayer "works". Prayer is contemplation, a spiritual exercise. Someone who truly connects with God rises to a level where things work out for him, just as one who is close to a powerful person finds his affairs sorted out. We do not pray to get something; prayer is valuable in itself, and the results follow.

3. Transformation (Rabbi Yosef Albo). A doctor tells a patient he needs an operation, but if he improves, a bitter medicine will do. The patient exercises, and the operation is cancelled. He keeps going, and the bitter medicine becomes a sweet one, and then none at all. The doctor never changed his mind; the patient changed. So too prayer changes us, and a different person merits a different decree. That, say Hazal, is why the matriarchs were barren: their prayers made them who they were.

How does prayer transform us? Albo gives two ways: - Self-judgment. The root of tefillah is pe-lamed-lamed, as in pelilim, judges, and "lehitpalel" is reflexive, something we do to ourselves. When I pray for wisdom or health, I ask: am I doing all I can? - Acknowledgement. A father would be hurt if his son asked his uncle for money. Prayer acknowledges God as the source and deepens the relationship. The serpent's curse, to eat dust, seems no curse at all, since dust is everywhere; but it means God has sent it away, like a father who says "here is a mansion, leave me alone".

Then how can praying for someone else help? On the first approach, energy is energy, whoever brings it down. On the others, the answer is arevut: Jews are bound together, as one person can make Kiddush for another. When one of us rises, it affects others, and more so when a great hakham prays.

Someone asked whether "teshuvah, tefillah and sedakah" cancel a decree like a mechanism. The Rabbi answered that they change the person, and the person on a new level is no longer subject to the same decree.

Part two: how idolatry began

The Rambam (Hilchot Avodah Zarah 1) describes how, in the generation of Enosh, people reasoned that God made the stars to govern the world and honoured them as His servants, so honouring them would please Him, as honouring a king's ministers pleases the king. They built temples to the stars, but at first only as a way to serve God. Their children forgot the reason, false prophets arose, and in time God's name was forgotten except by a few, until Avraham. As a child he wondered who turns the sphere, with no teacher, among idolaters, "and his heart was exploring". He was "Avraham HaIvri", on one side against the whole world.

The lesson: things meant as a means take on a significance of their own. The Rabbi warned that amulets, segulot and charms can become this. If a person believes an object has its own power to help him, "that's avodah zarah, black and white". People want a crutch because God feels abstract.

The golden calf, says the Ramban, was not a rejection of God forty days after Sinai. Moshe had been their link, and when he was late they wanted something tangible to connect to God. That too is forbidden. So is thinking that prayers "go up" only through a great rabbi. Someone else's prayer can help, but it never replaces our own.

Someone asked about the Talmud's statement that the urge for idolatry was once very strong. The Rabbi explained it as a psychological need, the feeling that we cannot reach God ourselves, which still has its traces today.

Part three: angels and intermediaries

The Rambam lists among the heretics "one who serves a star or constellation so that it should be an intermediary between him and God", completing the list that matches the first five principles. The Jerusalem Talmud says: when a person is in trouble, "let him not cry to Mikhael or Gavriel, but to Me, and I will answer him."

But the Rabbi raised a real difficulty. The Babylonian Talmud records a request to the angels on entering a washroom: "guard me, help me, wait for me". On Friday night we sing "Barkhuni leshalom", "bless me with peace", to angels, and many Ashkenazim say "Makhnisei rahamim" in Selihot, asking angels to bring our prayers before God. Some great authorities did not say these; Rav Moshe Feinstein omitted "Barkhuni leshalom".

He offered an answer from Rabbi Shimshon Pincus. If I hire a man to kill someone, the killer bears the guilt, because he has free will. If I set my dog on someone, I am the killer, because the dog has none. Angels, called "holy animals", have no free will. If we treat them as independent powers who give us peace, that is avodah zarah. But if we are sending them, so to speak, to carry our prayer to God, it can be justified. So when we say "Barkhuni leshalom", the intention must be "take my prayer for peace before God", never "you give me peace".

The same applies at a grave. We may pray to God at the grave of a righteous person, hoping the merit of the place helps. But to pray to the dead person is forbidden. Asking a living saddik to pray for us is different, because he has free will and prays himself, but his prayer never replaces our own.

Summary

  • The fifth principle has three parts: serve God, serve nothing else, and serve Him through nothing else.
  • Prayer can be understood as bringing down blessing through a system God made, as meditation, or as self-transformation; none of them means changing God's mind.
  • Idolatry began with honouring God's "servants", and any means that takes on power of its own risks becoming it.
  • Even a golden calf "for God", or reliance on a rabbi's prayers instead of our own, crosses the line.
  • Where our prayers address angels, they can only mean asking them to carry our words to God.
