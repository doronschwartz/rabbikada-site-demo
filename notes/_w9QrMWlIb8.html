Topic: The tenth principle: God knows the deeds and thoughts of human beings, and how this fits with free will Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah, chapter 5; Moreh Nevuchim Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's monthly Sunday-night series in Wembley on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Sunday 24 February 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The principle, and the last group of four

This class opened the third and final group of principles: 10, God knows our deeds; 11, reward and punishment; 12, Mashiah; 13, the resurrection of the dead. The Rabbi called the whole group "consequences for your actions". Principle 10 must come first: if God did not know what we do, there could be no reward and punishment.

The Rambam's tenth principle is short. God "knows the works of men and is not unmindful of them". It is not as those who said "the Lord has forsaken the earth". His proofs: "God saw that the wickedness of man was great" and "the cry of Sedom and Amorah is great".

The later summaries add to it step by step. The Ani Maamin adds that God knows our thoughts: "He fashions their hearts together and understands all their deeds." No one disputes this, the Rabbi said. It is why half of the Viddui on Yom Kippur is about the heart, such as pride and scorn. The Yigdal adds foreknowledge: "He sees each thing's end at its very beginning." That raises the great question of the evening.

God cares what we do

The first lesson is encouraging. God did not create the world and walk away. He is involved and interested. "Human beings crave attention," the Rabbi said, and the greater the person, the more we want their attention. To know that the Creator notices every deed, word and thought means that nothing we do is wasted.

This gives meaning to misvot. Why wait six hours between meat and milk, or come to pray, unless God commanded it and hears us? The Talmud says that everything God does is for the good (Berakhot), and that no one so much as injures his finger unless it was decreed above (Hullin). This is hashgahah peratit, personal and detailed providence.

In the Moreh Nevuchim the Rambam sets out views on providence: - Epicurus: there is no providence at all (hence the word apikoros). - Aristotle: the world has order, but what happens within it is random. A falling leaf and a sinking ship are the same. - Some Islamic thinkers: everything is decreed, down to every leaf and spider's web. - The Rambam, and in the Rabbi's view most Jewish thinkers: nature runs its course, but wherever human beings are concerned there is providence. Even an animal is watched over if a person's livelihood depends on it.

Free will: a principle of faith in ourselves

Free will is not listed as one of the 13 principles, but it lies underneath 10 and 11. You do not punish a microwave that breaks. The Torah says it plainly: "I have set before you life and death... therefore choose life."

The Rabbi recalled a Shabbat sermon in which he asked why free will is not one of the 13. His answer: the 13 principles are our faith in God. Free will is a principle of faith in ourselves.

Then the question: if God knows today what I will choose tomorrow, how is my choice free? He shared that as a teenager this question troubled him deeply for years. Some religious traditions answer with fatalism, and he mentioned Calvin's idea that God has chosen in advance whom to save. That, he said, makes life drab and depressing. Judaism has no single answer, but several.

Three answers before the Rambam

1. Rabbi Saadiah Gaon: knowing is not causing. God knows "the final conclusion of man's activity", what I will choose, but His knowing does not make me choose it. One participant found this perfectly satisfying. The Rabbi admitted that it was the answer he was given in yeshiva and "it never satisfied me at all". If God already knows every choice in my life, what is the point of living it out?

2. The Ralbag (Rabbi Levi ben Gershon), and Rabbi Avraham ibn Daud: God limited His knowledge. To give us real freedom, God chose not to know our choices in advance. Since He chose it, they argue, it is no imperfection. The Rambam rejects this: just as God cannot catch a cold, He cannot limit Himself.

3. Rabbi Hasdai Crescas: determinism, not fatalism. Fatalism says everything happens because God decrees it. Determinism says I choose, but my choice follows from my genes, upbringing and history. Someone who knew every factor could predict it, and God does. Crescas himself admits this makes reward and punishment hard to explain.

Someone asked about the Holocaust: if God knew, why did He not stop it? The Rabbi distinguished the questions. Under normal circumstances God does not interfere with human free will, even when it does terrible harm. God may decree that a person will live this year, yet another person can choose to kill him. Free will can also work for good: God may decree that someone will be poor, yet another person can choose to take him in. Why God did not intervene in the Holocaust remains its own question.

The Rambam: God's knowledge is not like ours

The Rambam takes "both horns": we have full free will, and God has full knowledge. The contradiction exists only in our minds.

His reasoning rests on the unity of God. A person has knowledge, kindness or wisdom as qualities added to himself. God's attributes are His very essence. "He and His knowledge are one." So just as we cannot grasp God's essence, we cannot grasp His knowledge. The word "knowledge" for God and for man is a homonym. All the two share is that neither is ignorant.

The Rabbi added the idea of time. We live in past, present and future. God is above time. Watching someone act in the present does not force him to act. For God, everything is present.

He read the Rambam in Hilchot Teshuvah (chapter 5). Each person can be "righteous like Moshe or wicked like Yerovam". Nobody decrees it. Otherwise how could the prophets command "do this, do not do that"? And the answer to the question of foreknowledge "is longer than the earth and broader than the sea". "My thoughts are not your thoughts." The Raavad objects that the Rambam raised a question without answering it. The Rabbi defended the Rambam: the answer is that God's knowledge is of a different kind.

A participant objected that this just means "believe it because it makes no sense". The Rabbi disagreed. It makes a lot of sense, he said, if we keep a pure idea of God's unity. The problem only arises when we imagine God in our own image.

The point of free will: Rabbi Dessler

He ended with a practical idea from Rabbi Eliyahu Dessler of Gateshead, the "nekudat habehirah", the point of choice.

Everyone is shaped by habit and upbringing. A boy raised in a home where stealing is normal does not really choose whether to steal. His choice may be whether to kill. Someone raised keeping every detail of Shabbat does not wonder whether to work on Shabbat. His battle is how much Torah to learn, or how much kavanah to have in prayer.

Rabbi Dessler compares it to a front line in a war. Behind each army's lines there is no fighting; the battle is only at the front. Each of us has a front line, and it moves. Someone who eats non-kosher food for the first time makes a real choice. A year later it is habit, and his point of choice has dropped, though he is still accountable for how he got there. Someone who takes on Shabbat raises his point of choice. The Rabbi linked this to the hardening of Pharaoh's heart: by his own choices he lowered his point of choice.

Someone asked: if God decreed that a person be poor, are we going against God by helping? On the contrary, the Rabbi said. A fatalist might leave the poor man alone. We do not know God's plan, and God wants us to use our free will to help. He cited Rabbi Akiva's answer to Turnus Rufus: God leaves the poor in need so that we can earn merit by helping them.

Summary

  • God knows our deeds, words and thoughts, and the Yigdal adds that He knows the future.
  • His providence over human life means that nothing we do is wasted; for the Rambam, the rest of nature runs its course.
  • Free will is a principle of faith in ourselves, and it underlies reward and punishment.
  • Saadiah Gaon, the Ralbag and Crescas each resolve the conflict with foreknowledge differently; the Rambam holds both fully, because God's knowledge is one with His essence and beyond us.
  • Rabbi Dessler: each of us has a point of choice that we can raise or lower.
