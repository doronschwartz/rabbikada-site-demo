A short set of laws

The laws of Hanukkah are brief: Siman 670 has three paragraphs and the section runs to about fourteen simanim. The Rabbi planned to cover the relevant parts over two Thursday classes. From the 25th of Kislev, eight days begin on which one may not give a hespeid (a eulogy) or fast. Work, however, is fully permitted, unlike Yom Tov.

Eulogies

The Rabbi explained that the hespeid of the old days was the wailing of hired women who beat their chests and cried out. A modern eulogy read from a page is hardly that, so he is somewhat lenient in practice. When a family is grieving during Hanukkah it is very hard to tell them they cannot eulogise a parent, so he asks them to make the speech a celebration of the person rather than one that stirs up sadness. This is similar to a Friday afternoon, when a hespeid is not given after midday. The one hespeid that is allowed on Hanukkah is for a hakham, in his presence at the funeral.

Women and work

There is a custom, not found in the Rambam or earlier sources but about six or seven hundred years old, that women do not do work while the candles are burning. Points the Rabbi made: - It covers only the arduous tasks, like the ones avoided on Rosh Hodesh: sewing and laundry. Cooking and baking are fine. - "While the candles are burning" means half an hour, not the hours the oil may last. On Friday it is half an hour after nightfall. - A woman who must finish something urgent, or whose job is laundry, can be lenient. - The authorities quoted in the Mishnah Berurah rejected the practice of not working all eight days: when you are idle you get bored, and boredom leads to sin. The Rabbi observed that eight days is short, but in the old days nobody had holidays. He compared the Mishnah in Ketubot: even with a hundred maidservants a woman still works, because idleness leads to sin. - The Mishnah Berurah's reason for the custom is that people might end up working by the light of the candles, and that women were part of the miracle, through the story of Yehudit. The Rabbi said that arguably this no longer applies, with electric light, and with modern laundry being the press of a button.

Is a Hanukkah meal a misvah?

The Rema writes that the many meals people hold on Hanukkah are voluntary and not a misvah, because Hanukkah is about praise and thanks (Hallel VeHodaah) and not about feasting. He cites a custom already known 700 years ago in the days of the Maharam of Rothenburg. The Rabbi noted that Chazal never obligated a meal on Hanukkah, while on Purim there is one. His explanation: Purim was a threat to the body, so we celebrate with wine, meat and bread. Hanukkah was a spiritual threat, an attempt to stop Shabbat and Brit Milah, so we celebrate with Hallel, the candles and Torah study.

The Rema nevertheless finds a small misvah in these meals. In those days the Hanukkat HaMizbeah took place. The Midrash says the Mishkan was finished on the 25th of Kislev but erected only on the 1st of Nisan, so Hashem owes Kislev its own inauguration and gives it back in Hanukkah. This is why we read the Nesiim in the Torah on Hanukkah. The Rema adds that when the meal includes words of Torah and songs, it becomes a seudat misvah, and the Rabbi pointed out that even a restaurant table where Torah is spoken counts.

So the nightly Hanukkah parties of today are well sourced, but the question is whether they come with Torah and praise of Hashem. If so, have as many as you like. If it is only a party, something is lacking. The Rabbi pointed to a blunt responsum quoted in the Biur Halakhah, saying that the joy should be mixed with the joy of Torah, and that regular Torah study should not be lost because of the parties, and a warning against spending the night playing cards.

What we remember on Hanukkah

The Rabbi listed four things we recall, and the way we do so: - The oil: lighting the menorah. - The military victory of the few over the many: Al HaNisim and Hallel. - The miracle of Yehudit: the Rema's note that some eat cheese, because she gave the general cheese before he fell asleep. This is only a custom. - The Hanukkat HaMizbeah: the reading of the Nesiim.

Oily food, doughnuts and latkes, came much later. It is a custom, and the halakha reminds us to look after our health, so a doughnut on the first night is fine but there is no misvah to eat fried food every night.

Siman 671: the menorah

The Shulhan Arukh uses unusual language: one must be very careful with this misvah. Even a poor person who lives on charity must ask for help or sell clothes or borrow in order to have one light per night. The Rambam also writes in unusually warm words that this misvah is especially beloved. The Gemara says that someone careful with the lights will have children who are Torah scholars, a reward the Gemara gives also for the Shabbat lights.

How many lights? The Shulhan Arukh says one lights one on the first night and adds one each night until eight. The Rabbi explained the levels: 1. The basic law: one light per household per night. 2. The beautified form: each person lights. 3. The most beautiful: each person adds a light each night.

The Rambam sees the levels as stacked on one another. Tosafot says that once you go to the third level you drop the second, since many people each adding lights would make it impossible to tell which night it is. The Rabbi observed the unusual feature that the Shulhan Arukh follows Tosafot, with one menorah per household, while the Rema follows the Rambam's view of each person lighting. The Taz noticed this four hundred years ago. The Rambam himself held the law as the Rema does, but recorded that the custom in Sepharad was one person per household, so the Sephardi custom goes back at least to his time.

The Rabbi also noted that the Shulhan Arukh skips the basic law and gives only the beautified form, but it must be known: someone with only a few candles lights one each night.

Practical cases from the class

  • Children who come home from school with a menorah: for Sefaradim, they should light in a different room or at a different spot, not next to the main menorah or in the same windowsill. The Rabbi does not think it correct for Sefaradim to place two menorahs together.
  • A guest who is staying for the whole Shabbat and eating there counts as part of that household. A guest who is not part of the household for the whole Shabbat is a harder case: he must find another room or entrance in which to light.
  • The Sephardi custom is to light indoors opposite the mezuzah. If there are small children around, it is better not to light on a windowsill. Draughts that blow out the flame are a concern.

Practical summary

  • The 25th of Kislev begins eight days with no hespeid and no fasting; work is permitted.
  • Women's custom of not working applies only to arduous tasks and only for half an hour after lighting; it can be relaxed when needed.
  • A Hanukkah meal is not an obligation; it becomes a seudat misvah when it includes Torah and praise.
  • One must go to great lengths, even borrowing, to light one light a night.
  • Sefaradim light one menorah per household, adding a light each night; Ashkenazim follow the Rema, with each person lighting.
  • Sephardi practice: light indoors opposite the mezuzah, and make sure that two menorahs are not placed in the same spot.
