Topic: The structure of the Rosh Hashanah service, part 1: the opening, the piyutim, the Amidah of Shaharit and the Torah readings Source: The Spanish and Portuguese mahzor Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Holland Park, Wednesday 18 September 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Purpose of the series

Over the summer the class had studied the general structure of prayer. Now the Rabbi turned to Shaharit and Musaf of Rosh Hashanah, to understand what happens from beginning to end and which parts matter most. Page numbers refer to the mahzorim used in the synagogue.

The opening and Yihud Hashem

From page 44 to page 73 the service is a regular Shabbat morning, the preliminary part from about 8.45 to 9.30. The only addition is "Hashem Hu HaElokim" before "Hashem Melekh."

This already introduces the theme of all the Rosh Hashanah prayers: Yihud Hashem, the unity of God. It does not just mean there is one God and not two. It means that everything in the universe comes from Hashem; nothing happens without His will. Yud-he-vav-he is the name of mercy, Elokim the name of judgment. People see evil and imagine a God responsible only for the good. "Hashem Hu HaElokim" says that good and bad flow from one source. "When you live with that, your life's very different," because nothing will anger you, though it is easier said than done.

Rosh Hashanah is the vision of how we want the world to be. In the Amidah we pray that all evil will dissipate like smoke. Smoke looks big, but when you touch it there is nothing there; so is temptation. He told the Hasidic story of a man who came to a marketplace shouting that he was selling the elixir of life, then ran. The whole town chased him, and when they caught him and forced open his hand, it was empty.

The piyutim

The first addition is a poem at the foot of page 73, which the Rabbi attributed to Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi of Spain, a poet who left his yeshiva and community to move to the Land of Israel and died soon after arriving. Most of our poetry in prayer comes from the Spanish Rishonim. It is an alphabetical poem asking Hashem to accept our prayer, with a melody that developed later.

Prayer proper only begins at Barekhu, so the poets took licence to add before it. But these poems sit within Pesukei DeZimrah, between Barukh SheAmar and Yishtabah, and many halakhic authorities were uncomfortable with this. Most Sefaradi communities therefore say them after the repetition of the Amidah, and that was the custom at Holland Park, as it had been in Wembley. - "Shofet kol haares," by Shelomoh ibn Gabirol, signed in acrostic "Shelomoh hazak": may our prayer be accepted like the morning offering in the Temple. - The second day's poem, by Yehudah HaLevi, built on Hashem's name: a section of ten lines beginning with yud, five with he, six with vav and five with he, each line beginning and ending with Hashem. - Different poems are said on each day because they are songs, not obligatory prayer, and a new song keeps things fresh. Their purpose is to get us in the mood, not to say every word.

The Amidah of Shaharit

The first two blessings are the same as every Amidah: the merit of the patriarchs, and Hashem as the source of life. The Geonim, about a thousand years ago, added requests for life ("Zokhrenu LeHayim," "Mi khamokha av harahaman"). How they could add requests to the first blessings, which are meant to be praise, is a well-known question; the Rabbi said he has a whole shiur on it. If you forget them, the prayer is still valid.

The third blessing is the heart. On a normal day it is short. On Rosh Hashanah it becomes a prayer about Hashem's sovereignty: may everyone recognise You, may the righteous see and rejoice, may wickedness vanish like smoke, and it ends "HaMelekh HaKadosh."

"Rosh Hashanah is not about praying for yourself." If you pray for yourself, you lose the focus of the day. The Zohar says that those who pray for children, healing and food are like dogs barking at their owner: give me, give me. Those things follow automatically when Hashem's presence is recognised. He said he does not like the phrase "serving God," which he called a Christian idea; God does not need us to serve Him. Rosh Hashanah is about sharing God's vision for the world.

The CEO parable: an employee of a huge company gets five minutes a year with the CEO. Most ask for a new company car, a laptop or a pay rise, and the boss says fine. One says: "This is an incredible company. What can I do to be part of your vision?" The boss replies, "I'll give you everything you need. We're on the same page." He added a candid warning from his own experience: you can end up praying for Yihud Hashem in the hope that your personal prayers will then be answered. It has to be genuine.

A participant asked about "mehayeh metim," reviving the dead. The Rabbi explained briefly that resurrection is one of the Rambam's principles of faith, based on the Talmud with hints in Tanakh.

Additions at the end of Shaharit

  • The fourth blessing is short. Its last paragraph again asks that the whole world recognise Hashem; the archaic English translation, he said, makes it sound like a big God who needs to shine, which is not what the Hebrew means.
  • In the repetition, "Hayom teamesenu" is a later alphabetical poem.
  • Avinu Malkenu is a beautiful but much later addition. "Anything in the prayer of Rosh Hashanah which is asking for something physical is a later addition." The Men of the Great Assembly, about 2,200 years ago, included no requests at all.
  • In the Kaddish we add the "gates": of light, blessing, healing, livelihood. The Hida observed that people answer amen loudly for healing and livelihood, and quietly or not at all for the gates concerning Hashem.

His theory for why these additions were made: the concept of Hashem's sovereignty is lofty, and most people arrive with their own worries. So the hakhamim added personal requests to draw people into the prayer. "Save your energy for the bits of prayer which are talking about God's presence in the world."

The Torah readings

  • Day one: Hashem remembered Sarah and she conceived. "Remember" here means to pay attention, to visit; on Rosh Hashanah Hashem takes account of everyone.
  • Haftarah, day one: Hannah and Peninah. Hannah's silent prayer is the origin of praying quietly. The story teaches that life is fickle: Peninah had ten children and Hannah none, and within a few years fortunes turned. The Ben Ish Hai tells of a wise man whose ring read "This too shall pass," which he looked at in bad times and in good. The Rabbi noted that no one at the previous Rosh Hashanah could have imagined the year that followed.
  • Day two: the Akedah: because it happened on Rosh Hashanah, for the merit of the patriarchs, and because the ram's horn caught in the thicket is, the Midrash says, the horn blown at Sinai and the one that will be blown for Mashiah.
  • Haftarah, day two: Yirmiyahu, read for its last verse: "Haven yakir li Efrayim... zakhor ezkerenu od," Hashem's remembrance of Israel.
  • Then "Et shaarei rason," a highly emotional poem on the Akedah, signed by Yehudah Shemuel Abbas, which prepares us for the shofar.

The shofar is blown 100 times on each day (in practice 101, the last simply marking the end), to be continued the following week.

Summary

  • Until page 73 the service is like Shabbat; the only addition, "Hashem Hu HaElokim," introduces the theme of Yihud Hashem.
  • The piyutim are lovely later poems meant to set the mood; at Holland Park, as in most Sefaradi communities, they are said after the Amidah.
  • The heart of Shaharit is the third blessing on Hashem's sovereignty; every request for personal needs is a later addition.
  • Rosh Hashanah is about sharing Hashem's vision for the world, not presenting a list of demands.
  • The readings and haftarot teach remembrance, the fickleness of life, and the merit of the Akedah.
