Topic: Why we sit in the sukkah, and how to live in it: eating, drinking, sleeping and the berakhah Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Simanim 625 and 639 (Seifim 1-2), with the Rema and the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in person and on Zoom, Thursday 12 September 2024 (the first class in the Hilchot Sukkah series)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

One verse, twenty simanim

With Sukkot five weeks away, the Rabbi set a new plan: instead of the usual Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur halachot, to get through Hilchot Sukkah, Simanim 625 to 645. There are twenty chapters on a misvah that only one verse in the Torah describes, and what matters most for us is how to live in the sukkah.

Siman 625 has one paragraph. It quotes the verse, "You shall dwell in sukkot for seven days... so that your generations know that I made the Children of Israel dwell in sukkot when I took them out of Egypt." The Gemara brings a debate: Rabbi Akiva says these were real huts, Rabbi Eliezer says they were the clouds of glory (Ananei HaKavod) that surrounded us. The Tur and the Shulhan Arukh take the second view and add a few words: these sukkot are the clouds with which HaKadosh Barukh Hu protected us from the sun and heat of the desert.

Why quote a reason at all?

The Tur and the Shulhan Arukh do not usually give reasons for misvot; that is the work of books like the Sefer HaHinukh. So why a whole chapter for a verse and its reason? The Bah (Bayit Hadash) explains that where the Torah itself says "do this so that you remember", the remembering is part of the misvah. The same applies to sisit ("so that you remember all the misvot of HaShem") and to tefillin ("to remember that HaShem took us out of Egypt").

So when we sit in the sukkah, it is not enough to intend to fulfil the misvah. In the first instance we should also have in mind that we sit there to remember the clouds of glory that enveloped us when we left Egypt.

Why in the autumn?

Why do it now and not at any other time? The Tur gives the famous answer: in the summer everyone sits outside in huts, so no one would see that you are doing it for the misvah. In the autumn, when people have come back from their holidays and gone indoors, going out into a sukkah is clearly for HaShem.

What people do not know, said the Rabbi, is that the Rambam in the Moreh Nevuchim answers the opposite way. The summer is too hot to sit outside and the winter too cold; HaShem does not give us a misvah to cause us pain, so He gave it at the perfect time, the autumn. Each Rishon speaks from his own climate: the Tur from cold Ashkenaz, the Rambam from Andalusia, where October is beautiful.

A friend once pointed out to him that this shows two ways of seeing the misvot. In one, the misvot are a test, meant to be hard so that we show we are doing them for HaShem. In the other, the Rambam's, the misvot are there "to make your life better", so HaShem gives them at the best possible time. Pesah and Sukkot fall six months apart on the same date, so each gives spiritual energy for the half year that follows. Asked about the simple agricultural reason, Hag HaAsif, he agreed that is part of it, but the Rishonim were giving deeper answers.

Building the sukkah, and living in it

The Rema brings the custom of the particular to start building the sukkah straight after Yom Kippur, going from one misvah to the next. One of the Aharonim held that you may not build it before Yom Kippur at all, based on a Midrash that the sukkah is like going into exile in case that was decreed on Yom Kippur. Others answered that the Midrash speaks about sitting in the sukkah, not building it, and an extra misvah before Yom Kippur is a good thing. So if you can build your sukkah before Yom Kippur, do it; if not, build it as soon as you can afterwards.

Siman 639 begins: you eat, drink, sleep and live in the sukkah all seven days, day and night, as you live at home, including phone calls and chatting with friends. The Shelah says not to speak about mundane things in the holy sukkah, but the Rabbi called that impractical: if you have business to attend to on Hol HaMoed, better to do it in the sukkah than outside.

Practical examples: - Bring your regular crockery, tablecloths and best glasses into the sukkah. Do not eat off plastic and run back into the house. Glasses may be left there. - Serving dishes may be brought in while serving, but should be taken out when the meal is over. Plates can wait until you clear up. - A cooking pot (kederah), which never comes to the dining table, should not be brought in at all. Some Rishonim, reading the Rif, hold that this can even invalidate the sukkah; the Ran understands it only as a sign that one is not treating it as a sukkah. Because of this view, take care, especially on the first night, that nothing is in the sukkah that would not be on your dining table, so that there is no doubt about the berakhah. - Candles: in a small or crowded sukkah, leave open candles outside. A candle in a covered holder is much less of a problem. - Nothing that you would not do in your dining room, such as washing up. The Biur Halacha writes that a married couple with privacy may be together there, as part of normal living.

"The sukkah is your home, but it's primarily the dining room. It's the living room of your house."

Sleeping in the sukkah

Seif 2 says one may not sleep outside the sukkah even a short sleep (sheinat arai), because even a nap refreshes a person. The poskim say that closing your eyes for less than the time it takes to walk a hundred amot (as we learned in Siman 44), a minute or two, is not a problem. The Ben Ish Hai writes that one should wake someone dozing in shul on Sukkot.

Why, then, do so few people sleep in the sukkah? The Rema gives the reasons. The Mordekhai says it is too cold, and one who is in discomfort (mistaer) is exempt. A second reason is that one lives with one's wife, and there is usually no private sukkah for that. The Magen Avraham adds that being unable to sleep together is itself a discomfort; the Mishnah Berurah limits this, but the Rabbi read the Magen Avraham more broadly.

He drew a principle: in pesak, ask whether a heter came before the practice or after it. But if you have an indoor sukkah where a heater does the job, or you are a couple, or a couple with small children, living somewhere warm, "there's no excuse not to sleep in the sukkah."

Someone asked whether one should simply build a bigger sukkah with space to sleep. He answered that a sukkah is meant to be a temporary dwelling; doing so is praiseworthy, but whether there is a duty to go that far is questionable.

Eating, drinking and the berakhah

A snack (akhilat arai) may be eaten outside the sukkah. Even bread up to a kebeisah (about 56 grams) counts as a snack. This helps those in London flats with no place for a sukkah: eat the first two nights in the synagogue sukkah, and at other Shabbat and Yom Tov meals at home have less than a kebeisah of bread. Drinks, fruit, meat and fish may be eaten outside, even when friends gather over wine. But once you have started a meal, even water must be drunk in the sukkah. Not drinking even water outside is praiseworthy, but not required even of a talmid hakham.

Then the berakhah. The Rambam holds that you say Leshev BaSukkah whenever you enter after a break, even with no meal. A custom developed in Europe, because of the cold, to say it only when eating. Sefaradim say it only when eating more than a kebeisah of bread, or about 170 grams of mezonot.

The Rabbi called this "one of the most difficult halachot for me." The rule that we do not say a berakhah when in doubt applies to a dispute, not to a custom, and he has never found an explanation for being so strict here.

Practical summary

  • When sitting in the sukkah, have in mind to remember the clouds of glory that surrounded us in the desert.
  • Build the sukkah before Yom Kippur if you can; otherwise as soon as possible after it.
  • Treat the sukkah as your living room: bring your proper tableware, take serving dishes out after the meal, never bring in a cooking pot, and keep open candles out of a small sukkah.
  • Sleep is not allowed outside the sukkah, even a nap, apart from a minute or two. Where it is warm or the sukkah is indoors, sleeping in it should be the norm.
  • Snacks, drinks and up to a kebeisah of bread may be eaten outside, but not in the middle of a meal.
  • For Sefaradim, Leshev BaSukkah is said only on more than a kebeisah of bread or about 170 grams of mezonot.
