Topic: What needs a hekhsher for Pesah: the one stringency of Pesah, four disputes between the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema, and medicines

Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Pesah (Simanim 447, 451), with the Rema

Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for the Montefiore diploma, Monday 21 March 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The one stringency of Pesah

This was the Rabbi's first class with the group in person after a series over Zoom. With Purim behind us and Pesah under thirty days away, he promised: "If you listen carefully, I'll probably save you a few hundred pounds over the course of Pesah. And I'm not joking." Most of the room was Ashkenazi, and he said there was room for leniency even for them.

All year, non-kosher food mixed into kosher food is nullified in sixty. Sixty is the number Hazal use for something present but not perceptible: sleep is one sixtieth of death, Shabbat one sixtieth of the World to Come. On Pesah, however, hames is not nullified even in sixty, because it is a davar sheyesh lo mattirin: it will become permitted after seven days, so there is no need to rely on nullification. This is a rabbinic rule; from the Torah, nullification works on Pesah too.

The question is what to do with this one stringency. Most Rishonim said: where the Gemara tells us Pesah is stricter, it is stricter, and everywhere else the ordinary leniencies of kashrut apply. Some Ashkenazi Rishonim extended it to other areas, and the Rema follows them.

Four disputes

1. Does nullified hames "reawaken"? All year, once something is nullified it stays nullified. Everyone agrees that hames that falls into food before Pesah is nullified at that point. The Shulhan Arukh rules, with the Rambam, that it does not reawaken on Pesah. The Rema brings those who are strict and hold that it does. Even for the Rema it matters: such food may be eaten on Erev Pesah afternoon, and it may be kept over Pesah and eaten afterwards. It is not hames in your possession, and the pot it is in may still be used for other things.

2. Must we assume something went wrong? The Shulhan Arukh applies the principle used throughout kashrut: assume things went right until you see otherwise. Unguarded Pesah food, an open box of almonds where children were eating sandwiches, is fine unless you saw something fall in. The Rema assumes a problem unless the food was guarded.

3. A pot unused for 24 hours. All year, after 24 hours absorbed taste is spoiled (noten taam lifgam) and does not forbid. The Shulhan Arukh applies this on Pesah: if by mistake you cooked in a hames pot unused for 24 hours, the food may be eaten. The Rema forbids it until after Pesah. This is only after the fact. "I'm not that radical."

4. Kashering by majority use. The Shulhan Arukh says to kasher a utensil according to its main use; one occasional use at a higher heat does not count. The Rema requires kashering according to the most severe use, even if it happened only once.

In each case, the Rabbi stressed, the Rema's wording is "some are strict, and that is our custom," not "this is the law". "Always pay attention to the wording."

Sugar, salt and yogurt

Consider sugar, salt, oil or 100% yogurt from the supermarket, with nothing but that on the label. Why would they need a Pesah hekhsher?

  • We do not assume anything went wrong, such as a worker's sandwich dropping crumbs into the sugar.
  • Even if it did, it happened before Pesah, so it is nullified, and for Sephardim it does not reawaken.
  • Hidden ingredients? Every ingredient must be listed by law. Just as many rely on Rav Moshe Feinstein for unsupervised milk, because companies fear the law, there is no reason to suspect unlisted hames.

So for Sephardim, as long as they are bought before Pesah, salt, sugar, tea, coffee, oil, 100% juices, pure yogurt, pure spices and dried fruit do not need a Pesah hekhsher. Jams with nothing but fruit and sugar are fine; some question citric acid, but the Rabbi's understanding is that it is not a hames ingredient. Buying on Hol HaMoed is arguably fine too, but it is better to buy before.

Rav Ovadia Yoseph goes much further. If bread fell into the barrel of wine for the four cups, strain it and use it. If wheat that became hames got mixed with wheat guarded for massot, the massot may be baked from it before Pesah and used at the Seder, even though you know there is hames inside. If so, "all the more so" innocent things like sugar and salt. He also records that the custom was to buy dried fruit in the market.

"If you want to be stringent and you have the money to do so, fine, great." But a stringency must not turn into a leniency: a family that spends everything on kosher-for-Pesah water and sugar and then cannot buy the children clothes for Yom Tov "is called not being balanced".

A word to Ashkenazim

The Rema is strict, which is why Ashkenazim generally buy these items with a hekhsher. But since the Rema himself calls it a stringency, the London Beth Din issued its list at the start of Covid allowing ordinary sugar and salt. For Ashkenazim who are struggling financially, the Rabbi suggested that one could arguably be lenient, with advice from their own rabbi, though as a rule Ashkenazim should be strict.

For Sephardim this is nothing new with Covid: "Every single year these things do not need a hekhsher," and any knowledgeable Sephardi talmid hakham will say the same.

From the questions

  • Does intention matter, if a product is made specifically for Pesah? No. "It's not about intention, it's about time": when did the mixture happen? Even for Ashkenazim, hames that falls in on Erev Pesah afternoon is nullified. This is why many cook everything before Pesah.
  • An irony pointed out by Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul: the food most likely to contain hames is massah. A baker only needs to lose track of the time. Yet because we ban so much else, people end up eating mainly massah.
  • Old spices from the year-round cupboard should not be used, since steam from boiling pasta may have reached them. Buy new packages.

Medicines and toothpaste

Hames unfit even for a dog is not hames from the Torah. But the Hakhamim said that one who chooses to eat it gives it importance, so it becomes rabbinically forbidden. Some therefore require a hekhsher on all medicines. Rav Ovadia Yoseph and many others, Ashkenazim too, reject this: when you take a tablet you want the medicine and care nothing for the drop of hames in it. So all tasteless or bitter medicines are permitted, whether or not they are life-preserving. Sweet syrups for children need a hekhsher. Toothpaste has sweeteners added to make it pleasant; the Rabbi does not believe it needs a hekhsher, but understands those who are strict.

"Even if you keep everything which we've learnt, you'll still have a hag kasher."

Practical summary

  • For Sephardim, products of pure, non-hames ingredients bought before Pesah need no Pesah hekhsher.
  • Ashkenazim generally buy with a hekhsher; in financial hardship, ask your rabbi.
  • Do not let stringencies cost the family what it truly needs for Yom Tov.
  • Use new packets of spices for Pesah.
  • Tasteless or bitter medicines are permitted; sweet syrups need a hekhsher.
