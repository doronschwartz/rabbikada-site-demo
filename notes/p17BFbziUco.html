This recording is the second half of that evening's class and begins in the middle of a sentence, continuing from the first part ("Permanent or Professional? The Three Kinds of Knot").

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where the class stood

The recording opens as the Rabbi finishes explaining the view of Rashi and the Rosh: even seven days is a "short time" (zeman katsar). A participant asked for clarification, and the Rabbi set out the difference between the two approaches.

  • The Rambam has two conditions for a knot to be forbidden by the Torah: it must be a professional knot (maaseh uman) and it must be permanent.
  • Rashi and the Rosh do not look at the skill of the knot at all. For them everything depends on how long it lasts (kayama).

The Rabbi noted that the Mishnah Berurah and the Rema are strict for both, but the point here is only to understand the opinion of Rashi and the Rosh. For them a knot is forbidden only if it is permanent. A knot made by a professional that is meant to last only a short time is permitted.

A test case: a professional knot for three days

The Rabbi then put a question to the class. Suppose someone ties a professional knot (maaseh uman) and intends it to last three days. What is the law?

  • According to the Rambam: since the knot is professional, it is Rabbinically forbidden, "no question about it".
  • According to Rashi and the Rosh: it depends on how long counts as a short time.

This is where the Taz and the Beit Yoseph differ. The Beit Yoseph holds that for Rashi and the Rosh a short time means up to seven days, so a three-day knot is permitted. The Taz speaks of one day, which the Pri Megadim explains as 24 hours, and then a three-day knot would be Rabbinically forbidden. The Rabbi stressed that it is important to know which Rishon each answer follows.

Intention

Does "intending to untie" mean that you must actually untie it? The Rabbi said the intention is what counts. If a person planned to untie the knot and some circumstance prevented him, such as having to take a flight before the end of Shabbat, then it is fine. But if it happens again and again, one must ask whether his intention was real or just a pretext. On an odd occasion, one goes after the intention at the time.

A non-professional knot for six days

The next question was whether one may make a non-professional knot (kesher hedyot) that stays for six days. The Rabbi's point was that with each of these questions one has to think through which approach, and which Rishon, one is following.

Reading the Shulhan Arukh

The Rabbi then went quickly through the Shulhan Arukh's examples, such as the knots cobblers make in shoes, a rope tied to a bucket and the reins of an animal. He said this fits the approach of the Mishnah Berurah rather than the Taz, since it depends on what ordinary people do. The Rema then records the two views, 24 hours (one day) and seven days.

He said that once these building blocks are clear (the Rambam and his dispute, Rashi and the Rosh and their dispute, seven days or 24 hours) the rest of the laws of Kosher will flow easily. The class would go over this again briefly at the start of the next week.

Looking ahead: shoelaces

The Rabbi introduced the practical question for the coming weeks: can you tie shoelaces on Shabbat? A single knot with a bow is definitely a kesher hedyot, a non-professional knot. He asked the class why it is permitted. Class members offered answers, and he pointed out that some people tie their shoes once and keep them tied for weeks, slipping them on and off, while others untie them each time.

He noted that the Beit Yoseph brings a source (the Rabbi was not sure which Rishon) saying that in earlier times people did not take off their shoes for seven days, only from Shabbat to Shabbat, and that they did not go to bed in their shoes. The coming weeks would cover shoelaces, double knots, bows and ties.

Practical summary

  • The Rambam forbids (by the Torah) a knot that is both professional and permanent. Either one alone is Rabbinic.
  • Rashi and the Rosh look only at how long the knot lasts, not the skill.
  • A professional knot for three days: Rabbinic for the Rambam, and for Rashi and the Rosh it depends on whether "a short time" is seven days (the Beit Yoseph) or 24 hours (the Taz).
  • A knot tied with the genuine intention to untie it soon is judged by that intention, but repeated "circumstances" raise doubts.
  • A single knot with a bow (kesher hedyot) is the basic case for shoelaces; double knots and ties were left for later classes.
