Topic: Two customs shared by every Seder table, and Sefaradi and Ashkenazi customs compared Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Wembley to a joint gathering with neighbouring communities, March 2015, shortly before Pesah (date estimated from the recording)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Similarities first

The Rabbi had been asked to share Sefaradi Pesah customs with the Ashkenazim present, and the differences among Sefaradim from Spain and Portugal, Syria, Iraq and India. Before the differences, he said, let us look at two things every Seder table in the world shares.

Similarity one: questions

From Mah Nishtanah onwards, the whole night is built to arouse questions, not only from children but from adults. The Torah introduces the four sons with "when your son asks you." Every night we mention the Exodus; on Seder night we relive it through question and answer.

So what if you are alone at the Seder? Must you ask yourself the questions? Those present offered thoughtful answers. The Rambam rules that even alone, a person asks Mah Nishtanah.

Why? Egypt was not only hard physical labour; it was a dictatorship. Under a dictator you cannot ask, object or say what you think. When Moshe asked Pharaoh to lighten the work, Pharaoh's answer was more work, so they would have no time to think about freedom. Pesah celebrates both freedoms, and above all the freedom of the mind. "How is intellectual freedom manifested the most? When you can ask." The question is not just a means to an answer; asking is itself freedom. The Kabbalists read Pesah as peh sah, the mouth that speaks.

He gave a Shabbat example. Sad, trapping, is one of the thirty-nine melakhot. May you put a child in a cot, or lock someone in a cell, on Shabbat? The poskim permit it. A human being is not trapped by locking up his body, because his mind is still free, thinking about a holiday in Australia. His true essence is his mind and soul, and that is what Pharaoh tried to take.

Rabbenu Bahya in Hovot HaLevavot asks why human beings stay dependent for so many years when most animals fend for themselves almost at once. Imagine coming out of the womb with the mind of a thirteen-year-old. You would see the sunrise, the moon and stars, a passion fruit full of seeds, and cry "wow." It would be so obvious there is a Creator that there would be no free choice. So HaShem gives us years in which we grow used to nature. The task of the thinking Jew, from bar or bat misvah, is to stop and ask: who is behind all this? That was Avraham, asking who owns the palace.

Similarity two: red wine and white massah

Throughout the Haggadah, whenever the cup is raised the massot are covered, and when it is put down they are uncovered. Why, all night long?

The Gemara says a child has three partners. The father gives the white, the bones and the brain; the mother gives the red, the flesh and blood; and HaShem gives the soul. Asked what they associate with red, people said danger, blood, warmth, passion, love; with white, purity, calm, clarity.

The Rabbi then made a generalisation he stressed was only general: when a child comes home upset, the mother's first reaction is often to comfort and the father's to solve the problem. In general, he suggested, emotion tends to come first for women and problem-solving for men. Red stands for emotion and passion; white for clarity and intellect.

He took this into Jewish history. Yaakov's two enemies were Esav, called Edom, red, who wanted to kill him, and Lavan, white, who tried to cheat him, changing his wages a hundred times. There has been "red" persecution, which wants to kill the Jew for who he is, like Haman, and "white" persecution, which wants to change our beliefs, like the Greeks or the Spanish Inquisition. That, he said, is the difference between Purim, celebrated with a feast because our bodies were saved, and Hanukkah, celebrated with lights, praise and learning because our faith was saved.

Pesah is the birth of the nation, from our fathers and mothers. Three white massot for the three Avot; four cups of red wine for the four Imahot. Judaism needs both intellect and emotion. He touched on the old rivalry between the Hasidim, who stressed loving prayer, and the Vilna Gaon, who stressed Torah study; both agreed both were needed. When we raise the wine, it is a time for emotion, like prayer, and the massah is covered. When we put it down and uncover the massah, it is a time to think, question and study.

Eleven customs compared

The Rabbi went through the Seder in order, inviting those present to add their own: 1. Shehiyanu at candle lighting: Ashkenazi women say it; Sefaradi women do not, relying on the Shehiyanu said later at kiddush. 2. Shalom Aleikhem on Friday night: most do not say it on Yom Tov; those following the Kabbalists, more the Iraqi and Syrian custom, do. 3. Women leaning: generally Sefaradi women lean and, to his knowledge, Ashkenazi women do not, unless their family custom is otherwise. 4. Karpas: Sefaradim use celery or parsley, often dipped in lemon juice or vinegar; Ashkenazim often potato or celery in salt water. The Shulhan Arukh simply says a vegetable. 5. Yahas: many Sefaradim, following the Kabbalists, break the middle massah into shapes like the letters dalet and vav. 6. The afikoman: the Ashkenazi custom of children hiding it is, he said, relatively recent. The Sefaradi custom is to carry it on the shoulder and answer: where are you coming from? Egypt. Where are you going? Jerusalem. What are your provisions? Matzah and maror. 7. Spring onions: an Iranian and Afghan custom of lightly whipping one another during Dayenu. 8. Berakhot on the cups: Ashkenazim bless each of the four; the Sefaradi custom is to bless only the first and third. 9. Maror and haroset: Sefaradim usually use lettuce and halek, a date-based haroset; Ashkenazim often horseradish and apple-and-nut haroset. 10. The egg: recalling the hagigah offering, eaten at different points. An egg is the only food that hardens when heated. So in exile, when the heat is on, the Jewish response is not to crumble but to stand firm and be proud. 11. The ten plagues: many Sefaradim pour out wine for each plague, and Kabbalistically it should be disposed of straight away.

Questions

  • When to start: kiddush and the massah only after nightfall. Someone elderly or unwell may begin earlier, from plag haminhah, but should still eat the massah after nightfall, since the massah is a Torah obligation and the heart of the night.
  • Am I still enslaved? Someone who came from a dictatorship to a free country asked why he must still feel he left Egypt. Even in the land of Israel, the Rabbi said, until the Temple is rebuilt and our relationship with HaShem returns with the Pesah offering, we are not fully free. Even at the Kotel we sing "next year in Jerusalem."

Summary

  • Asking questions on Seder night is itself the experience of freedom, even alone.
  • A human being's freedom is in the mind, which is what Pharaoh tried to take.
  • The red wine and white massah stand for emotion and intellect, our mothers and our fathers; Judaism needs both.
  • The customs of different communities differ in detail, but every table shares the same core.
