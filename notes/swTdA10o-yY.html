Topic: How Pharaoh could be punished if God hardened his heart: the Rambam, the Ramban and the Sforno, and a third approach Sources: Shemot 7:3; Rambam, Hilchot Teshuvah 5:3 and 6:3; Ramban and Sforno on Parashat Vaera; Berakhot 32a Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Torah Way, North West London, Thursday 14 January 2021 (Rosh Hodesh Shevat, Parashat Vaera)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Rosh Hodesh Shevat

The Rabbi opened by noting that people do not always remember how important Rosh Hodesh Shevat is. The Torah states explicitly that on the first day of the eleventh month Moshe began to speak to the people (Devarim 1:3). Over the following thirty-six days, until the seventh of Adar, he went over the whole Torah again, including the rebuke of Sefer Devarim. The Midrash compares the day to the day the Torah was given at Sinai: a time to strengthen ourselves in Torah.

He then turned to the parashah. "We've spoken about halacha things in the past." This time he took up what is probably the most famous question in the book: Pharaoh's free will.

Why free will matters

Reward and punishment is the eleventh of the Rambam's Thirteen Principles, and it depends entirely on free will. You would not reward your microwave for heating your food, or punish a robot programmed to harm you. Neither chose what it did. We feel grateful or upset with people because they chose to act. That is also why we do not take offence when someone who is mentally unwell insults us. He has no choice in the matter.

The Rambam calls free will "a great principle and the pillar of the Torah and the misvot" (Hilchot Teshuvah 5:3). So why is it not one of the thirteen? One answer is that it is included in the eleventh, since reward and punishment make no sense without it. Another explanation the Rabbi had seen is that the thirteen principles are our beliefs in Hashem, while free will is, as it were, a belief Hashem has in us. It is a fourteenth principle of a different kind: not our faith in God, but our faith that we are able to choose.

The problem in Parashat Vaera

At the start of the parashah Hashem says, "I will harden Pharaoh's heart" (Shemot 7:3). But that does not happen at once. In the first five plagues the Torah says that Pharaoh hardened his own heart. He chose not to send the people. In the last five it says that Hashem hardened his heart, in the causative form. There is a big difference between "he hardened" and "He hardened".

So the question is plain. If Hashem strengthened Pharaoh's heart, why was Pharaoh punished for refusing? It would be like punishing the robot.

The Rambam: you put yourself there

The Rambam (Hilchot Teshuvah 6:3) writes that a person can sin so greatly that, as a consequence, he is prevented from doing teshuvah. That is what happened to Pharaoh. He chose freely for the first five plagues. As a result, the door to teshuvah was closed, and in the last five he no longer had a choice.

The Rabbi pressed the obvious difficulty. Losing the ability to return may be a fair punishment for the first five refusals. But how can Pharaoh be punished for what he did during the last five, when he could not choose?

His explanation was addiction. Someone who starts smoking or drinking chooses freely the first time. Over time the substance can make him dependent. Imagine, he said, a person who keeps every misvah but is so addicted to tobacco that he cannot stop himself smoking on Shabbat. Assume, for the sake of argument, that he truly has no control any more. Is he someone who profanes Shabbat?

The answer is that you take responsibility for the outcome of your decisions, even in the long run. He may no longer have a choice now. But he put himself in that position, and he answers for it. "That's a huge hiddush. Pharaoh did not have behirah, but he's still punishable, because you put yourself in that situation."

A web of lies

He linked this to the English saying, "Oh, what a tangled web we weave, when first we practise to deceive", and to an explanation he had seen in the Maskil LeDavid, a supercommentary on Rashi. The Torah says, "Do not deny and do not lie" (Vayikra 19:11). Why both? Because once a person tells the first lie, he finds himself having to build a whole web of lies to protect it. Do not tell the first lie, and if you have, do not keep building the web.

The lesson, the Rabbi said, is a big one. Sometimes we find ourselves in situations where we really cannot control ourselves. The question then is why we put ourselves there in the first place. The laws of yihud work this way: do not enter a situation where you will later say you had no choice.

He added the Gemara in Berakhot (32a). After the golden calf, Moshe argued on behalf of the people: it is like a father who dresses his son well, perfumes him and puts money in his purse, then leaves him at the door of a place of temptation. What did you expect? You gave them all that gold. They had been put in a situation where they could no longer resist.

The Ramban and the Sforno: restoring the balance

The Ramban and the Sforno read it the other way. Pharaoh did have free will throughout. After five plagues, with the hand of Hashem so clear, he would have had no real choice but to let the people go. Once might be a fluke, twice a coincidence, but five times? He would have sent them out without any genuine test.

So Hashem strengthened his heart to recreate the balance, the level playing field, so that he could still choose either way. The Rabbi drew a principle from this: in any test, a person has the ability to choose good or bad, and Hashem ensures that balance. Something done without choice earns neither reward nor punishment. The purpose of life is to choose good when we are equally able to choose otherwise.

He pointed out the contrast. For the Rambam, Pharaoh could not choose to send them. For the Ramban, he could not have chosen to refuse.

A third approach: room for nature

A third explanation among the commentators is that "I will harden" does not mean Hashem changed Pharaoh's heart at all. Rather, with every plague Hashem left room to attribute it to nature, to call it a fluke. The hail was miraculous, yet it destroyed the flax and the barley and left the wheat and the spelt (Shemot 9:31-32). Pharaoh could wake up the next morning and tell himself it was just the season. Hashem left open a path for him to harden his own heart.

Asked whether this made it harder for Pharaoh, the Rabbi preferred to say that it left the door open. He still had to open his eyes and see.

Summary

  • Reward and punishment depend on free will, which the Rambam calls a pillar of the Torah.
  • The Rambam: after five refusals Pharaoh lost his choice, yet was punished because he brought himself to that point. Do not put yourself where you will have no choice.
  • The Ramban and the Sforno: Hashem hardened Pharaoh's heart to restore his free will after the overwhelming miracles.
  • A third view: Hashem left every plague open to a natural explanation, so Pharaoh could still harden his own heart.
