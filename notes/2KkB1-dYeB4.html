Topic: What truth and falsehood really mean, six kinds of falsehood, and the six cases where Hazal permit changing the facts Source: "Midvar sheker tirhak" (Shemot 23:7) and the sources in Hazal and the Rishonim Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a weekly home shiur on middot, the third evening after judging favourably and controlling anger (undated recording, 2015-16)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Distance yourself

"Midvar sheker tirhak": distance yourself from falsehood. Of all the 613 misvot, the Rabbi said, only here does the Torah not simply forbid, but tells us to keep far away. The Sefer HaHinukh explains that falsehood is abominable in everyone's eyes, and the Torah uses the word "distance" to show how disgusting it is. Society can only exist if people trust each other. And one of HaShem's attributes is emet, truth. Walking in His ways, becoming more godly, means being truthful.

What is truth?

The Rabbi asked a hard question. In the war, a non-Jew hiding a Jewish child is asked by a Nazi officer: is there a Jew here? Of course he says no. But is that a lie told for a good cause?

The answer he brought, from Rav Eliyahu Dessler, which the class read together in English, changes how we think of truth. What we learned at school, truth means stating the facts and falsehood means deviating from them, works only in simple cases. Truth is better defined as whatever is conducive to good and in line with the will of the Creator; falsehood as whatever serves evil. Saying "there is a Jew here" would be falsehood, though the fact is correct, because it brings evil. Saying "no" is not a lie told for a good cause. "That is the truth to say in your situation."

Someone rightly objected that this could be misused. Exactly, he said, which is why Hazal gave clear boundaries, which the class went through.

The Gemara in Sanhedrin tells of a town called Kushta, truth, where no one ever lied and no one died. A man who lived there once said his wife was out because she was washing her hair and he felt it immodest to say so. His two sons died, and the townspeople told him to leave. The Maharal says this is a parable: a world where both the message and the words are always true is not this world. Here, sometimes, changing the words is the truth.

Truth stands, falsehood does not

"Falsehood does not stand; truth stands." How do you know your dreams are not real? Because they are not continuous; the table is still here in the morning. Truth is what lasts. Lies usually get caught out.

The letters say the same. Emet, alef mem tav, takes the first, middle and last letters of the alphabet: true from beginning to end. Its letters each stand on two legs. Sheker, kuf resh shin, letters with one leg each, cannot stand. Beged, betrayal, bet gimel dalet, appears at the very start of the alphabet, as sheker appears near the end: falsehood shows up at the beginning or the end of a story, but has no inner core.

The seal of HaShem is emet. The Maharsha adds: knock the alef off emet, even with a small lie, and you are left with met, death. A society without trust can turn deadly.

Six kinds of falsehood

The Rabbi listed six categories brought by Rabbenu Yonah, from most to least severe: 1. Lying that causes another a loss: an employee calling in sick to take a day off, an employer saying the cheque is in the post when it is not. 2. Lying for one's own gain. Rav Moshe Feinstein writes about someone who cheats in an exam and gains a university place that should have gone to someone else; a career built on that degree is built on sheker. 3. Inventing stories that harm no one, just to entertain or be popular. 4. Offering something you never intend to do, like "I'll clean up later" with no intention of it. 5. Backing out of a promise. Saying you will give charity is a binding vow, which is why people add bli neder. 6. Pretending to do a favour, such as telling a friend "for you, a discount" when everything is on sale anyway, or accepting thanks for "coming to visit" when you were just passing.

Falsehood need not be spoken. Silence or appearance can lie: coming to court dressed as a police officer, or bringing a well-dressed friend to a house viewing to make the buyer think the property is in demand. A seller need not point out a defect the buyer can see, such as a crack in the wall, but must disclose a hidden one. On problems outside the house itself, he said he was not sure and would need to ask.

When it is permitted

Categories one and two are never permitted, short of a danger to life. The permissions apply only to changing facts in a way that harms no one, and only when there is no other way: 1. For a misvah, such as cheering up a sick person with a funny story, or motivating a child. He warned to check honestly that it is not the yeser hara speaking, and that children may learn that lying is acceptable, so one must explain to them when it is allowed. 2. For peace. Aharon would tell each side of a quarrel that the other wanted to make up. When HaShem told Avraham what Sarah said, He left out that she called Avraham old. Yoseph's brothers told him their father had asked him to forgive them. 3. To praise something already bought, or a bride and groom. If a purchase can be returned, honest advice is the kind thing. If it cannot, praise it. If someone will truly embarrass himself, tell him, kindly. Exaggeration that everyone understands, such as "the whole town came to the wedding," is not a lie. 4. Humility: you may say you have not finished Shas when you have. If someone needs to know, to ask you a question, tell them. If you can simply decline to answer, do so. 5. Modesty: not revealing private matters, such as a wife's pregnancy, when explaining a lateness. 6. To protect others from harm, for example not revealing that a generous host takes in guests, if he does not want to be overwhelmed by collectors.

He ended with a story of Rav Yishak Elhanan Spektor of Kovno. Two men in a nearby town, known for stirring up trouble, sent him a question their own rav had answered wrongly, hoping to embarrass their rav with his reply. Sensing the trap, he sent a letter with the same mistaken ruling, then a faster telegram: ignore my letter, it was a mistake, here is the correct ruling. The right halacha was given, and the local rav was not shamed, since even the great rav of Kovno had "made a mistake."

Practical summary

  • The Torah demands that we distance ourselves from falsehood, not only avoid it.
  • Truth means what is conducive to good; stating a fact that causes harm can be falsehood.
  • One may never lie to cause another a loss or to gain for oneself, short of danger to life.
  • Hazal permit changing the facts for a misvah, peace, praise of what cannot be changed, humility, modesty and protecting others, only when there is no other way.
  • Silence, dress and body language can also be a lie.
