Where we are

The Rabbi recalled the previous class: the prohibition of a pesua dakkah (a man whose reproductive organs are damaged) marrying into the congregation, the reasons of the Rishonim, and the parts of the injury: the gid (the organ), the beisim (the testicles) and the hutei beit hasemanim (the internal ducts). Prostate surgery is fine according to Rashi, and probably even according to the Rambam, because the ducts are more internal.

Today's focus was Seif 10. He said Seifim 2-9 are technical and he had little to add, so he went to Seif 10, which holds the main mahloket between an injury caused bidei adam (by a person) and one bidei shamayim (from heaven). The end of the siman, on causing the injury to oneself, to others or to animals, was left for later.

The Gemara: only a person's act makes a pesua dakkah

The Gemara in Yevamot says that a man with only one testicle has the status of a saris hamah and is kosher. A saris hamah has nothing to do with the sun, it is simply a man who is impotent from heaven. So already we see that a saris who is so bidei shamayim is permitted.

The Gemara then learns from the verse that the Torah does not say "a man who does not have testicles" but speaks of someone to whom something was done. The Torah places this next to the laws of the mamzer in Parashat Ki Tese. Just as a mamzer is created by a person's act, a pesua dakkah is created when a human being causes it, as opposed to bidei shamayim.

The big question is how to define bidei shamayim, and where the emphasis lies:

  • Is the emphasis on bidei shamayim, meaning every injury is a problem unless it is clearly from heaven? Or,
  • Is the emphasis on bidei adam, meaning only a human act makes a problem, and everything else is kosher?

The practical differences (nafka minot) come out of this.

The Rishonim

Rashi says two things. In one place, where the Gemara speaks of the Kerem BeYavneh, he calls an illness (holi) "from heaven". In the main sugya he defines bidei shamayim as "thunder and hail". The Rabbi said it is odd that Rashi here does not mention illness, and it looks as if he takes "from heaven" quite literally: it fell from the sky, or Hashem made him that way. The Rosh seems to have seen only this second Rashi.

The Rambam defines bidei adam as an injury caused by a person or by a dog, or by one's own act, such as sitting down on a thorn or splinter. Note that a dog is counted as bidei adam. Bidei shamayim is being born that way, or becoming ill: the organs stopped working, or boils crushed or cut them. The Rambam clearly places the emphasis on bidei shamayim: to be kosher, you have to prove it was from heaven, and "from heaven" is a limited category: born that way or illness.

The Rabbi mentioned an understanding of the Rambam that includes a case where an illness begins and a doctor or the man himself then cuts the organ. It is still bidei shamayim because the starting point was from heaven. Then we need to ask where in the body the illness must start. This is the prostate question.

The Rosh is stricter than the Rambam. For him an illness (holi) is not bidei shamayim. It has to be literally from heaven, or being born that way. The Rosh brings support from the plain meaning of Rashi and the wording of the Yerushalmi.

The Semag usually follows the Rambam. Here he quotes the Rambam but adds that it must be caused "through a person's pesiah" (negligence), and he leaves out the dog. The Rabbi noted a possible reading: the Semag puts the emphasis on bidei adam. A person who sits on a thorn did an act. A person mauled by a dog while completely passive did none, so perhaps it is not bidei adam. This is only an inference, and the Rambam does not say it.

The Shulhan Arukh and the Rema

The Shulhan Arukh seems to say that to be kosher it must be bidei shamayim, and then explains this with wording that the Rabbi said is not the usual Shulhan Arukh style but more like the Beit Yoseph: it brings the Rosh's proof from the Yerushalmi. Normally the Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam against the Rosh (he mentioned a claim that about 70 percent of the Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam), so this wording seems to say that here he follows the Rosh.

The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel disagree. They say the Shulhan Arukh did not mean this, because the plain meaning of Rashi is like the Rambam, and the Yerushalmi is not a clear proof for the Rosh. So the Rabbi's conclusion is as follows:

The halakhah in practice: Being born that way, an illness, or a blow from heaven are all bidei shamayim, and the man is kosher. All the later authorities rule that an illness counts, which is the Rambam's line. The rest of the discussion only matters because illness is included. If illness did not count, none of the cases below would arise.

A point the Rabbi raised himself: he does not think the Rambam would count hail as bidei shamayim. The Rambam understands "from heaven" as natural, from Hashem: born that way or illness. Hail or a falling rocket is more like the dog, a freak accident. He stressed this is his own thought and not a quoted source.

A mixture of illness and human action

The second part is the combination of bidei shamayim and bidei adam. The Rabbi went through the Pithei Teshuvah, which brings a number of similar teshuvot.

Human act first, illness after. The Pithei Teshuvah brings a teshuvah about a man whose member was tied tightly with a cord by children who were mocking him. This caused a swelling and a serious infection, and the doctors then operated. The ruling is that this is permitted. It began through people but it finished from heaven: the illness took over. The proof is the Rambam in Hilchot Deot, who says that most illnesses come from a person's own lack of care. Still the Rambam calls illness bidei shamayim, even though a person's negligence may have caused it. So even if a person or someone else began it, once it becomes illness it counts as from heaven.

Illness first, human act after. What about the other direction: an illness began, and then a person (a doctor) had to operate and cut the organs? The Pithei Teshuvah says this is also permitted. For example, a cancer in the reproductive organs: since the organs are damaged internally, they are considered as if they are no longer there (keillu einam), so taking them out is not damage.

Illness elsewhere in the body. The hardest case is the prostate, where the illness is not in the reproductive organs at all, but the operation then affects them. Here there are two views:

  • Rav Moshe Feinstein holds that the illness has to be in the reproductive organs themselves in order to allow the operation. If the illness is elsewhere, he is not satisfied.
  • The Sis Eliezer takes the other side, based on the Semag: as long as the injury did not come through a person's negligence, and came through an illness, it is sufficient.

The Rabbi explained that the Semag's approach is the opposite extreme to the Rambam's. For the Semag only an active or negligent act of a person makes a pesua dakkah, so an illness anywhere in the body, followed by a medical operation, is not a problem. A medical operation is not a pesiah, it is done to heal.

A summary of the Seif

The Rabbi summed up the Seif:

  1. The Gemara makes the distinction between bidei adam and bidei shamayim.
  2. Rashi reads bidei shamayim literally in the main sugya, though elsewhere he calls an illness "from heaven".
  3. The Rambam places the emphasis on bidei shamayim, and bidei shamayim is metaphorical: born that way, or illness.
  4. The Rosh does not accept illness. The Shulhan Arukh brings both views, but the Aharonim rule that illness counts.
  5. An illness that began through a human act is still fine, and so is an illness followed by an operation.

Questions from the class

Medication and chemicals. A student asked about a person who takes pills or chemicals (he mentioned puberty blockers, called chemical castration) that stop the organs developing or damage them internally. The Rabbi said the sources discuss only physical injury to the organ or the testicles and no more. Taking the point as a fair one, he said it could be a modern form of pesua dakkah, since we saw in the Pithei Teshuvah that an illness that rots the organs is treated as if they are not there. But he still thinks that if there is no physical act on the testicles, and the testicles are still there, even if they are useless, it is not a pesua dakkah. He said he would need to check this again.

Vasectomy. A student asked whether a vasectomy would count as a pesua dakkah, with the marriage consequences that follow (for example for a Kohen and a convert). The Rabbi did not remember the discussion and said they would look into how a vasectomy works medically and what the poskim say next time. He did not give a conclusion.

Practical summary

  • A pesua dakkah applies only when the injury was caused by a person. If it is bidei shamayim, the man is kosher.
  • The Rambam places the emphasis on proving it was from heaven. Bidei shamayim means born that way or illness.
  • The Rosh excludes illness, but the Shulhan Arukh is read by the Aharonim as ruling that illness counts.
  • An illness that began through a person's negligence, or an illness followed by an operation, is still treated as bidei shamayim.
  • If the illness is not in the reproductive organs (the prostate), the authorities differ: Rav Moshe is stricter, and the Sis Eliezer and others are lenient, following the Semag.
  • Chemical treatments and vasectomy are open questions that need further checking.
