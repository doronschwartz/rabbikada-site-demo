Topic: What is really judged on Rosh Hashanah: the scales, the beinoni, and the difference between who we are and what we have done Source: The Rambam, Hilchot Teshuvah, chapters 2-3; Gemara Kiddushin 40b and 49b; Minhat Hinukh, Misvah 364; Rosh Hashanah 16b Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Torah Way's "Start Your Day with Torah", North West London, Tuesday 15 September 2020 (26 Elul)

An idea that changes Rosh Hashanah

In his first Torah Way talk since the lockdown, a few days before Rosh Hashanah, the Rabbi shared an idea he first heard many years ago, which he said can change a person's whole perspective on the Yamim Noraim.

Three questions on the scales

The Rambam (Hilchot Teshuvah 3) describes the judgment as a weighing. If a person's misvot outweigh his sins he is sealed for life; if his sins outweigh them, for death; if they are equal he is a beinoni, left hanging until Yom Kippur. Children come home from school with pictures of scales. But there are three problems.

1. Nothing cancels out. Hashem "takes no bribe" (Devarim 10:17). The Ramban and the Meiri explain: a person cannot say, "I have a thousand misvot and a hundred sins, so cancel the hundred and reward me for nine hundred." Every misvah is rewarded and every sin is answered for. So what is there to weigh?

2. Who is exactly equal? Even if such things could be measured, how many people in the world would have exactly as many misvot as sins? You could count them on your fingers. Why set aside ten days for them?

3. What is special about teshuvah? The Gemara (Kiddushin 40b), brought by the Rambam, says that one who regrets a misvah he did loses it. If regretting a sin wipes it out, and regretting a misvah wipes it out too, why do we keep hearing what a great kindness teshuvah is? It seems to be the same system both ways.

A saddik without teshuvah

Teshuvah has stages: regret for the past, leaving the sin now, commitment for the future, and finally vidui, spoken confession. The Rambam is clear that without spoken vidui there is no atonement, however sincere the thoughts.

Yet the Gemara (Kiddushin 49b) rules that if a man betroths a woman "on condition that I am a saddik", she is betrothed even if he is known to be thoroughly wicked, because perhaps he had a thought of teshuvah in his heart. But we watched him a minute ago and he made no vidui, so he has no atonement. How can he be a saddik?

The Rabbi had wanted to call the talk "Can you be a saddik without doing teshuvah?" The answer is yes. The Minhat Hinukh (Misvah 364) explains that the name saddik or rasha has nothing to do with atonement. Misvot and sins are what we do. Saddik and rasha are who we are. A person can be full of sins that still need atonement, and yet in one moment change his identity and commit to a different path. At that moment he is a saddik, even though the sins remain on his back until he goes through the full process of teshuvah.

Rosh Hashanah judges who you are

This is the essence of Rosh Hashanah. We do not mention sins on Rosh Hashanah, because it is not about the details. It is the judgment of identity: are you a saddik or a rasha? Yom Kippur is the judgment of deeds. "We'll get into the details" on Yom Kippur, with vidui said again and again.

So the scales are not a count of misvot and sins. In Hebrew, kavod (honour) and kaved (heavy) are the same word. What is weighed is the weight, the value, we give to Torah and misvot in our lives. Someone can spend a fortune on a misvah, and he will be rewarded for it, but if it carries little weight for him, it weighs little on the scale. Someone else may stumble often, yet the sins are not who he is.

That answers the second question too. Very few people live consistently, wholly good or wholly bad. Most of us live with contradictions: we want to do good and fail. That is why most of us really are beinonim, and why we need the ten days to decide who we are.

Why teshuvah is unique

Back to the third question. If a person regrets having put on tefillin because it made him late for a meeting, no rav would tell him to put them on again. The act of the misvah cannot be taken away. What he loses by regretting it is the value he gave it, its weight on the scale.

Teshuvah is different. Regret over a sin does not only remove the importance he gave it. Through kapparah, which means cleansing, the sin itself is wiped away as if it had never been done. That is why teshuvah is so much greater than regretting a misvah.

The Gemara in Rosh Hashanah (16b) says a person is judged "as he is now", without looking at his future. The same applies to his past: he may be full of sins, but is he on the right path now?

Summary

  • Every misvah is rewarded and every sin answered for; nothing cancels out.
  • Saddik and rasha describe who a person is, not a tally of deeds, as the Minhat Hinukh explains.
  • A person can become a saddik in a moment of real change, even before atonement for his sins.
  • Rosh Hashanah judges our identity and values; Yom Kippur deals with the details of our deeds.
  • Teshuvah is unique because it erases the sin itself, while regretting a misvah only removes its value.
