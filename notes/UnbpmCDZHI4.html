Topic: The slave mentality at the Red Sea, inspiration that is given and taken away, and why a person is a tree Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Wednesday 1 February 2023, the week of Parashat Beshalah and Tu BiShvat

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Trapped at the sea

The Rabbi split the evening between the parashah and Tu BiShvat, promising that by the end the two would connect. It was an open discussion, with plenty of questions and answers from those present.

Seven days after leaving Egypt, Bnei Yisrael stand at the Red Sea. Pharaoh has changed his mind once more and is pursuing them. In front is the sea, behind them Egypt, and on either side, the Midrash adds, wild desert. "Put yourself in their shoes. What would you do?" Someone answered: fight. That, he said, is exactly the question. They do not fight; they cry to Moshe, "Were there no graves in Egypt?"

Yet they had every reason to fight. There were 600,000 men of fighting age, and the Torah says they left Egypt hamushim, armed. They had just seen the ten plagues. HaShem was clearly with them. Contrast the end of the same parashah: Amalek attacks, and this time Israel fights, with Moshe raising his hands.

The answer of Ibn Ezra

The Rabbi brought the explanation of Rabbi Avraham ibn Ezra, the twelfth-century Spanish commentator. They had the physical ability to fight, but not the psychological ability. After 210 years of slavery, the sense that "these are my masters" was almost inborn, handed down from parents and grandparents. They could not break out of it.

That is why Moshe raised his hands at the battle with Amalek. It was like a coach on the touchline: he is not playing, but he gives the team its confidence. And that, says Ibn Ezra, is why the generation that left Egypt died in the desert. Entering the land meant fighting thirty-one kings, and only a generation that had never known slavery could do it. Moshe, raised in Pharaoh's palace with confidence and abundance, could look upwards; the people could not.

He quoted Wittgenstein: the purpose of philosophy is "to show the fly the way out of the bottle." The fly bangs against the sides and never looks up. What Ibn Ezra taught 800 years ago, we now take for granted: psychological health matters as much as physical health, and above all we must instil belief in our children.

Inspiration that is taken away

A second idea. Before the sea, HaShem seems to be everywhere: blood, frogs, the sea splitting. Three days after crossing, there is no water, and they complain. Then no food, then no water again, then Amalek. It is as if, once they crossed, HaShem said: now look after yourselves.

The Rabbi called the first stage "free inspiration," something you receive without working for it, which does not last. His examples: - A child learning to walk: the parent holds him, then lets go, and he falls, again and again, until he walks by himself. The holding showed him he could do it. - The Torah learned in the womb: the Midrash says every child learns the whole Torah before birth and forgets it. The point is to know that you can reach it, and then work to get there. - Romance and love: there is no old Hebrew word for romance, and the burst of chemistry fades. The word for love, ahavah, comes from hav, to give. We love most those we give to most, which is why we love our children so much. - Pesah to Shavuot: Seder night brings great spiritual energy for free. Then it is taken away, and we have forty-nine days to earn it back.

Why does HaShem work this way? The Rabbi gave a short answer, while saying the full subject needs its own shiur: HaShem created the world to give us goodness, and the ultimate goodness is in the World to Come. But what you are given is not yours in the same way as what you earn. We are meant to make goodness part of ourselves, as it is intrinsic to God. A Porsche you worked for is not the same as one you inherited.

Rabbi Sacks, he noted, pointed out that the complaining stops once the building of the Mishkan begins. The people give so much that Moshe has to announce: stop giving. A bored child is not cured by one treat after another, but by a project of his own. Someone asked what had changed. Resilience, he said, and an objective of their own. Another asked whether Adam in Eden followed the same pattern. Different, he answered: Adam was not meant to leave, and even in Eden he had a test.

Tu BiShvat: a person is a tree

What is a new year for trees? Originally it is a halachic date, like the start of a tax year, marking which year's fruit is tithed. Over time it has become a much more spiritual day.

The Torah says "a person is a tree of the field." Why trees and not vegetables? Before the sin, Adam ate only from the trees. After it, the ground was cursed and he had to eat from the earth. The difference: pick a fruit and the tree remains, with seeds for more. Eat a potato and that is all there is.

But a person is also called Adam, from adamah, the ground, though animals too were made from the ground. A clod of earth has nothing in it, until you plant a seed and it grows something beautiful. An animal, behemah, is bah mah, "what is in it is all there is." A human being is a patch of earth with the potential to become a tree. Our body pulls downwards, like every animal; our neshamah aspires upwards. That is the battle of life.

Tu BiShvat celebrates that potential, especially in our children. "Every child is a piece of earth." Nurtured and watered, it grows into a tree; neglected, it stays as earth. The saddik is like a palm tree, standing straight and looking upwards. Someone asked whether this is the purpose of life. Yes, he said; every festival reminds us of it in its own way.

Summary

  • Israel could not fight at the sea because the slavery was in their minds, not their arms.
  • Confidence is given to us, as Moshe gave it at Amalek, and we must give it to our children.
  • HaShem often gives a taste of greatness for free and then takes it away, so that we earn it ourselves.
  • Tu BiShvat reminds us that each person, and each child, is earth that can grow into a tree.
  • The fly in the bottle gets out only by looking up.
