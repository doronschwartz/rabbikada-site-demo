Where we are

This class finished Siman 4. The Rabbi recalled last time: the shtuki and the asufi, the big mahloket between the Rambam and Rashi about the asufi in a city with a majority of Israelites, and whether he needs conversion. Today's three seifim are 35, 36 and 37. Seif 35 is the main one; the other two are more straightforward.

Seif 35: the midwife is believed

The case. Several women gave birth in one house, and the babies were mixed up. The Shulhan Arukh says the midwife (hayah) is believed to say whose child is whose. There is no hazakah (established status) on the child, so we rely on her word. But if one person comes and says she is lying, she is no longer believed, and the child is kosher but has no yihus.

The Gemara (Kiddushin 73). The Gemara teaches that the midwife is believed, and then asks the question that drives the whole seif: how can a single person's challenge (ira) override her? We normally need two.

First question: who is the "error" about? The Rabbi asked the class to think about this before reading the commentators. - Rashi reads the ira as a challenge to the child's status (the "blood"), not to the midwife. - The Rambam reads it as a challenge to the midwife.

Second question: when can the challenge come? According to Rashi, the Gemara speaks only where the ira came out before the midwife testified. Once she has testified, the child has a hazakah and a later challenge does not help (the Prishah points to Rashi's past tense). According to the Rambam, the challenge works before or after. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam.

How each side answers the Gemara's question. - Rashi: the child has no hazakah, so one challenger is enough. - The Rambam: the midwife herself is believed, but she is testifying about something with no hazakah (the child), so a single challenge is accepted.

The Ran and the Rambam: why can one challenger cancel her?

The Ran raises a difficulty. Whenever the Torah believes one person, it counts as two witnesses. So why can one challenger undo the midwife?

The Ran. At root the midwife is not believed by Torah law. Hazal allowed it only because otherwise we could not run a community (din derabbanan). Because it is a rabbinic arrangement, even weak opposition cancels it. The Ran stresses that the principle "one believed person counts as two" applies where the matter can later be reversed, such as an agunah case where a husband might return. Here we would never find out the child's true status, so Hazal gave her belief and it can be undone easily.

The Rambam. The midwife is believed by Torah law, as two pesulim (like two women). The Torah's "two" is of the weaker kind, so it can be overridden by one kosher witness, a Jewish man, but not by another woman or a non-kosher witness. This is why the Rabbi said the word Adam in the Shulhan Arukh must mean a kosher witness, a Jewish man.

What happens to the children?

The Rabbi gave an example: four babies in one house. The Shulhan Arukh ends: "the child is in a state of kashrut and has no yihus." There are two ways to read that, and he followed the Beit Shemuel: - Not all four children become kosher. The Shulhan Arukh speaks of the one child whose status the midwife had called into question. - A kosher witness who challenges the midwife does not only cancel her testimony. He can give that child a hezkat kashrut, since the matter is only a safek mamzer, and in a safek about mamzerut one kosher witness is believed. - The other three children remain safek mamzerim. The Helkat Mehokek agrees. The child declared kosher is a kosher Israelite, but with no yihus for Kehunah.

The Rabbi added a contrast with the firstborn (bekhor): the midwife is believed about the bekhor only immediately, while here her belief works later too. The summary he gave: the Rashi/Rambam difference is who the error is about, when it can happen, and what kind of belief the midwife has.

Seif 36: who a shtuki or asufi may marry

A shtuki or an asufi may not marry a shtuki, an asufi, or a safek mamzer, since each might be a mamzer and the other might be a kosher Israelite. If they marry, they must divorce, and any child would be a safek. The only people they may marry are converts (ger or giyoret).

Why not a shifhah? A vadai mamzer may marry a shifhah so that his children are kosher. The Rabbi reminded the class of the two reasons: - The Rambam: marrying a shifhah is only a rabbinic prohibition, which Hazal waived to repair the children. - The Ran and others: it is a Torah prohibition (lo yihyeh kedeshah), meaning "someone who is still to be set aside" (kadesh leomed), so a mamzer, already set aside, is not affected.

The Shulhan Arukh does not mention a shifhah for a safek mamzer. The Rabbi walked through the logic: on the Ran's view, the Torah exception covers only a definite mamzer, which explains the Shulhan Arukh. On the Rambam's view, it would seem a safek mamzer could also marry one. The Pithei Teshuvah brings the Radbaz, who relies on the Rambam and says a safek mamzer may marry a shifhah, but he said the plain wording of the Shulhan Arukh does not go that way.

He briefly mentioned a Kuti (Samaritan) who converts, and a Natin, and said we do not need them today.

Seif 37: a non-Jew in the town

This seif follows the Rambam's view from last week. If there is even one non-Jew in the town, an asufi there has the status of a safek goy. If he marries a convert (which he should not do), she is a safek eshet ish, and if a Jewish man has relations with her he is not put to death. The "error" of Yisrael does not help his yihus, and he is treated as a safek goy until he converts properly.

A shtuki who marries. The Shulhan Arukh says a shtuki who marries a woman who might be forbidden to him (for example a sister born around his birth, or a divorced woman who might have been his father's wife) does not give a status of eshet ish that carries death. The Rabbi raised a good question: if the shtuki was checked with his mother and found kosher, does this still apply? He noted the Beit Shemuel says that if he was checked, he is permitted and we do not worry about forbidden relatives. A student's suggestion that the worry is only local was left open.

Karaites, Anusim and the groups of today

The Rabbi ended with the Karaites, quoting Rabbenu Shimshon, as brought in the Rema: do not marry them, they are safek mamzerim, and we do not accept them back. - The reason is that they do kiddushin but not a proper get, so their marriages are valid but their divorces are not. - This contrasts with the Anusim (Marranos), who did no kiddushin at all, so there is no mamzerut and we welcome them back. He noted this is one of the proofs against a view discussed earlier about civil marriage.

The Radbaz asks the obvious question: Karaites do not keep halacha, so how is there valid kiddushin at all? Then there would be no mamzerim. His answer: in Rabbenu Shimshon's time the Karaites still relied on the Jewish community and used Jewish witnesses. The Rabbi said this means that today, someone from these groups who wants to join us, including the Samaritans, can be accepted, with a proper conversion, and they would not be mamzerim. He compared it to how some non-Orthodox movements today still rely on Orthodox infrastructure.

Practical summary

  • A midwife (hayah) is believed about who a child is, but her word can be overturned.
  • The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam: the challenge is to the midwife, and it works before or after she speaks.
  • Only a kosher witness, a Jewish man, can overturn her, according to the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh. The Ran would accept weaker opposition.
  • The child she called into question becomes a kosher Israelite with no yihus; the other children remain safek mamzerim.
  • A shtuki or asufi may marry only a convert, and the question of a shifhah for a safek mamzer depends on the Rambam/Ran dispute.
  • Karaites were treated as safek mamzerim in the Rishonim's time, but the Radbaz explains why this need not apply to groups that want to join the community today.
