Topic: The two judgments of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, and what "life" means in our prayers Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 24 August 2021, two weeks before Rosh Hashanah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The recording begins after the opening part of the shiur, in which the Rabbi had spoken about serving Hashem rather than using misvot to manipulate Him, and had asked how we may request life in the first blessings of the Amidah.

Three books, and a contradiction

The Gemara in Rosh Hashanah (16b) says three books are opened on Rosh Hashanah. The completely righteous are immediately written and sealed for life. The completely wicked are immediately written and sealed for death. Those in between wait until Yom Kippur: if they merit, they are written for life; if not, for death. On this Gemara, writing and sealing happen together, on one day or the other.

But the page before says that all of humanity is judged on Rosh Hashanah and the verdict is sealed on Yom Kippur. There is no distinction between types of people, and writing and sealing are separate. The prayer Unetaneh Tokef says the same: "On Rosh Hashanah they are written and on Yom Kippur they are sealed." The Rabbi told its traditional origin: Rabbi Amnon of Mainz, summoned to convert, asked for three days to answer, regretted even that hesitation, refused, and was mutilated. He composed the prayer as he was dying, just before Rosh Hashanah.

"Let's be brutally frank"

The bigger problem is that the first Gemara does not match reality. Every year righteous people die and wicked people live. Tosafot goes further: it is not even meant to match reality, since sometimes the righteous suffer in this world as atonement and the wicked prosper so as to lose their share in the next. Many answers have been given; the Rabbi took Tosafot's own. The life and death in this Gemara refer to hayei haolam haba, the life of the world to come.

That seems strange. Why would there be a yearly verdict on the world to come? The Gemara tells of people who changed their whole life at the very end and acquired their world to come "in one moment." Our share is surely decided by our whole life, when we die.

Still, Tosafot shows that two systems are at work. One is our physical life: health, wealth, children. For that, all humanity is written on Rosh Hashanah and sealed on Yom Kippur. The other is hayei haolam haba, and there the righteous, wicked and in-between are treated differently. So what is it?

The beam of light

The Ramhal, in Derech Hashem, writes that the root of all service is for a person to turn to his Creator. The source of all a person's ups and downs is whether Hashem is shining His light upon him or hiding it. The more light, the more completeness and happiness.

The Rabbi explained this as hearat panim and hester panim. We have all had moments of feeling the divine: on a mountain on holiday, at Neilah, on a Shabbat, the sense that someone cares for me. Most of life is mundane, and then we can sink into darkness, what today might be called depression, a low where we do not feel meaning or connection. The kabbalists call these yemei gadlut and yemei katnut. Everyone has both.

Birkat Kohanim expresses this. "May Hashem bless you" is material blessing, says Rashi, and "may Hashem make His face shine upon you" is a smiling, beaming face. The Kohen, who taught Torah and served in the Mikdash, felt that illumination most, and blessed the people to feel it too.

How do we get it? The Ramhal continues: Hashem is always shining on those who come close; nothing stops Him. The question is whether we step into the light. The Rabbi's image was the opening of each Mr Bean episode: a single pillar of light in the dark. That is Hashem's light. Step into it through misvot and the desire to connect, and you feel the divine in your life. It never goes out. It is up to us.

Hayei olam haba in this world

Now Tosafot makes sense. Our place in the world to come is decided when we die. But the world to come is not an immature picture of physical pleasures. It is closeness to Hashem. His analogy: invited to tea with the Queen, you would spend months, even years, refining how you eat, speak and dress. The more refined, the less of a barrier and the more time she can spend with you. So too, the more we refine ourselves, the more we can bask in Hashem's presence. That is deveikut.

In this life we also have moments of that elation. That is what Tosafot calls hayei haolam haba, and it is judged each year: how much will you feel it this year? The righteous are written and sealed on Rosh Hashanah for a year full of it. The wicked may have health and wealth, but are written for a year with very little of it. Those in between are decided on Yom Kippur.

Spot the difference in the Amidah

During the Ten Days we add four lines. "Remember us for life," and "who remembers His creatures for life": life, unqualified. Then towards the end, "write all the children of Your covenant for a good life," and "in the book of life, blessing and peace." Why qualify it only later?

The Ramhal says that when life is mentioned without qualification it means real life: spiritual, meaningful, connected to Hashem. When it has to be qualified, it means physical life. So the first two requests, even in the first blessings where we do not make requests, ask for a relationship with Hashem, which fits the theme of the Avot. Only afterwards do we ask for a good life: health, livelihood, children.

Serving in order to serve

This brought him back to the beginning. If we use misvot to get things, a mezuzah for luck, tefillin for a good day, we are serving ourselves and not stepping into the light. When we serve in order to serve, "remember us for life" means: I want a year in which I feel You when I pray, learn, do misvot and help others.

The Zohar, he said, compares those who spend Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur crying only for children, health and livelihood to dogs barking at their master for food. "I'm very passionate about this," he said, because people turn these days on their head. We must think about our physical needs, but also about the bigger picture. The title of the shiur asked what life is and where it is. Life is physical, and even more it is spiritual. And it is here, in this world, each year.

After the class, someone said that this was hard, because we grow up with the idea that if you do good you get good. The Rabbi agreed that is true, "but we don't serve for that."

Summary

  • Two Gemaras disagree about when we are written and sealed; Tosafot reveals two separate judgments.
  • One judgment covers physical life; the other covers hayei haolam haba, how much we will feel Hashem's light this year.
  • The Ramhal: Hashem's light always shines; we choose whether to step into it.
  • Unqualified "life" in our prayers means spiritual life; only later do we ask for a good physical life.
  • Serve in order to serve, and do not let Rosh Hashanah become only a list of personal requests.
