Topic: The talmid hakham, the dayan and the Gadol HaDor, and what Emunat Hakhamim really means Shiur: Part 3 of a four-part series given by Dayan Daniel Kada in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 30 May 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why we need a definition

After a break for Shavuot, the Rabbi reviewed the series so far. According to the Rambam, Lo Tasur applies only to the Beit Din HaGadol. According to the Sefer HaHinukh, it applies to the hakhamim of every generation, and possibly even to the advice they give outside strict halacha.

But even the Hinukh speaks of the great hakham of the generation. "Yiftah in his generation is like Shemuel in his generation": you may have to listen to a lesser leader, but he must still be a hakham. Nobody says you must obey someone simply because he was elected or appointed. So before asking whether we must listen to a Gadol, we must know what a hakham is. "The word rabbi is overused nowadays," the Rabbi said. You can have semikhah on the whole Shulhan Arukh and still lack the qualities we are about to see.

He added a reminder from the previous week. A true talmid hakham may disagree with those before him, even the Rambam, since the Rambam's authority rests on acceptance and not every community accepted him. The Shulhan Arukh (Hoshen Mishpat 25) and the Vilna Gaon draw the line at the Gemara.

Level one: the talmid hakham

The Shulhan Arukh discusses the talmid hakham in the context of exemption from communal taxes. The Rabbi noted that in Israel those in full-time study still pay reduced council tax, based on this law. Not everyone can claim it: - Torah must be his main occupation. Working a few hours a day to pay the bills is right and proper. He told the well-known story of the Hafes Hayim, who kept a shop open only until he had earned what he needed, then closed it and returned to learning. - The Rema adds that he need not hold any position. - He must know how to "give and take" in learning, and know most of the Talmud and its commentaries.

Today, he said, it is easy to look up answers online, but you can test a talmid hakham on Shabbat: does he know his way around most of the sugyot of the Shas and most of halacha? Semikhah exams cover a few big areas, such as Shabbat, Niddah and Kashrut, and in theory someone could pass them knowing little else.

Knowledge is not enough. The Gemara teaches: "If the teacher is like an angel of Hashem, seek Torah from his mouth; if not, do not." A person with serious faults of character, however learned, is not a talmid hakham in this sense. The Shulhan Arukh also rules that one who does not follow the right path, though everyone needs his answers, should not be learned from until he repents.

Level two: the dayan

The Rambam lists seven qualities a dayan must have, all drawn from the verses where Moshe and Yitro appoint judges: - Wisdom - Humility - Fear of Heaven - Hatred of money - Love of truth - Being loved by people - A good name

The Rabbi explained several. Being liked by people comes from seeing the good in others and speaking kindly. A man of stature cares about the poor and the oppressed, not only the wealthy, as Moshe cared for Yitro's daughters at the well. Hating money does not mean neglecting your family; it means not chasing more and more once your needs are met. "These are tough, tough things," he said.

Level three: the Gadol HaDor

The Rambam writes that a later Beit Din can annul an earlier one's decree only if it is "greater in wisdom and in number". But every Beit Din HaGadol has seventy-one members. What does "number" mean?

The Rabbi explained it as popular support. The Hakhamim decreed against non-Jewish wine and also against non-Jewish oil, but the people could not keep the decree on oil, and it was rescinded. The same happened with the requirement of tevilah for a baal keri. The Gemara also says: "Leave Israel be; if they are not prophets, they are the children of prophets." Popular acceptance matters.

But he added two cautions. A crowd can follow the wrong person, and there have been celebrated figures who did bad things. And your own circle, your school or yeshivah, may be a narrow window on the Jewish people. Today, he said, it is hard to name any living rabbi whom every community would accept as the Gadol HaDor.

Rav Moshe Feinstein is an example of how acceptance comes. In his introduction to Iggerot Moshe he says that people asked him questions, he wrote answers, people asked about the answers, and so the volumes grew. He became accepted across the board. One rabbi of his time attacked him in a pamphlet, rudely, over a ruling on a new medical question. History has forgotten the critic. "Even if you disagree, disagree respectfully."

The eyes of the community

The Mishnah in Avot says, "Make for yourself a rabbi." The Rabbi stressed that your own rav, who knows you, your history and your circumstances, is the one to ask. It is wrong to take personal questions to great rabbis who do not know you and expect an answer. A public proclamation by a Gadol is something else.

Popular support alone is not enough. He read from a letter by Rav Eliezer Melamed, whom he regards as a true hakham of the generation. Rav Melamed writes that he respects the great talmidei hakhamim of other communities, but that greatness in Torah also requires "an all-embracing, fully accountable handling of the serious issues facing the generation", including the attitude to all of Am Yisrael in its diversity, and to the social and economic questions of the day. It is not decided by erudition alone, but by "comprehension and penetration to the roots of the matter".

The Rabbi added his own point. In Vayikra 4 the Sanhedrin are called "the eyes of the community". You can be the most learned, most pious person, studying in a corner day and night, and still not be a Gadol HaDor, because a Gadol must see the needs of the whole generation, not only his own circle. And knowledge must be deep: knowing every book by heart is not the same as grasping the reasons behind things and extending them to new questions.

Someone asked about Moshe's words to Yitro in Behaalotekha, "you will be eyes for us". That is a different context, he said, but the idea is the same: a leader is an example the people look to. Another asked whether community rabbis need people skills. Practically, yes, he said; a talmid hakham in the strict sense does not, but a community rabbi needs much more than knowledge.

Two warnings

  • Don't be dazzled. Brilliance, eloquence and instant answers are impressive but not sufficient. One lapse can be judged favourably, but if there is a recurring pattern of basic breaches, question whether this is someone to learn from.
  • Stay out of disputes you cannot assess. To take sides in a bitter dispute between two great hakhamim, you would first need to master the works of both. If you cannot, stay out of it.

Emunat Hakhamim

Faith in the sages is one of the forty-eight ways the Torah is acquired (Avot 6). The Mahzor Vitry, from a student of Rashi, explains it simply: believing in the Oral Torah, against the Sadducees. The Tiferet Yisrael writes that it means paying close attention to what the sages say, not accepting it blindly; "only a fool believes every word". Another of the forty-eight ways is pilpul haverim, debate among students.

A modern authority he quoted puts the two together. Without faith in their words, you skim and dismiss them. With simplistic faith, you never think. True faith means trusting that there is wisdom in their words, and so probing, asking respectfully and challenging until you understand.

Summary

  • A talmid hakham knows most of the Talmud and halacha, has good character, and keeps the Torah himself.
  • A dayan needs the Rambam's seven qualities, of which knowledge is only one.
  • A Gadol HaDor needs popular support, depth of understanding, and a view of the whole of Am Yisrael: "the eyes of the community".
  • Ask your own rav personal questions; do not join disputes you cannot assess.
  • Emunat Hakhamim means trusting their wisdom enough to work hard to understand it. Next week: Ruah HaKodesh and Daat Torah.
