These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Starting the commentary

The class had spent the previous months on the Shemonah Perakim, the eight chapters the Rambam wrote as his introduction to Pirkei Avot, because, as he explains, Avot is easy to read but hard to put into practice. Now the Rabbi began the commentary itself, going through every Mishnah of the first chapter and reading a few longer pieces inside.

Mishnah 1. Moshe received the Torah from Sinai and passed it down to the Men of the Great Assembly, who said three things: be patient in judgement, raise many students, and make a fence for the Torah. The Rambam says the purpose of his commentary is to encourage us to acquire perfect character and keep away from bad traits. Patience in judgement matters because someone in a hurry will not grasp the full context of a case. The "fence for the Torah" includes every rabbinic enactment.

Mishnah 2. Shimon HaSaddik said the world stands on three things: Torah, avodah and gemilut hasadim. The Rabbi asked the class to think back to the eight chapters. The Rambam reads them in exactly those terms:

  • Torah is intellectual perfection.
  • Gemilut hasadim is perfection of character.
  • Avodah, service, is the misvot: putting what we have learned and what we have become into practice in daily life.

Sakhar and peras: Antigonus and the sects

Antigonus of Sokho taught: do not be like servants who serve their master in order to receive a reward. The Rambam distinguishes two Hebrew words for reward:

  • Sakhar is what you are owed. You did a job, you agreed a fee, and you are paid.
  • Peras is a prize. Nothing was agreed, but you do something for someone half expecting that he will give you something back.

The Mishnah tells us not to serve HaShem even for a peras, but out of love. Yet it immediately adds that we need fear of heaven too. Love makes a person want to do what HaShem commanded; fear keeps him away from what HaShem forbade. The combination makes the complete person.

The Rambam then tells how sects arose from this Mishnah. Students of Antigonus, Sadok and Baitos, heard "do not serve for a reward" and concluded there was no reward at all, in this world or the next, and so no Torah. When that won few followers, they changed tack: "of course we keep the Torah, but who says the Hakhamim interpret it correctly?" These were the Sadducees and Boethusians. In the Rambam's own time, he says, they were replaced by the Karaites, whose name comes from likro, to read: they follow only what is written.

An open house, and employing the poor

Mishnah 4. Let your house be a meeting place for the hakhamim, sit in the dust of their feet and drink their words with thirst. The Rambam adds that your house should be known as a place where anyone who wants to talk Torah can come.

Mishnah 5. Let your house be open wide, and let the poor be members of your household. The Rambam reads the second half practically: rather than buying servants, employ poor people who need a livelihood. The sages, he says, praised a man whose household staff were the poor. The Rabbi drew a general principle: you give a job to the best person, but where you have a real choice, give it to the one who needs it more.

The same Mishnah warns against excessive conversation with women, and the Rambam explains that most such conversation in his day was about forbidden relationships. The Rabbi said this must be understood in its time. The words of the Mishnah stand forever, but today men and women work together and relate in many ways, and he doubted that the Rambam's description would be accurate now, so perhaps there is less stress on this today.

Make a teacher, acquire a friend

Mishnah 6. Yehoshua ben Perahyah said: make for yourself a teacher, acquire for yourself a friend, and judge every person favourably.

"Make" a teacher. The Rambam says make: even if the person does not know more than you or is not greater than you, find someone to learn from. Learning alone cannot compare with learning from another person. Even the greatest rav has his own rav.

"Acquire" a friend. Why acquire? Because you must be willing to invest, even to pay, to have someone you can talk things through with. The Rabbi compared it to paying a therapist when no friend is available. Going through life alone "is like death"; someone who thinks he can manage everything by himself will end up not living the right life. He added advice he loves: do not look for friends who are like you, but bend yourself to others. Manipulative people shape relationships around themselves; true friends each think how to fit around the other.

Three kinds of friendship. The Rambam lists:

  1. A friendship for benefit, like two business partners who work together to make money.
  2. A friendship for pleasure, of two kinds: the pleasure of love, as between husband and wife, and the friendship of trust, someone you can always rely on.
  3. A friendship for virtue, where two people come together to help each other reach perfection. This is the friend the Mishnah means.

The Rabbi found it striking that the Rambam places the ordinary love of husband and wife below this third friendship, though a husband and wife can also reach it, as can any two people. "To find someone who you actually bond with and your souls come together, it's a beautiful thing to have in life." It takes time and effort, which is why the Mishnah says acquire.

Judging favourably, and the students of Aharon

The Rambam gives the halachic framework for judging others favourably:

The person

When the act looks wrong

Known to be righteous

Judge favourably, even if the wrong reading is more likely

An ordinary person (beinoni)

Judge favourably when it is evenly balanced; not required when wrongdoing is more likely

Known for wrongdoing

No duty to give the benefit of the doubt

The Rabbi's example: a rav known never to talk during tefillah is seen talking during the repetition. Judge him favourably: something pressing must have come up. A congregant who talks every week does not get the same benefit. And if the rav starts doing it every week, he loses his presumption. He also stressed that benefit of the doubt applies only where there is no evidence. When a person of high office is found to have done something terrible and there is a mountain of evidence, invoking this Mishnah is wrong.

Mishnah 12. Hillel said: be among the students of Aharon, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving people and bringing them close to Torah. The Rambam describes Aharon befriending a person who was not behaving well. That person would think: if Aharon knew what I really do, he would not look at me; let me improve before he finds out. And so he became one of Aharon's students. The Rabbi applied it to children and students: when we project failure on people they become failures, but love and affection, even when undeserved, make them want to change.

Silence, and the five kinds of speech

Mishnah 17. Shimon, son of Rabban Gamliel: "All my days I grew up among the sages and found nothing better for a person than silence." The Rabbi called this one of the Rambam's longest and most famous pieces on Avot. Whoever speaks too much will inevitably say something wrong, since most of what people say is unnecessary. A wise person is known by speaking little, and "the voice of a fool comes with many words."

The Rambam quotes a sage from a book of ethics who divided speech into four kinds: wholly bad, such as cursing and vulgarity; good in itself but harmful in context, such as praising someone in front of his enemies; neither good nor bad, such as how someone made his money or how a palace was built; and beneficial, about wisdom and virtue. That sage spoke only in the fourth category. The Rambam refines this into five categories: commanded (misvah), forbidden (nizhar), repugnant (nimas), desirable (ahuv) and permitted (mutar). The Rabbi suggested trying it over the next few hours and seeing where most of our speech falls. "It's fascinating, I've tried this."

He had just explained the first category, the study of Torah, when the recording ends.

Summary

  • The Rambam reads Torah, avodah and gemilut hasadim as intellectual perfection, the misvot, and perfection of character.
  • Serve HaShem without expecting even a peras, with both love and fear; the sects grew out of misreading this teaching.
  • "Make" a teacher even if he knows less than you; "acquire" a friend even at a cost, and aim for the friendship of virtue.
  • Judge favourably according to a person's reputation, but never against clear evidence.
  • Most speech is unnecessary; the Rambam sorts speech into five categories.
