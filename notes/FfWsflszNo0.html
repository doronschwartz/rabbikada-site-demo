Know what is required

The Hida writes that on Pesah the Jewish people are strict about many things that are not necessary, which is praiseworthy. But "the more I teach, the more I realise how many misconceptions there are." We must know what is from the Torah, what is from the Hakhamim, what is custom and what is a stringency. A stringency that leaves someone exhausted for the Seder, with no energy for the Haggadah, "ends up with a kulah."

What the Torah forbids

Eating a kazayit of hames on Pesah carries karet; eating even a crumb is still a Torah prohibition, since half a measure is forbidden. Owning a kazayit of hames transgresses two Torah prohibitions.

But from the Torah it is enough to relinquish ownership sincerely, as we do in Kol Hamira. Thousands of pounds of whiskey could then stay in the house. Sincerity is the test. If burglars took your whiskey over Pesah and you claimed on the insurance, you never truly gave it up. So the Hakhamim required us to remove hames, because we may not give it up sincerely, and because we may come to eat it. They added the search as the first step towards removal.

Pieces, not crumbs, and where to look

Since searching and removing are rabbinic, the authorities' dispute over whether crumbs are included is a doubt in a rabbinic matter. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul and many others rule that we search only for a kazayit, a real piece. The Rabbi gave figures from 27 grams down to about 16 or 17, but stressed the point: "You're looking for a piece of hames. You're not looking for crumbs." The exception is where food is prepared or eaten: there even a crumb matters, because of eating.

The Shulhan Arukh says to search only where hames is brought during the year. His example is a wine store. So:

  • Sheds, toilets, bathrooms and the attic need no search if hames never goes there; nor do bedrooms if you strictly keep food out. No moving beds.
  • The garden needs a glance if you recently ate there, such as last week's Shabbat lunch outside. It "takes five minutes".
  • With young children who do not follow instructions, check wherever they can reach, but not high cupboards.
  • The car needs checking, unless you never eat in it.
  • Pockets: the Rema mentions checking them, but only for those who put food in their pockets, such as cakes taken home from a brit.

Not only on the night: before, during and after

If the search was missed at night, do it the next day. If you were ill, do it during Pesah, even on the last day.

A riddle: can there be a bedikat hames on Mosei Pesah? Yes. The Shulhan Arukh rules that hames owned over Pesah is forbidden even for benefit for ever. So someone who never searched must still search after Pesah, so as not to use such hames. Even a forgotten bottle found under the sofa after Pesah must be poured away. "It shows you how important it is to check for hames properly."

Travelling before Pesah. "People don't know this." If you leave for Israel within thirty days of Pesah, the Shulhan Arukh says you must search the night before you leave, and search again on the night of the fourteenth where you are staying, with a berakhah. What exempts you at home is selling the hames, or appointing someone to search or sell, before you go. Waiting to do it from abroad is too late. This, he explained, is why many Ashkenazim sell on the thirteenth.

In answer to questions: if you clear the whole house and go round checking it as you do so, that is a search. If everything in a flat abroad has been checked and the hames areas sold and marked off, a family member arriving on Hol HaMoed need not search again.

Time. Search from nightfall (8.45pm that year), but since it is a rabbinic misvah, in England, where it is late, one may begin from sunset, around 8pm.

Biur: destroy, not necessarily burn

The berakhah is al biur hames. Biur means to destroy. The Mishnah records a dispute over whether it must be done by fire. The Shulhan Arukh rules that any act of destruction is enough: crumble and scatter it, flush it, or put it in a communal bin or one about to be collected. The Rema writes that the custom is to burn, and the Ben Ish Hai calls it a good custom. Last year, when Erev Pesah fell on Shabbat, we did not burn at all.

Why is the berakhah on the night "on removing" rather than "on searching"? Because the search is only the first step towards removal. So a search that finds nothing is not a berakhah in vain. That is why the Raavad called the ten pieces a custom of the unlearned. We still keep it, but "in many houses... the bedikat hames is the search for ten pieces. That's wrong." Rav Ovadia Yoseph writes that each piece should be less than a kazayit, so a lost one is covered by the nullification. The feather helps reach into cracks.

Selling. "If a person doesn't need to sell, he should not sell. Selling is a big leniency." If you have only a little bread and a few cakes left, get rid of them and do the misvah. Crumbs in a toaster need no sale, since they are nullified. Selling utensils raises its own question of tevilat kelim after Pesah.

Medicines, toothpaste and hekhsherim

Medicines. Anything tasteless or bitter needs no hekhsher, even if it contains hames, since it is not food fit for a dog. Some invoke akhshevei, that by eating it one gives it importance, but Rav Moshe Feinstein and Rav Ovadia Yoseph rule that this does not apply: you want the tablet, not the drop of hames. Rav Ovadia advises not taking something known to contain hames for a slight headache, but writes that most medicines today contain none. Flavoured syrups, such as Calpol, must be checked unless there is danger. Toothpaste has added flavour, so the Rabbi personally thinks it is better bought with a hekhsher. Soap needs no hekhsher.

Natural products. "I recognise this year especially, with all the economic hits people are having, and inflation... people are struggling." For those who follow the Shulhan Arukh and Rav Ovadia Yoseph, pure products bought before Pesah, such as sugar, salt, oil, water, tea and coffee, need no hekhsher. Hames that fell in before Pesah is nullified and, for the Shulhan Arukh, does not reawaken. Even the Rema, who is strict that it does, agrees that strictly it does not. This is why the London Beth Din allowed such items at the start of Covid. "If you follow the Ben Ish Hai, then what I'm saying is incorrect": he is strict like the Rema.

Kitniyot and massah

Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul said he found the custom of kitniyot difficult. The more foods we ban, the more people eat massah, and massah, "water and wheat", is the food most likely to contain hames. Anyone who has seen a bakery knows it comes down to seconds. Still, it is the Ashkenazi custom from the thirteenth century. But kitniyot are not hames: they need not be sold, and Ashkenazi guests may be served food cooked in a pot used for rice, since the custom covers the pulses themselves, not their taste.

Practical summary

  • From the Torah, sincere nullification suffices; the search and removal are rabbinic, for pieces of about a kazayit.
  • Search only where hames is brought, plus the car and garden; with small children, wherever they reach.
  • If you travel within thirty days of Pesah, search before leaving or sell or appoint an agent first.
  • Hames may be destroyed by any means; burning is a good custom. Do not sell if you can simply dispose of it.
  • For Sephardim following the Shulhan Arukh, pure products bought before Pesah need no hekhsher; flavoured medicines and toothpaste do.
