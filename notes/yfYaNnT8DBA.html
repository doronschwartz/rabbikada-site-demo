Background: What the Mishneh Torah Was

"His magnum opus is certainly the Mishneh Torah. There was no one like him to do anything like it before him, and no one has done anything like it since."

The problem it solved

The Talmud is a discussion. Many things come up that aren't relevant to the point, and the laws of any one topic are scattered across Shas.

The Rif came closest, but it isn't the same

Rav Yitzchak Alfasi (the Rif), from North Africa, a century or more before the Rambam, abridged the Talmud, keeping only the halachic material. But it still follows the Talmud's order:

Rif

Mishneh Torah

Method

Goes through the Talmud tractate by tractate, keeping the halachah

Reorganises all of halachah by topic

To learn all the laws of Shabbat

Tractate Shabbat in the Rif contains material not about Shabbat, and misses Shabbat laws found elsewhere in Shas

One section: Hilchot Shabbat

Sources

The Babylonian Talmud

Bavli, Yerushalmi, Sifra, Sifrei, Tosefta, baraitot: all of rabbinic literature

Shape

An abridged Talmud

Fourteen books of clear rulings

Controversy

His books were later burnt, "but that was more because people didn't like the Rambam's philosophy in other works." Here, by contrast, Pinchas criticises the Mishneh Torah itself.

Pinchas's Four Criticisms

The Rambam quotes Pinchas: your Mishneh Torah is wonderful, but only for those who already know Talmud and the names of the Sages.

#

Criticism

1

You don't name the Sages. The Mishnah and Gemara are full of Rabbi Yehudah says, Rabbi Meir says; you never say who said what

2

People misread your book and err in halachah

3

You don't make clear that Talmud study is essential. People think the Mishneh Torah is enough. You should preach that they mustn't neglect the Talmud

4

You give no sources. How do I know you're right? I find laws whose source I can't locate

Behind criticism 3: the Rambam's own introduction says that one who learns Torah sheBichtav and then his book will know the whole Oral Law, which is why it's called Mishneh Torah, the "second Torah."

"Some of these criticisms recur over and over throughout the generations." As recently as a generation ago, Chabad, for whom daily study of the Mishneh Torah is central, was criticised by Rav Shach for the same reason: it isn't enough to study the Rambam; study the Talmud.

"So the Rambam addresses it in black and white here, and that's why this is such a crucial letter."

The opening rebuke

I have to tell you off. I know deep down how you feel about me, even if you didn't say it: you don't give weight to who I am and what I've written.

Answer to Criticism 3: "I Never Said Don't Study Talmud"

Hashem is my witness: around where I live, only two or three people are studying parts of my Mishneh Torah. Most of my students I've taught the whole Rif. Whoever asked me to learn Talmud with them, I learned whatever they wanted.

"Imagine asking the Rambam, I want to learn Talmud with you, and he says yes, sure."

Did I ever ask, or think, that every earlier book is no longer relevant and only mine should be learned? I wrote at the start of the Mishneh Torah that it's for those who don't have the time, or the capacity to work through the Talmud. For them I made a kitzur, the plain halachah. But I never, chas veshalom, said don't study Talmud.

A tension to note

In another letter, to his translator Ibn Tibbon (to be learned in the summer), the Rambam does seem to suggest that people waste their time on the Talmud's dialectic: "I'm not saying these words lightly." "We will try to understand that when we learn that letter." Here, though, he is unequivocal: people must study Talmud, and that's what he teaches.

Answer to Criticisms 1 and 2: No Names, and Misreadings

a) "I listed them all at the start"

The introduction to the Mishneh Torah traces the whole chain of tradition, generation by generation. "We've never done this together; we will, with Hashem's help." I listed all the names there in order. Why repeat them?

b) "No one has done what I did since Rabbeinu HaKadosh"

There were many books before me that abridged halachah, in Hebrew and in Arabic. But no one since Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, who wrote the Mishnah, has done what I did: condense all of halachah into clear statements.

On misreadings:

"When you write a book, I can't walk around with it explaining it to everyone. You write it, you give it out, people make mistakes. It's their problem."

c) Chibbur versus perush: the heart of the answer

Anyone who writes a book, in Torah or any other discipline, Jewish author or not, takes one of two approaches.

חיבור, chibbur: composition

פירוש, perush: commentary

What it is

Your own composition, to the point

Explains, dissects and analyses an earlier text

Contents

Only the correct points: no questions, no answers, no proofs. "The dry facts"

The correct view and the contradictory ones, questions on each, the answers, and the proofs that this view is right and that one wrong

Model

The Mishnah: Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi gathered the views and said this is the halachah

The Talmud: the Amoraim commenting on the Mishnah. (Likewise Rashi on Shas)

The Mishneh Torah

✔ "I wanted it in the way of the Mishnah"

✘

You didn't pay attention to what I wrote. You don't understand the difference between Mishnah and Gemara. That's why you keep writing that you find laws in my book whose source you don't know.

He softens it with a title ("my chieftain"), "I'm respecting you, giving you nice titles, but you haven't understood."

d) "But the Mishnah does quote names!"

The obvious challenge: the Mishnah often says this Rabbi says, that Rabbi says. The Rambam explains why, "an important principle when learning Mishnah":

  1. Most mishnayot have no names at all. A סתם משנה, an anonymous Mishnah, is authoritative: that is the halachah.
  2. Even named statements don't trace the chain. Rabbi Meir got his teaching from his teacher, and he from his; the Mishnah never lists the chain back.
  3. Rabbi sometimes merged two Sages' views he thought correct into one anonymous mishnah, without saying where each came from.
  4. He named Sages only where he was unsure of the halachah and communities followed different views. Naming them showed that each practice had authority (Rabbi Meir's, Rabbi Shimon's), so those following it shouldn't be castigated.

The proof: Mishnah Eduyot, chapter 1. Why record Shammai, or one Sage against the majority, if the law doesn't follow them? So that a beit din or community following that minority view has an authority to rely on.

e) Why that doesn't apply now

In the Mishnah's time, some communities did this and some did that. Once the Talmud was sealed, there is one Jewish approach; the vast majority of the vast majority practise it. There are no longer two camps.

f) Names invite people to reject the law

If I wrote this law comes from Rabbi X, people would say "that's his opinion; I don't have to follow it," and they'd be בועט, kick against it. All Israel accepted the Talmud, and so its Sanhedrin-level authority. I want every halachah to appear to the one studying it as the halachah, stam, divrei hakol. Not "he thinks this, he says that." This is the law. Follow the law. Full stop.

Summary of his answer on names

#

Answer

1

All the names are in the introduction

2

I wrote a chibbur, a composition like the Mishnah, not a perush like the Talmud

3

The Mishnah named Sages only where communities followed differing valid views; since the Talmud, all Israel follows one halachah

4

Names would let people dismiss the law as one man's opinion

"You criticised me because you misunderstand my purpose. If you'd taken a bit more time to understand, you wouldn't have this problem."

Answer to Criticism 4: "Where Are Your Sources?"

"Perhaps the most amazing part of the letter." Pinchas had implied the Rambam wrote things from his own mind and passed them off as established law.

I never did any such thing. Open your ear and your wisdom and pay attention.

The Rambam's own key to his book

How it's written

Where it comes from

Stated plainly, no qualification

Certainly from the Sages: Bavli, Yerushalmi, Sifra, Sifrei, Mishnah, Tosefta, the halachic works of Chazal

"The Geonim ruled…"

From the Geonim (c. 650–1000 CE; "like we learned on Monday nights")

"It seems to me…" / "From this I deduce…"

My own conclusion, labelled as such

So what's your problem? Why should I write the sources? Look in the Talmud.

What Pinchas should have said

What you should have said is that you sometimes can't remember where the source is. That happens to rabbis. Only great rabbis like you (the barb and the compliment together) can appreciate how much toil I put into this book.

"Not like the Rif. I swear."

Most people think my work is like the Rif's: that I abridged, removed the questions and answers, and gave the conclusion. That's not what I did, I swear.

The scale: 14 books, each of roughly 10 sections, each of roughly 10 chapters, and a single chapter is sometimes drawn from ten places in rabbinic literature.

"To write a chapter with the entire framework of Chazal in mind, and select this goes here, that goes there. Who can do that? That's incredible."

I didn't follow the order of the Talmud. I gathered everything together so that it would no longer be scattered, so you can find it in one place. That was my whole purpose.

It's impossible for anyone in the world to remember the entire Talmud constantly. When I had it all in my head (Bavli, Yerushalmi, Sifra, Sifrei), I put it together, sometimes from ten different places for one chapter.

The story of the dayan and the law of murder

A certain dayan came to me (older, apparently: he speaks down to him) holding the Book of Damages, open at the laws of murder. He showed me a law: "What's your source?"

Where the Rambam looked

Result

Makkot and Sanhedrin, where laws of murder belong

Went through all of both. Not there

The Yerushalmi

Not there. "I was very shocked, confused"

Gittin: "it must come up incidentally in the laws of divorce"

Not there either. "I was really astonished at myself. Where did I get this from?"

"Please give me a moment." The dayan left, and immediately I remembered

Sent someone to bring him back, and showed him: it came incidentally, in a tractate dealing with marriage. The dayan was astonished, and left

"I always get scared"

Whenever people ask me "what's your source?", I get scared and upset. Sometimes I answer at once: look in this tractate. Sometimes I don't know until I search for it. If even I can't remember, what will others do?

How do I console myself? I remind myself that that wasn't the purpose. My purpose was the dry laws, all the scattered laws together in one place.

The book he planned and never wrote

With Hashem's help, I plan another book giving the sources for every law, though it will be very cumbersome.

The plan:

- A law in Hilchot Shabbat sourced in Tractate Shabbat gets no citation: "157 pages of Shabbat; go and find it yourself."

- A Shabbat law sourced elsewhere in Shas gets a citation: this halachah stems from this tractate, that page.

- A separate book, because putting sources in the Mishneh Torah would ruin its purpose: it is a chibbur, not a perush.

I repeat: everything in the Mishneh Torah comes from Bavli, Yerushalmi, Tosefta, Sifra or Sifrei. It's hard to find because the Talmud's rules are scattered. Full stop.

The Rabbi noted in passing: the Rambam calls the Pesach laws Hilchot Chametz uMatzah, while the Shulchan Aruch calls them Hilchot Pesach, "interesting in itself." Under the plan, a law there drawn from Pesachim would go unsourced; one drawn from elsewhere would be cited.

The Irony: The Most-Commented Book of the Rishonim

Did the Rambam write that book? No. But dozens did it for him:

Commentary

Kesef Mishneh

Rav Yosef Karo (author of the Beit Yosef)

Maggid Mishneh

Rabbeinu Manoach

Lechem Mishneh

…and many more

Each trying to find the sources

"I'm almost sure this is correct. Yes, I say this with confidence: there is no book of the Rishonim, maybe Rashi aside, with as much commentary on it as the Mishneh Torah." The Rif and the Rashba have a few; but the Ra'avad was already writing on the Rambam right away, "and then on and on."

"This is the funny thing. The Rambam made a book where all you need is the Torah and my book. Now you learn his book, and around it are tens and hundreds of commentaries."

Why? Because he never wrote the source book. Much of the debate over the Rambam is what his source was, because what he means depends on where he took it from. One commentator says it's from here, so he means this; another says from there, so he means that, and you get a machloket over the Rambam's meaning. Had he written the sources, much of that would evaporate.

One of the Rabbi's teachers: "If the Rambam had known what they would do to his book, he wouldn't have written it." "The whole purpose was a clear this is it, open it, that's it."

Takeaways

Learn the Mishneh Torah, in order

If you've never studied it in order, try to find the time and complete it: once a week, once a day, one or two halachot. Especially for someone without much time for halachah or Talmud. "If you know the Mishneh Torah inside out, you basically know the whole Torah."

A test of authority

Since all of Chazal is condensed into those 14 books, anything you never came across in the Mishneh Torah is not from Chazal. Someone else said it, and it doesn't carry the same authority.

Kitniyot: there's no mention at all in the Rambam. It came in around the 1300s, after him. So "it can't have the same tokef, the same power and authority. The only thing that binds us is the Torah and the Sages of the last Sanhedrin: anything in the Bavli and Yerushalmi binds us. Other things are a notch down."

Summary

  1. Pinchas's four criticisms: no names, people err, it discourages Talmud study, no sources.
  2. Talmud: "Hashem is my witness": only two or three around him study the Mishneh Torah; he teaches the Rif and Talmud to the rest. The book is a kitzur for those without the time, never a replacement. (Contrast the letter to Ibn Tibbon, to come.)
  3. Names: listed in the introduction; the book is a chibbur like the Mishnah, not a perush like the Talmud. The Mishnah named Sages only where communities followed different valid views (Eduyot 1). After the Talmud, one halachah for all, and names would invite people to dismiss the law as one man's view.
  4. Misreadings: an author can't walk around explaining his book. No one since Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi attempted this.
  5. Sources: plain statement = Chazal; "the Geonim" = Geonim; "it seems to me" = my own. Chapters drawn from ten places; no one can hold the whole Talmud in mind. The story of the law of murder: even the Rambam sometimes couldn't find his own source, until he did.
  6. The unwritten source book: planned, never written; so the Kesef Mishneh, Maggid Mishneh, Lechem Mishneh and countless others did it, making it the most-commented work of the Rishonim.
  7. Practical: learn the Mishneh Torah in order; what isn't in it isn't Chazal (e.g. kitniyot).
