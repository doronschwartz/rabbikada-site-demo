Part A — Choresh (Ploughing)

Derivatives of ploughing

We rarely plough a field, but many derivatives (toldot) are equally forbidden by the Torah. Ploughing in the Mishkan prepared the land for planting the dye-plants; so any act that improves the soil for planting is Choresh:

  • Fertiliser, manure, compost.
  • Weeding (among other issues).
  • Levelling the ground (ashvuyei gumot) — filling in a hollow or flattening a bump outdoors.
  • Making a furrow or a hole in the earth deep enough to plant in — even without planting.

Walking sticks, buggies, heels and football studs

A walking stick pokes holes with every step; a buggy cuts furrows in soft ground; high heels and studs make holes. Since a pesik reisha (inevitable result) is normally forbidden even if unintended, why are these permitted?

  1. Melacha she'einah tzrichah legufah — the holes aren't made for planting, the Mishkan purpose, so it is only rabbinic. (Likewise squeezing a pickle just to get the juice out of it, not to use the juice.)
  2. Shinui — no one makes holes with a buggy or a walking stick; an unusual manner is only rabbinic (like writing with one's weaker hand).
  3. Arguably destructive (mekalkel) — you don't want the holes.

A pesik reisha involving a doubly-rabbinic act is permitted. So one may push a buggy through a park, wear heels or studs, and use a walking stick — provided one does not intend the holes (e.g. to plant in them later), which would be forbidden (rabbinically, being a shinui).

Games needing a level surface

Because levelling is Choresh, Chazal forbade games that need a level surface on earth — originally rolling nuts, today marbles — lest one fill in a dip mid-game. They even forbade it indoors when most houses had earth floors; today, with tiled floors, such games are fine indoors, and on concrete.

  • Golf — forbidden on Shabbat (needs a level surface on earth).
  • Tennis — usually on hard courts, so not this problem.
  • Football — a casual game doesn't really need a perfectly level pitch, so this isn't the issue.

Is a ball muktseh? And football on Shabbat

The Shulchan Aruch rules a ball is muktseh — in those days balls (made from things like an animal's stomach) had no recognised use. Today, when balls are manufactured for play, many poskim say it is hard to call a ball muktseh — designating something for use removes muktseh (as a stone designated as a doorstop). Some remain hesitant. Children may certainly play with a ball.

Even so, getting changed and playing a competitive game is hardly in the spirit of Shabbat. A casual kick-about for someone who needs an outlet is acceptable; but Shabbat holds so much more to do than play football.

Sweeping

Sweeping inside the house, even with a broom, is permitted; sweeping an earth area outside is not (levelling).

Questions from the floor

  • Putting garden furniture on soft earth? — Permitted. A "hole" is made by poking in and pulling out in one act; placing a chair and later lifting it is a different act.
  • Removing a stick or stone from the ground? — You are taking something out, not actively making a hole.

Part B — Zore'a (Planting)

Any act that assists growth

Not only planting seeds: anything that assists growth is forbidden by the Torah — including watering.

  • A sprinkler or hose set before Shabbat, or on a timer, may run on Shabbat.
  • Don't tamper with a multi-valve sprinkler system: closing one valve sends more water to the others — at least an indirect Zore'a.

Washing hands or pouring water on grass

Washing hands (e.g. netilat yadayim at a garden lunch) over grass, or tipping yesterday's rain off a garden chair onto the lawn — even in someone else's garden with no interest in it — is a pesik reisha and forbidden. This applies to water and water-based drinks (juice, Coke); not wine or chemicals, which don't benefit growth.

Indirect is permitted: washing your hands on concrete from which the water later trickles onto the grass — unintended and indirect (gerama). The same for flushing toilets or drains that run into the garden. If you intend to water the grass, it is forbidden.

Flowers in a vase — clearing up the confusion

  • Cut flowers are detached, so water cannot make them grow — there is no Torah prohibition.
  • Flowers not yet fully opened (e.g. half-opened roses): a rabbinic prohibition on putting them in water — it looks like planting. This applies even if the vase was filled before Shabbat, and even to put back a flower that fell out.
  • Fully opened flowers: Sephardim may fill a vase from the tap and put them in on Shabbat.
  • Flowers are not muktseh — vases and flowers may be moved, and flowers taken out.
  • A lulav (or anything muktseh) may not have water added on Shabbat.

Flower pots: watering a pot that stands on earth with drainage holes is a Torah prohibition; indoors, a rabbinic one — do not water plant pots on Shabbat. Moving them may be permitted in some circumstances — ask.

Questions from the floor

  • Plant food that comes with a bouquet? — Fine if the flowers are fully open; into a plant pot it would be forbidden by the Torah.
  • Removing dead flowers from the vase? — Large, clearly distinct flowers are not a "mixture", so it is not Borer.
  • Flowers that open in daylight? — Moving them to a sunny windowsill so that they open is a problem (because of the light).

Part C — Kotser (Harvesting)

Definition

Kotser = detaching something from its source of growth. Once detached, cutting it further is not Kotser — e.g. breaking a cut myrtle (hadas) leaf to release its scent, or snapping the stems of a bouquet by hand (scissors are muktseh anyway).

  • Flower pots: bringing a perforated pot standing on soil indoors detaches it from its source of nutrients — a Torah prohibition; taking an indoor pot outside onto earth is planting.
  • Cress sold growing in its punnet — cutting it on Shabbat is real harvesting, very possibly a Torah prohibition. Cut it before Shabbat and keep it in the fridge.
  • Weeds or plants growing in a non-earth spot where they took root — still their place of growth; detaching them is forbidden.
  • Walking through grass is fine; running through long grass should be avoided (it may inevitably uproot).

Using trees

Chazal were very strict about trees. One may not climb a tree on Shabbat — remarkably, one who climbed it deliberately and then repented must stay up until Shabbat ends (one who climbed by mistake may come down). Using a tree also includes:

  • Leaning on it to the extent that you would fall if it were removed (merely touching it is fine).
  • Prodding a ball out of a tree or bush.
  • Hanging a coat on a branch — or even on a peg directly fixed to the tree.

Something twice removed is permitted:

  • A hammock tied directly to the tree — forbidden; hung from pegs or hooks fixed to the tree — permitted.
  • A ladder leaning directly on a tree — forbidden; on a peg attached to the tree — permitted (assuming the ladder is designated for a permitted use, else it is muktseh).

Summary

  • Choresh: anything that improves soil for planting — fertilising, weeding, levelling, making holes. Walking sticks, buggies, heels and studs are permitted (no intent). No marbles or golf on earth; indoor tiled floors are fine. Balls are arguably not muktseh today; competitive football isn't in the spirit of Shabbat.
  • Zore'a: anything that assists growth. Don't pour water or wash hands onto grass; indirectly via concrete is fine. Fully opened cut flowers may be put in water; unopened ones may not. Don't water plant pots.
  • Kotser: detaching from the source of growth — beware plant pots and cress. Don't touch hard-stemmed or edible plants; smell inedible ones freely. Don't climb or lean on trees; a hammock only via pegs.
