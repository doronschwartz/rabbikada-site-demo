Topic: The custom of pouring wine at the Ten Plagues, its origin and its proper meaning

Sources: The Rokeah (Rabbi Elazar of Worms); the Arizal; the Hakhamim

Daily Halacha: Pesah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • Sepharadim pour from the cup into a basin at each of the 16 mentions (Dam, Esh, Timrot Ashan, the ten plagues, and Desakh Adash Beahav), emptying the cup, and the wine is then thrown away. Ashkenazim sprinkle with a finger at each point.
  • The first source is the Rokeah of Worms, 13th-century Germany. It spread among Ashkenazim; the Arizal adopted it, and through him it spread among Eastern Sepharadim, so today almost all communities do it.
  • A 19th-century explanation: we lose some wine, a symbol of joy, to express sadness that the Egyptians perished, since the Hakhamim teach not to rejoice excessively when the wicked are punished.
  • The original reason was somewhat superstitious: that these plagues should not fall upon us. This led to customs like pouring the wine onto enemies' property (recorded among the Jews of Kurdistan) or naming an enemy at each pouring (an Iraqi custom).
  • If it is one's custom, continue it, but there is nothing dangerous or frightening about this wine. Refusing to speak until it is poured out on someone's property and the like is superstitious and wrong.
  • Its purpose is to bring the plagues alive, especially for the children, and nothing more. Western Sepharadim never adopted it, since it came from Europe through the Arizal, whose customs they generally did not take on.

Sources quoted

  • The Rokeah: the earliest source for the custom.
  • The Arizal: adopted it, spreading it to Eastern Sepharadim.
  • The Hakhamim: not to rejoice excessively at the downfall of the wicked.

In one line

Pouring wine at the plagues is a fine custom to bring the story alive, but it must be kept free of superstition.
