Topic: Onaah: overcharging and underpaying, the one-sixth rule, and how it applies in today's markets Source: Shulhan Arukh, Hoshen Mishpat, Siman 227 Shiur: Part 2 of a series on monetary ethics, given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 22 June 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where Torah is ahead of the law

The first class in this series had covered paying wages on time and bankruptcy. Next week's, on intellectual property, would show halacha "playing catch-up" with secular law. Tonight was the reverse: an area where the Torah protected the vulnerable long before modern law.

He opened with a question to keep in mind. You buy an etrog before Sukkot for £100. You show it to your rabbi, who tells you it is worth £30 or £40. Can you return it? Or can the seller say: you saw what you were buying, it's your problem?

The prohibition

"When you sell something to your fellow or buy from your fellow, do not wrong one another" (Vayikra 25:14). Fraud, deception and misrepresentation are obviously forbidden, and that was not tonight's subject. The verse speaks in the context of the Yovel, when the price must reflect the years left until the land returns. Onaah means taking advantage. It applies to the seller who overcharges and the buyer who underpays, to sales and to rentals, and the Rambam and others count it as a Torah prohibition.

As far as the Rabbi knows, English law has nothing like it. A contract entered without duress, undue influence or misrepresentation stands, however one-sided the price. Halacha, by contrast, protects people who lack knowledge or expertise. Being savvy in business is fine; "that's how you make money". But there is a line where it becomes taking advantage of someone vulnerable. The Sefer HaHinukh explains: no one can be an expert in everything. An expert in cars knows nothing about washing machines. So the Torah limits how far anyone may profit from another's ignorance, and in this way we are all protected.

Because secular law offers no protection here, there is no conflict with the law of the land, and such a claim belongs in a Beit Din, which can enforce it.

The one-sixth rule

The Gemara in Bava Mesia sets three levels of deviation from the true price: - Less than a sixth. Normal in trade. The sale stands and the loss is accepted. - Exactly a sixth. The sale stands, but the difference must be returned. - More than a sixth. The sale is void. The buyer may return the item and get his money back.

So on an item worth £100, charging £130 voids the sale.

May a seller charge slightly above the market price, under a sixth, in the first place? The Rishonim disagree. The Shulhan Arukh brings both views without deciding. The later poskim rule that it is permitted, but a God-fearing person should avoid it.

Prices without a fixed price

In the time of the Gemara there was a known market price. Today most products sell within a range. If a product sells for £50 to £100, how do you measure a sixth? One opinion brought by the Beit Yosef said onaah applies only where there is a fixed price, but everyone disagreed: otherwise the misvah would have no meaning today. The poskim measure the sixth against the market range.

Contemporary poskim discuss how. Do you measure from the average, where most sellers are, or from the top of the range, if a significant minority sell there? On an etrog that most sell at £75 and some at £100, a sale at £110 is within a sixth on the second view.

This brings in a key principle of Hoshen Mishpat: kim li. The defendant in possession can say "I hold like that opinion," and as long as it is a credible view, the money cannot be taken from him.

Compare like with like

A coffee costs £2 to £4 in a chain. A hotel lounge charges £7, the Ritz perhaps £10. A suit in Mayfair costs ten times one from a high street chain. A claim of onaah in such cases would be thrown out. You compare the same product in the same kind of market, because the setting, the service and the name are part of what you pay for. Compare a department store with a similar shop and you may have a claim.

His favourite example: someone carries a cold can of Coke to the top of Everest and sells it for £20. That is not onaah. Compare it with other sales at the top of mountains, if there are any.

But a buyer's desperation is no excuse. If a woman has long been looking for a stone to match her own, and the seller knows she will pay anything and charges £15,000 for one worth £10,000, the sale can still be voided. Someone asked about something truly rare. Then, he said, the price is what people will pay, and the law does not apply to antiques in the same way.

The buyer's choice and the time limit

The Shulhan Arukh rules that the choice belongs to the one who was wronged. If you overpaid, and the item then rises steeply in value before the sale is voided, the seller cannot insist on taking it back. You may keep it.

How long do you have? The Shulhan Arukh gives no fixed time: as long as it normally takes to show the item to someone and check the price. For something easy to show, perhaps a week. For a rare item, perhaps longer. He gave the example of someone who paid £100,000 for a Sefer Torah and learned a year later that most sell for £25,000 to £40,000. That is too late, unless he was actually deceived, which is fraud and has no time limit.

Someone asked: if a friend shows you a purchase and you can see he overpaid, should you tell him? It depends, the Rabbi said. Based on the Gemara in Ketubot, if he can no longer return it, you should tell him it was a good buy. If he can still return it or claim, you should certainly tell him.

Conditions and exceptions

Can the parties agree in advance that there will be no claim of onaah? In English law, the Unfair Contract Terms Act lists terms one cannot contract out of. The Shulhan Arukh rules that such a condition is invalid, because you cannot forgo an amount you do not know. But if the buyer knows exactly how much he is overpaying and accepts it, he cannot later claim, and a wise seller will have witnesses. A sign saying "no refunds" does not prevent a claim of onaah.

Inflation and real market shifts are not onaah. If a storm is coming and every taxi charges £200 for a £20 journey, that is now the market price.

Exceptions he listed: - Barter. Swapping goods, a sheep for a horse, is not onaah, since each side wants what it receives. - Private sellers. Some argue the rule applies only to dealers, but most poskim apply it to personal sales too. - Inventions and copyright. You may sell what you created for any price. - Antiques and investments. An investment is priced on expected future value.

Someone asked about reselling special edition trainers online at ten times the price. The makers may charge what they like for their own product, the Rabbi said. Once the editions are sold out, the resale price becomes the market price and there is no claim, though whether it is the right thing to do is another question.

Back to the etrog, and Pesah prices

So, the etrog bought for £100 that is worth £30 to £50: once the market price is established with a few sellers, and the claim is made in reasonable time, before Sukkot, the sale is void and the money returned.

And Pesah prices? Sadly, the shops cannot be accused of onaah, because that is now the market price. The Gemara speaks very harshly of the mafkia shearim, who artificially raises prices, for example by buying up stock: it compares him to one who lends on interest, and the Beit Din may punish him. Whoever created such a market bears responsibility, but those selling at today's market price are not overcharging.

At the end, someone asked whether charging people in an emergency is not taking advantage. The Rabbi agreed it feels wrong, but once every driver charges that amount, it is the market price, and the halacha of onaah does not apply.

Practical summary

  • Overcharging or underpaying by more than a sixth of the market price voids the sale; exactly a sixth must be refunded; less than a sixth stands.
  • Today the sixth is measured against the market range for the same product in the same kind of market, and a defendant may rely on a credible opinion (kim li).
  • The wronged party chooses whether to void the sale, and must claim within the time normally needed to check the price.
  • A waiver of onaah is valid only when the buyer knows exactly how much he is overpaying.
  • Barter, inventions, antiques and investments are not subject to onaah; genuine market prices, even high ones, are not onaah, but artificially raising prices is condemned.
