After the break for the Hagim, the Rabbi recalled the plan: three shiurim on principles, two on important melakhot (Kosher, Zorea, Shohet), and next week a last shiur on when we may be lenient (shvut deshvut and similar rules). Tonight, since no series on Shabbat is complete without Bishul, was one more practical shiur.

What Bishul is

The Rambam says that whoever bakes, cooks, dyes or boils water is liable. The Mishnah calls the melakhah "Ofeh" (baking), but the Gemara explains that the real work in the Mishkan was Bishul, boiling the dyes for the coverings. Baking was just the first example in the list. Bishul is cooking with liquid and Afiyah is baking without, but the Rambam treats them as one melakhah, with no av and toladah between them.

It is not limited to food. The principle is to harden or soften something through fire. Melting wax or tar and firing pottery are Bishul too. We speak mostly of food because the other cases usually also involve Mav'ir (kindling).

Yad Soledet Bo: how hot is cooking?

Bishul needs fire, or something heated by fire, at the level of "Yad Soledet Bo". The Gemara defines this as heat that would scald the stomach of a baby, which nobody can test.

  • The Ben Ish Hai is unusually lenient: if you can still eat or drink it, it is not Yad Soledet Bo. That could mean 60 to 70 degrees, since we drink tea that hot. Most poskim, including Hakham Ben Sion and Hazon Ovadia, reject this as far too high.
  • A proof from Hulin: the blood of a freshly slaughtered animal does not make the knife forbidden, so that warmth is not Yad Soledet Bo. Animals run a little hotter than humans, so the figure is somewhere above 40 degrees.
  • The accepted range is 40 to 45 degrees: Hakham Ben Sion takes 40, Hazon Ovadia 45. It is only an estimate. Below it, everything is permitted.

Because 45 is only an estimate, a liquid heated to 45 before Shabbat is not "already cooked" for Bishul Ahar Bishul. For that, some poskim require 70 to 80 degrees.

Toladot of Bishul: Kli Rishon, Irui and Kli Sheni

Toladat Hamah. Cooking by a vessel that was itself heated on the fire is a Torah prohibition like the fire. An egg placed next to a boiling pot is an example.

Irui Kli Rishon (pouring from a vessel that is on the fire, such as an urn). The Rambam, who files it among the rabbinic prohibitions, seems to forbid it only rabbinically: the Torah prohibition covers the vessel on the fire. The Rashbam, the Ramban and the Rashba permitted it. Rabbenu Tam forbade it and the Shulhan Arukh follows him. So you may not put tea leaves in a cup and pour from the urn onto them. The Rabbi stressed that this is a dispute among the Rishonim, so where there is another reason to be lenient, one may be.

Kli Sheni. The Gemara is clear that a Kli Sheni does not cook. Tosafot explains that the walls of a pot on the fire stay hot and keep cooking, while a Kli Sheni cools quickly. The Rabbi suggested a simpler reason: the Torah's melakhah is what happened in the Mishkan, where nobody cooked in a Kli Sheni, so it falls outside the framework even if the water is very hot. A student asked whether this reflects the science of Hazal's day; the Rabbi said whether a ruling stays fixed when science changes is a separate dispute (as with lice).

Why do people think Kli Sheni is a problem? The Mishnah says food not cooked before Shabbat may be "rinsed" (poured on) with hot water, except for the salted fish Kulyas HaIspanin. Tosafot asks how this fits if Irui Kli Rishon is forbidden, and gives two answers. First: a Kli Sheni does cook, but only rabbinically, because it "looks like cooking". Second: a Kli Sheni does not cook at all, and the Mishnah mentions pouring only to teach that very sensitive foods cannot even be rinsed.

The ruling. Hazon Ovadia notes that this is a double doubt: the Rishonim who permit Irui Kli Rishon certainly permit Kli Sheni, and Tosafot himself is unsure. So Sefaradim may use a Kli Sheni freely, except for very sensitive foods like the Mishnah's salted fish (today perhaps herring) and possibly mint leaves. A Sefaradi may pour from the urn into a cup and then add the tea bag, leaves, coffee or sugar. Ashkenazim follow the Mishnah Berurah: they pour from one cup into another first, and many prepare tea essence before Shabbat.

Solids. The Maharshal distinguishes Kli Rishon from Kli Sheni only for liquids, since solids retain heat. Many Ashkenazim follow this: a hot potato or meat taken off the hot plate onto your plate is still Yad Soledet Bo, so no spices or salad next to it. Move it to another dish, or wait until it cools. Sefaradim do not hold this.

Questions on tea

Taking out the tea bag. It looks like Borer, since the bag filters the leaves. But simply lifting it out is permitted, like the Gemara's straw carried to the trough in a sieve even though waste falls through. The Rabbi explained that some acts are so far outside a melakhah that even Pesik Reisha does not apply, as when you walk past a neighbour's sensor light on Friday night. That differs from pouring water on grass to wash your hands, where the act itself is Zorea. What is wrong is leaving the bag on top to drip, which uses it as a filter. A student asked about squeezing it to avoid a mess in the bin; the Rabbi said it is still a problem.

A mesh tea infuser in the pot is fine for Bishul, but there may be a Borer issue, depending on the leaves.

Salted fish. A student suggested that the Mishnah simply concerns washing off preserving salt, and that dissolving salt is like melting wax. The Rabbi answered that salt is an exception: it does not dissolve or cook even in a Kli Rishon off the fire (Shulhan Arukh, Siman 318). He agreed it is a fair question whether heavy salting makes fish cook faster, and that the science would need checking.

Bishul Ahar Bishul: reheating

Everyone agrees solid food cooked before Shabbat may be reheated, which is why we use the hot plate. For liquids the Rishonim disagree. Maran is strict, like the Rosh; the Rema is lenient while the liquid is still warm. For Sefaradim, a liquid that is no longer boiling hot is subject to Bishul again.

Two ways out. The Hida (start of Siman 318): Amirah LeAkum is only rabbinic, and since the Rishonim are divided, one may ask a non-Jew explicitly to reboil it. And Hazon Ovadia permits putting the liquid on the hot plate before it switches on, since that is Gerama, which we treat leniently.

Bishul Ahar Afiyah: baking then cooking

The Yere'im (12th century) said one should not cook what was baked or bake what was cooked, since the second process adds a new dimension. He proved it from matsot (baked matsah boiled is no longer matsah) and the Korban Pesah (roasted, then boiled, is still a violation). The Beit Yoseph rejects both proofs: matsah needs a matsah taste, and the Torah specifically demands roasting.

The Shulhan Arukh writes "some forbid, even in a Kli Sheni, and some allow", and he meant that some allow it completely. But the Rema adds "in a Kli Sheni", which changes the reading. The Rabbi said the Rema often adds words that give the Shulhan Arukh a meaning Maran did not intend; asked if the Rema means to interpret Maran, he thought not, only to give his own view.

Hazon Ovadia rules that it is permitted both ways round, so already roasted coffee is fine in a Kli Sheni. The Ben Ish Hai and the Kaf HaHayim are strict, and would not pour boiling water directly onto ground Turkish coffee.

Practical summary

  • Bishul covers anything softened or hardened by fire, not only food. The accepted limit is about 45 degrees, but only as an estimate.
  • Irui Kli Rishon is forbidden for Ashkenazim and Sefaradim: do not pour from the urn onto tea leaves or a tea bag.
  • For Sefaradim a Kli Sheni does not cook: pour into a cup first, then add tea, coffee or sugar. Very sensitive foods, like salted herring, are the exception. Ashkenazim follow the Mishnah Berurah.
  • Lifting out a tea bag is permitted; do not leave it on top to drip.
  • A liquid that has cooled may not be reboiled. Ask a non-Jew explicitly, or put it on the hot plate before it comes on.
  • Hazon Ovadia permits Bishul after Afiyah and Afiyah after Bishul; the Ben Ish Hai and the Kaf HaHayim are strict.
