A question about Bnei Brak

Having finished the fourth chapter of Derech Hashem, the class paused for Pesah. The Rabbi promised one of his most beautiful ideas, one that would "make your Pesah special."

The Haggadah tells of Rabbi Eliezer, Rabbi Yehoshua, Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah, Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Tarfon reclining together in Bnei Brak, telling the story of the Exodus all night. About 1,900 years ago, long before Pesah holiday programmes, what were they doing there? Rabbi Akiva lived in Bnei Brak, but Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Tarfon had their communities in Lod. What about their families and their communities? And why travel to Rabbi Akiva, the youngest of them?

One question, one answer

The two most important paragraphs of Maggid are the question and the answer: Mah Nishtanah and Avadim Hayinu. The misvah of the night is to tell the story through question and answer; everything else adds to it.

Mah Nishtanah is one question with four examples: why is this night different? The child is confused by mixed messages. Massah is the bread of haste and redemption, yet it is "ha lahma anya," the bread of affliction. We eat bitter maror, yet drink four cups of wine like kings. We dip twice, a sign of royalty. Is tonight about suffering or about freedom?

The answer: "We were slaves, and Hashem took us out." Both are true, so we commemorate both.

Why remember the slavery?

Why not just celebrate the redemption? - The simple answer: only one who has known darkness can appreciate light. Coming out of a long tunnel, you appreciate daylight. - The deeper answer: with complete emunah, a person understands that exile is itself part of the redemption. The bad is not just a contrast to the good; it is an essential part of it.

That is why we first eat massah alone and maror alone, as people naturally see good and bad as separate parts of life. Then we eat Korekh, the two together: they are one. At the end of days we will see that what looked bad was all for the good.

A participant asked how 210 years of slavery, or the Holocaust, could be called part of the good, when it was not merely bad but evil. The Rabbi said he would return to it and addressed it later in the evening.

Three stories of Rabbi Akiva

The idea that everything Hashem does is for the best was Rabbi Akiva's motto, and he lived it. 1. Berakhot 60b: turned away by a town, Rabbi Akiva slept in a field. The wind blew out his candle, a cat ate his rooster, a lion ate his donkey, and each time he said, "Everything Hashem does is for the best." That night an army took the town captive. A light or a noisy animal would have revealed him. 2. Sanhedrin 101a: when Rabbi Eliezer was ill, Rabbi Tarfon, Rabbi Yehoshua, Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah and Rabbi Akiva visited him, the same group as in the Haggadah. The first three praised him: you are better than rain, than the sun, than a father and mother. Rabbi Akiva, the youngest, said, "Suffering is precious." You would expect the sick man to be upset. Instead Rabbi Eliezer said, "Support me, so that I can hear the words of Akiva my student," and asked for his sources. The Rabbi explained: when someone who has never suffered tells you pain is good, it sounds hypocritical; but everyone knew Rabbi Akiva had lived through suffering and stayed upbeat. Rabbi Eliezer wanted to make that view part of himself. 3. Makkot 24b: Rabban Gamliel, Rabbi Elazar ben Azaryah, Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Akiva heard the noise of Rome and later saw a fox coming out of the Holy of Holies. The others wept; Rabbi Akiva laughed. If those who violate Hashem's will live in such tranquillity, how much more will those who do His will be rewarded. And now that the prophecy of destruction has been fulfilled, the prophecy of rebuilding will be too. The depth of the exile, he said, shows the depth of the coming salvation.

Why they went to Rabbi Akiva

This was a very troubled time, after the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. Between the First and Second Temples the exile had lasted only 70 years, so people hoped for a quick redemption. Rabbi Akiva himself believed Bar Kokhba was Mashiah. When Bar Kokhba was killed and Beitar fell, the last traces of hope seemed to vanish, and it dawned on people that the exile might be long. He compared it to Ground Zero in New York: while the ruins stand, there is still a memory; plough them over and even that is gone.

They needed hizuk, strength. Who do you go to? Rabbi Akiva, who could see the good in everything. And why Pesah? Because that was when the loss of the Temple was felt most, with the memory of everyone coming to Jerusalem and eating the Korban Pesah together in their groups.

At dawn their students came: "Our teachers, the time for the morning Shema has come." Shema Yisrael, Hashem Elokenu, Hashem Ehad: Hashem is the name of mercy and Elokim of judgment, and they are one. We cover our eyes for Shema because we cannot yet see that the bad is good; we can only believe it. On Yom Kippur we say Barukh shem aloud because we feel like angels. That night, guided by Rabbi Akiva, the rabbis reached a level at which they could truly say the Shema.

He related this to the past year, which had been full of difficulty, illness and sadness, but also of laughter and sunshine. With perspective and maturity, the hiccups in life are part of its beauty.

Birth, redemption and Pidyon HaBen

His second idea began with a question: why is there no Pidyon HaBen after a Caesarean birth? The halakhah is learnt from a verse and is certain; the question is why.

The Rabbi recalled his own thoughts when his wife gave birth: someone passing a labour ward would think something terrible was happening. "What looks and sounds like death is actually life." This is a microcosm of Jewish history: - Egypt was an "iron furnace," and out of that suffering came a nation that experienced the revelation at Sinai. - Purim: from the threat of annihilation, overnight, Mordekhai gained great influence. - The Holocaust: stressing he did not want to be misunderstood, he said that in 1945 it was easy to write off Jewish continuity, yet three years later the Jewish people came home to Israel. "If you can't see God's hand in that..."

He added a seed that decomposes before it sprouts, and Thomas Kuhn's idea that scientific discoveries come from anomalies, when the existing rules fail.

Pidyon HaBen commemorates Hashem's own intervention in Egypt: "I, and not an angel." A natural birth is the same pattern: what seems close to death turns into life with no intervention but Hashem's. A Caesarean is no less special, but it involves human instrumentality, the kind of human building he associated with Shavuot rather than Pesah, which is passive and entirely Hashem's doing. "That's why I suggest Pidyon HaBen is not relevant for a Caesarean birth." Asked about natural births with medical help, he said those helpers support the natural process rather than replace it.

He closed by urging everyone to use Pesah to recharge their emunah, to understand the Seder, and to pass the story on.

Summary

  • Seder night holds both slavery and redemption, and Korekh joins them: the bad is part of the good.
  • Rabbi Akiva's "gam zu letovah" was not a slogan but his life, as three Gemaras show.
  • After the destruction and the fall of Bar Kokhba, the rabbis went to Rabbi Akiva in Bnei Brak for hizuk, and reached the level of truly saying Shema.
  • From birth pangs to Egypt to the return to Israel, darkness is often the beginning of light.
  • The Rabbi suggests Pidyon HaBen applies only to natural birth because it mirrors Hashem's direct redemption in Egypt.
