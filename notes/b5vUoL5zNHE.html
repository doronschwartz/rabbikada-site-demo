Topic: The first cup, eating and drinking during the Haggadah, and setting the Seder plate Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 473, paragraphs 1-4 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 23 March 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

"They pour for him"

After the previous class on Kadesh and reclining, Siman 473 continues the Seder from the first cup up to the meal.

The Shulhan Arukh opens: "They pour for him the first cup." This is the language of the Mishnah in Pesahim, and the Rema makes it explicit that others should pour for you, as a sign of freedom. It is a good custom, not a requirement; someone alone pours for himself. At a family Seder each can pour for the other, with the usual care between husband and wife when she is niddah. The Rema says this at least for the head of the household.

Saying "leshem yihud". The Rabbi noted that the Mishnah Berurah, unusually for him, suggests having in mind before each step that one is doing the misvah. The Ben Ish Hai has a "leshem yihud" for every part of the Seder. "It's a lovely thing," but having it in mind is enough.

Shehiyanu said at Kiddush should be meant to cover all the misvot of the night. Someone who missed it, for example because he was ill on both Seder nights, may say it whenever he remembers during the festival, even on the seventh day, even in the street.

Saturday night. When the Seder falls on Saturday night, Havdalah is added to Kiddush. If you forgot Havdalah and remembered during Maggid, do not take an extra cup, which would make five. Wait until the berakhah at the end of Maggid and say Havdalah over the second cup.

Reading the Haggadah by candlelight

On Shabbat and Yom Tov one may not read by the light of a lamp alone, in case one tilts it to see better, as happened to Rabbi Yishmael. If the electricity fails late at night, one may still read by streetlights coming in. But one may read the Haggadah, and say Birkat HaMazon, even by candlelight alone, because they are fluent in one's mouth. The Shulhan Arukh says elsewhere that "even the most ignorant know the Haggadah", which the Rabbi suspected is "debatable nowadays".

The Rema also notes that the custom of some Ashkenazim to wash hands before Kiddush on Shabbat is not done on Pesah night, since Urhas must come after Kadesh.

No berakhah after the first cup

One drinks the first cup reclining and does not say the berakhah after wine, because the cup is meant to carry on to the next ones. Sefaradim do not say borei peri hagefen again on the second cup, though Ashkenazim do. How can a berakhah last two or three hours? Some say it is all one sitting, and the night is one continuous obligation. Intention alone could not stretch a berakhah forever: "kavanah doesn't help forever. There's a limit."

Eating and drinking during Maggid

The question asked every year: can I eat or drink during Maggid?

Drinking. Paragraph 3 says one may drink more cups between the first and the second, but not between the third and fourth, so as not to become drunk and unable to focus on the Haggadah. The clear implication: other drinks are fine.

Someone asked why there is no such worry before Birkat HaMazon, after four cups. Because Birkat HaMazon is fluent, while the Haggadah needs thought and discussion. The Rabbi stressed this every year: "Haggadah is not a text to read. It's not a prayer." The Torah's misvah is to tell the story. The Haggadah text is "a springboard" that the Hakhamim put together to guide the discussion. "If you ask the one question with the four parts and answer Avadim Hayinu, and the rest of the night you spend learning the midrashim on what happened, you fulfil the misvah perfectly."

Eating. There is nothing intrinsically wrong with eating before the massah. The problem is the berakhah. Karpas is eaten in less than a kezayit so that its borei peri haadamah carries on to the maror, and so no berakhah is said after it. If you eat enough to need borei nefashot, you cut that link. So:

  • Small amounts are fine: a piece of chocolate, and another ten minutes later, since a kezayit only counts if eaten within about five to nine minutes.
  • A meal, or something like three eggs, is not.
  • One may even take more karpas a little later. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul was bothered that the berakhah on karpas should cover maror hours later, and suggested eating a little more karpas periodically to keep the link.

The egg is a custom, and communities eat it at different points: some at the start of the meal, the Syrians just before the afikoman. There is no halacha that a whole egg must be eaten.

The Seder plate

Paragraph 4: they bring before the head of the household a keara containing three massot, the maror, another vegetable, and two cooked dishes, customarily an egg and meat.

One plate per household. Only the person leading needs a keara, however many are at the table. The Rabbi had seen a posek suggest that a married couple staying with their parents should have their own; he disagreed. It is one household and one Seder.

The massot go on the plate. The Shulhan Arukh says the plate "has in it three massot". The Arizal was particular that the massot are inside the keara, with the other items arranged above them according to the Kabbalistic structure. Those who can should do this, with the items on dishes on top of the massot.

Why three massot? The Rabbi said he had complained to his children's school for teaching that they represent Kohen, Levi and Yisrael. "These are lovely ideas. That's not the reason." First know the plain reason: on Yom Tov we need lehem mishneh, two whole loaves. We also break one massah to fulfil lehem oni, the bread of poverty, which is half a loaf, since a poor man always saves a piece. Two whole and one to break makes three.

Karpas. The Gemara's preferred vegetable is karpas, celery. Ashkenazim in Europe used potato because they had nothing else; today celery is available. Any vegetable works; even a strawberry could be dipped. What matters is the dipping and keeping it under a kezayit. Why do we dip at all? It was the practice of the aristocracy to start with an appetiser, and it arouses curiosity, since normally after Kiddush the meal begins at once. The salt water recalls the tears and the bitterness. Later explanations (perekh and the letter samekh, 600,000) are nice additions.

Washing for dipping. All year, food dipped in liquid requires washing the hands without a berakhah, which is why Urhas comes before Karpas. This applies to washed fruit eaten wet, such as grapes. Biscuits dipped in tea are debatable, and the Rabbi tends to be more lenient there.

The two cooked dishes recall the Pesah offering and the festival offering (hagigah). It became customary to use a roasted shank bone and an egg, though the Mishnah Berurah allows two different pieces of meat. Why an egg? It is the food of mourning: the first night of Pesah always falls on the same weekday as Tisha BeAv (that year, Wednesday night). The shank bone is not eaten, and one must not call it "the Pesah offering", lest it look like eating sacrifices outside the Temple.

The Rema arranges the plate so that one never has to pass over a misvah: the karpas nearest, then the salt water, then the massot, and so on. Customs differ widely, and Sefaradi families mostly follow the arrangement printed in their Haggadah.

Practical summary

  • It is a good custom for others to pour your cups, at least for the leader of the Seder.
  • No berakhah after the first cup; Sefaradim do not repeat borei peri hagefen on the second cup.
  • Between the first and second cups you may drink anything. Avoid wine between the third and fourth.
  • Before the massah you may eat small amounts, under a kezayit within a few minutes, but not a meal.
  • The leader alone needs a Seder plate, ideally with the three massot inside it: two for lehem mishneh and one to break for lehem oni.
  • Celery is the ideal karpas, eaten in less than a kezayit; wash the hands without a berakhah before dipping.
