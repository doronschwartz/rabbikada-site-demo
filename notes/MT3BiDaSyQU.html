Topic: Yihud Hashem as the focal point of Rosh Hashanah, and the two modes in which Hashem runs the world Source: The Ramhal, Derech Hashem, Part 4 (on the Yamim Noraim and the divine conduct) Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in an online shiur for Torah Way, Tuesday 5 September 2023 (19 Elul)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

An idea to change how we approach the Yamim Noraim

Ten days before Rosh Hashanah, the Rabbi offered one idea which, if fully understood and internalised, would make the whole season "far more meaningful" and "far less stressful", and would also be the most authentic to the words of Hazal. The idea is how Yihud Hashem, the oneness and sole sovereignty of Hashem, runs through the whole of the Yamim Noraim.

He started with two puzzles.

The first is the famous question: Rosh Hashanah is the Day of Judgment and Yom Kippur is the day of forgiveness. Surely forgiveness should come first, and judgment afterwards?

The second is that the Rosh Hashanah prayers, in the text of Hazal, do not ask for anything. There is no request for livelihood, health or blessing, and there is no Vidui, no confession, at all. (The Sim Shalom blessing is the standard text used all year, and is kept as it is.) For a day of judgment this seems absurd. Yet the central addition of the day, in the third blessing of the Amidah, speaks only of Hashem's kingship: "And so, Hashem, place Your awe on all Your works... and all will recognise that You are King."

Two modes of divine conduct

The answer, he said, is a passage in the Ramhal's Derech Hashem, and "when you understand this, everything falls into place. It is a beautiful puzzle." The Ramhal describes two modes (hanhagot) in which Hashem runs the world.

Hanhagat HaMishpat, the mode of justice. This is how the world normally runs. More precisely, it is the mode of consequences. The Rabbi stressed that reward and punishment (sekhar va'onesh) are better understood as consequences than as prizes and penalties. A baby left on a table who falls off is not being "punished"; gravity is a law of nature. Someone who trains daily and loses weight has not been "rewarded"; that is a consequence. A true reward would be arbitrary, such as a parent promising ice cream with no connection to the schoolwork. In the same way, a person who keeps the misvot and stays away from sin becomes a better person, and that has its own results, including in the afterlife.

Hashem, so to speak, is bound by the system He made. He loves us and wants the best for us, but where there are rules there must be consequences. He compared this to a parent who sets rules at home and must keep to them, or the rules mean nothing. The Gemara in Bava Kamma (50a) warns that whoever says Hashem is simply lenient (a "vatran") will lose his life. It cannot be that everything is overlooked.

Hanhagat HaYihud, the mode of oneness. Occasionally, said the Ramhal, Hashem suspends the system. The Torah's source is in Parashat Ki Tisa, after the Golden Calf: "I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and I will show mercy to whom I will show mercy", which Hazal explain to mean even if the person does not deserve it. This is called the mode of Yihud because it shows that Hashem is the one sovereign: He made the rules, and if He wishes He can set them aside.

The Rabbi's image was a presidential pardon. Law says you serve your sentence. The pardon cannot become routine, or the law collapses. But the occasional pardon shows who is in charge. In the same way a father who set a bedtime rule can say, now and then, "today is a holiday, today we ignore the rule", and the rule stays in place the rest of the year.

How this explains Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur

Yom Kippur is the day of Mishpat. The whole day is Vidui: "I did this, I should have done that", with every detail listed. The Rambam is clear that Vidui is the most powerful moment of the day, and that without it there is no forgiveness.

Rosh Hashanah has a different aim. "What is the best way to get through the din? Cancel the court case." If on Rosh Hashanah we go through the details of what we did and what we could have done, we are playing into the mode of Mishpat, which is exactly what we are trying to leave. So we do not confess, and we do not make personal requests. The focus is on Malkhut and Yihud: Hashem, You are King, and there is no other force or power. That is why the addition is in the blessing that ends "HaMelekh HaKadosh", and why the shofar is blown as a call proclaiming His sovereignty.

Only by recognising that Hashem is in complete control can we come into the second mode, where He may give us what we do not deserve. This is also why one comes to Rosh Hashanah in a spirit of confidence, in white clothes. The details can wait for Yom Kippur, ten days later.

What Yihud Hashem means in practice

Yihud Hashem, the Rabbi explained, does not mean only that there are not two gods. It means that nothing in the world can happen outside the will of Hashem. If a person truly lived with that, there would be no temptation to sin. A sin is always the belief that I will gain something I would not otherwise get. If nothing can benefit me outside Hashem's will, there is nothing to gain.

In reality this is hard, because Yihud Hashem is something we believe intellectually, but we cannot see or feel it in daily life. The Ramhal says this is why we cover our eyes when we say the Shema: we do not see it yet. We wait for the time when "Hashem will be One and His name One", when everyone will see it.

This is also the meaning of "like smoke, evil will vanish". Wrongdoing looks grand and promises gain, but in the long run it is smoke. He told the Breslov story of a man who ran through a town shouting that he had the secret of life. The whole town ran after him, and when they caught him he opened his hand and there was nothing in it. "We pursue, we pursue, we run, we run, we think it will give us gain." Living with Yihud Hashem means knowing that nothing we do against it will ever give us a real gain.

He added the Ramhal's image from the opening of Derech Hashem: a garden of beautiful roses is wasted if there are thickets and no path. Rosh Hashanah, he said, lays out the path, the framework, before we get into the detail. That framework is Yihud Hashem.

Summary

  • Hashem runs the world in two modes: Mishpat, in which consequences follow from our actions, and Yihud, in which He can show mercy beyond what we deserve.
  • Reward and punishment are best understood as consequences, like gravity or the results of training, and not as arbitrary prizes.
  • Yom Kippur belongs to Mishpat, with its detailed Vidui. Rosh Hashanah is about moving out of Mishpat by proclaiming Hashem's kingship and oneness, which is why its prayers have no confession and no personal requests.
  • Living with Yihud Hashem means believing that nothing happens outside Hashem's will and that sin brings no lasting gain. That is hard in practice, which is why we return to the idea each year.
  • Rosh Hashanah is the time to set this framework. The nitty-gritty of repentance follows ten days later on Yom Kippur.
