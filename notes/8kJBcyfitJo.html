Topic: Civilian casualties in war: the Rambam, the Ramban and the Maharal on Shekhem, the contemporary poskim, the Geneva Conventions, and whether soldiers must be put at risk to reduce civilian deaths Sources: Bereshit 34 and 49; the Rambam, Hilchot Melakhim 9:14; the Ramban on Bereshit 34; the Maharal, Gur Aryeh on Bereshit 34; the Radak on Divrei HaYamim I 22:8; the Minhat Hinukh 425; Bava Kamma 92a; Yehoshua 9; Shmuel I 15; Rav Shaul Yisraeli; Rav Hershel Schachter; Rav Asher Weiss; Rav Yaakov Ariel; Rav J. David Bleich, Contemporary Halakhic Problems III; Rav Aharon Lichtenstein Shiur: The third class of the Torat Am Yisrael series, given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Tiferet Eyal, in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 21 November 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The question

The first class of the series dealt with the hostages, the second with self-defence. Tonight's question is how far the Jewish nation must be concerned with civilian casualties when it goes to war. For two thousand years in exile this was never a practical halachic question, so almost everything on it comes from the poskim of the last sixty or seventy years.

Groups such as Hamas exploit international law and Western sensibilities by embedding themselves in civilian infrastructure and using civilians as human shields. Any civilised nation fighting them faces the same balance: defeat the enemy, and limit civilian deaths as far as possible.

The Rabbi gave three cases that the poskim discussed: - Jenin, 2002. After the Pesah night bombing in Netanya, in which 29 people were killed at the Seder, the IDF could have bombed the camp the terrorists came from. Instead it sent soldiers in on foot, house to house, to limit civilian casualties, and 13 soldiers were killed. - Lebanon, 2006. Rather than carpet bombing southern Lebanon in response to Hezbollah's rockets, Israel combined air strikes with ground forces, and over a hundred soldiers fell. - The taxi. Rav Yuval Sherlow was asked whether a dangerous terrorist leader could be targeted from the air while he sat in a taxi with its driver.

With some 48 soldiers already fallen in the ground operation in Gaza, the question is pressing: is it right to put our soldiers at risk in order to reduce enemy civilian casualties?

Shimon and Levi at Shekhem

The starting point is the story of Dinah. After she was abducted and violated, Shimon and Levi killed not only Shekhem and his father Hamor, the ruler of the town, but all the men of the city.

The Rambam (Hilchot Melakhim 9:14) justifies them. One of the seven Noahide laws is dinim, to set up courts and remove evil from society. The men of Shekhem saw what was done, did not protest and did not act, so they were complicit. Even on this view, the Rabbi stressed, only the men were killed, since in those days only they had the ability to protest.

The Ramban disagrees. Shekhem and Hamor could be killed, but the townspeople had no power to stop their ruler, and even if they deserved death for other reasons, it was not for Shimon and Levi to carry it out. He brings support from Yaakov's rebuke, "you have troubled me". The Rambam could answer that Shimon and Levi have the last word, "shall our sister be treated as a harlot?", and that Yaakov's words on his deathbed, "ish" and "shor", may refer to the sale of Yoseph, who is called "shor". The verses can be read both ways.

The Maharal (Gur Aryeh) takes a middle path. Like the Ramban, he holds that the people of Shekhem were not guilty, since they acted under duress. Yet Shimon and Levi were right, because when a nation attacks us the Torah permits war in response, and war is waged against the nation as a whole without distinguishing guilty from innocent.

Applying the three views today

The Rabbi set out what each view would mean in Gaza, stressing that he was analysing the halachic sources, not making military or political recommendations, and that he was not saying any of this should be done: - On the Rambam, a population that elected and does not resist its rulers could be held complicit. He added that videos of medical staff involved with the hostage-taking strengthen that case, and wondered aloud whether, unlike in Shekhem, women today might also be counted as complicit, though never children. - On the Ramban, without clear proof that everyone is complicit, only the terrorists may be targeted, one by one, on the ground. - On the Maharal, civilians do not deserve to die, yet civilian deaths in the course of a justified war are a tragic reality of war and are permitted.

He noted that the Maharal is in one sense stricter than the Rambam: if the terrorists could be removed without harming anyone else, the Maharal would require it. In another sense he is more lenient, since he does not depend on proving complicity.

Answering a question on how complicity could ever be proved, he said that war is not a Sanhedrin trial with warnings and witnesses. One works with the facts available, while recognising that protest in Gaza, unlike in Iran, may cost a person his life.

The contemporary poskim

Rav Shaul Yisraeli, founder of Eres Hemdah, held that one cannot decide between the Rambam, the Ramban and the Maharal, so there is no basis to act against a whole community that has not removed the murderers from its midst, as long as fear and coercion can reasonably excuse it. On his view one could not bomb a building with one terrorist and thirty civilians in it.

Rav Hershel Schachter, Rav Asher Weiss and Rav Yaakov Ariel rely on the Maharal to allow harm to members of the enemy nation in wartime. Their proofs: - The Radak explains that David could not build the Beit HaMikdash because innocent people died in his wars. He was not punished, since this is part of war, but the Temple had to be built by clean hands. - Those who disagree with the Maharal on Shekhem may only dispute whether the abduction of one woman was a cause for war, not his principle. - The Minhat Hinukh writes that the usual rule against endangering oneself does not apply in war, which is why the Sis Eliezer requires a soldier to risk his life for a comrade. So too, Rav Schachter argues, the usual protection of innocent life gives way in war.

Rav J. David Bleich writes that one searches in vain in classical sources for any ruling that treats the likely death of civilians in a legitimate war as a halachic or moral problem. He cites the Gemara in Bava Kamma: the cabbage is struck together with the thorn beside it.

The Rabbi's conclusion was that the Maharal's position is not a minority view, and that it is a strong and legitimate approach. He stressed, "has veshalom", that none of this permits harming anyone at random. It applies only where there is no other way to reach the terrorists.

The Geneva Conventions

Rav Asher Weiss raises a possible objection. Yehoshua kept his treaty with the Givonim even though they had deceived him, so perhaps the Fourth Geneva Convention is a treaty binding Israel. The Rabbi, who studied public international law, gave two answers: - Yehoshua may have kept the treaty because of hillul Hashem, not because a treaty overrides halacha. - International law rests on the conduct of states. The protection of civilians has been ignored in wars from Dresden to Hiroshima, and a rule that is never enforced is theoretical.

Three final questions

  • Are we at war? Clearly. Rav Schachter already held that Israel is in a constant state of war, and this is not targeting individuals but thousands of attackers and more who want to follow them.
  • Against a nation? Israel says it fights Hamas, not the Palestinians. But Hamas are the elected government of Gaza, and Rav Asher Weiss notes that Shaul warned the Keini to leave before he fought Amalek, so warning civilians to evacuate fits this framework.
  • Must soldiers be put at risk to reduce civilian casualties? This is the hardest question, and there are no sources either way. Rav Aharon Lichtenstein held that human life is human life, and that everything must be done to reduce civilian deaths even at risk to our soldiers. Rav Schachter wrote that the ground operations in Jenin and Lebanon were wrong, since reducing civilian casualties should not come at the cost of Israeli lives. Taken to its extreme, that would justify bombing all of Gaza, and the Rabbi said he struggled to say that would be right. He concluded that it depends on a person's moral sense.

Questions

  • Celebrating the attacks. Those in the West Bank who publicly glorify the massacres are complicit on every view, but where a person can be imprisoned rather than killed, as all civilised countries accept, that is the right course.
  • Jewish and non-Jewish life. Asked whether the answer depends on valuing Jewish life above non-Jewish life, he cited the rule that one may not kill another to save oneself, since the other person's blood is no redder than yours, and noted that there are poskim who hold the same for a non-Jew. A claim, raised in the class, that one may kill a non-Jew to take his organs he called morally repugnant and unrelated to this discussion.
  • Children. Babies and young children are not complicit in any way. A child who is actually fighting, however, has the status of a combatant.
  • Future terrorists. May someone be targeted because he will probably grow up to be a terrorist? He left it open, pointing to the tension between Yishmael, judged "baasher hu sham", as he is now, and the ben sorer umoreh, judged on his future.
  • Wars of expansion. None of this discussion applies to a war of expansion, only to a war of defence against an enemy that has attacked.

Summary

  • The poskim divide between the Rambam (the population is complicit), the Ramban (it is not, and only the guilty may be targeted) and the Maharal (it is not, yet civilian deaths in a justified war are permitted).
  • Rav Shaul Yisraeli would not rely on the Rambam or the Maharal; Rav Schachter, Rav Asher Weiss, Rav Yaakov Ariel and Rav Bleich follow the Maharal, with support from the Radak, the Minhat Hinukh and the Gemara.
  • The Geneva Conventions are not comparable to Yehoshua's treaty, since they are not observed or enforced.
  • Israel is at war, against a nation; whether soldiers must be risked to reduce civilian casualties has no clear source and depends on moral judgment.
  • Harming anyone who can be avoided is never permitted. May Hashem give the IDF the strength to end the war quickly, and make our discussion theoretical rather than practical.
