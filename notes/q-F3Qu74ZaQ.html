Topic: Lighting from an existing flame, creating a new flame, and lighting a nahalah candle on Yom Tov

The halacha

  • Lighting for food is fully permitted on Yom Tov: one may light a new flame from an existing one with a match, or add fuel to strengthen an existing fire.
  • The Gemara forbids creating a new flame, a rabbinic prohibition according to the vast majority of Rishonim, because it is molid, creating something new. One view links this to muksah; the Rambam explains it could have been done before Yom Tov. So one may increase an existing flame but not create a new one.
  • Since it is rabbinic, where there is a misvah and no alternative one may ask a non-Jew to create a flame: for example, if on the second night of Yom Tov the flame prepared beforehand went out and one cannot light the candles or heat food.
  • Rarely, if it was impossible to prepare a flame before Yom Tov, such as the Hida's example of a man released from prison straight into the hag, Hakham Ovadiah rules one may light oneself. Generally, though, one should ask a non-Jew.
  • By mitokh, lighting from an existing flame is permitted for any purpose, such as extra candles for light or ambience at the table.
  • Lighting for a purpose must have a clear benefit or a misvah, as the Shulhan Arukh permits lighting a candle in the synagogue. The poskim are not comfortable with lighting a nahalah (yahrzeit) candle for a parent on Yom Tov itself; even if the nahalah falls on Yom Tov it should be lit beforehand. Some communities had the minhag to light it on Yom Tov, and one with that established minhag has what to rely on.

Sources quoted

  • The Rambam: a new flame is forbidden because it could have been made before Yom Tov.
  • Hakham Ovadiah Yoseph, citing the Hida: one who could not prepare a flame beforehand may light on Yom Tov.
  • Shulhan Arukh: one may light a candle in the synagogue on Yom Tov.

In one line

On Yom Tov light only from an existing flame, use a non-Jew for a new one when a misvah requires it, and light a nahalah candle before Yom Tov.
