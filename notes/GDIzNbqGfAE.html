Topic: The structure of the Rosh Hashanah service, part 2: the build-up to the shofar, the blessings over it, and Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot in Musaf Source: The Spanish and Portuguese mahzor Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Holland Park, Wednesday 25 September 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Building the drama of the shofar

The previous week covered the piyutim, the Shaharit Amidah and the Torah readings: Hashem remembering Sarah, and the binding of Yishak. Now, from page 106, "we are beginning the drama of the shofar."

"Et shaarei rason." A poem about 900 years old by Yehudah Shemuel Abbas, signed in acrostic: Abbas, Yehudah, Shemuel. It is sung in all Sefaradi communities, with the Spanish and Portuguese and Middle Eastern tunes slightly different but recognisably the same. It retells the Akedah with many Midrashim: Avraham tells Sarah he is taking Yishak to learn to serve Hashem, Yishak asks where the lamb is, and asks his father to tell his mother he is sorry to leave her after she waited ninety years for him. The refrain recalls "the binder, the bound and the altar." It ends by asking Hashem to listen to the sound of the shofar we are about to blow.

Why the Akedah before the shofar? The merit of Avraham's willingness; the tradition that it happened on Rosh Hashanah; and the ram caught by its horn in the thicket, which we recall through the shofar.

Saadiah Gaon's ten reasons. The Spanish and Portuguese mahzor prints Saadiah Gaon's ten reasons for the shofar on page 110. This is not said aloud; it is for reading and meditation. The Rabbi found it striking, because nowhere else in the Spanish and Portuguese liturgy are reasons for misvot printed; the shofar is that essential.

"Alah Elohim biteruah." A short poem whose refrain quotes Tehillim: God goes up with the sound of the shofar. The hakhamim and the Kabbalists explain that He rises from the throne of judgment and moves to the throne of mercy.

Two thrones

The Rabbi explained this briefly. Hashem has, as it were, tied His own hands. He created a world with Torah and misvot, and they have consequences. A parent who says, "If you run into the street without asking, you go to bed early," has to honour the rule or lose all authority. But on the child's birthday the parent can say, "I made the rules, and today we are suspending them." On Rosh Hashanah we try to do two things: - Sweeten the judgment: still in court, but with mitigating circumstances. - More effective: get Hashem to rise from the throne of judgment and sit on the throne of mercy, where the system of consequences is suspended. This ties in with Yihud Hashem: Hashem is unrestrained, and nothing has power over Him.

Then come verses from Tanakh containing the word shofar, read by the hazan and repeated by the congregation.

The misvah is to hear

"This is the misvah of the day." If you had only two minutes on Rosh Hashanah, because you were saving lives in hospital, you would not pray Shaharit or Musaf: you would hear the shofar.

The blessing is "lishmoa kol shofar," to hear the sound of the shofar, not to blow it. The Mishnah says that someone who blows into a pit and hears only the echo has not fulfilled the misvah. Hebrew "shema" means to hear, to listen and to accept. Hearing is sound waves on the eardrum; listening is letting them penetrate you. "Watch most debates today. People are hearing each other." The shofar is a raw, penetrating sound with no musical notes: let it in.

Shehiyanu. Sefaradim say Shehiyanu over the shofar only on the first day; Ashkenazim say it on both. Women say Shehiyanu on both nights when lighting for Yom Tov, since it greets the day. He always looks around on the second day for anyone who did not hear the shofar on the first, because for them Shehiyanu would be said again. Asked whether one could hear the shofar on one day only, he said no: both days of Rosh Hashanah must be kept, as they are even in Israel.

A three-day Yom Tov. That year Rosh Hashanah fell on Thursday and Friday, leading straight into Shabbat: "three days switching off from the world," which he said he loves. Asked about cooking, he explained that you may not cook on the first day for the second, but on the second day you may cook for that day and for Shabbat, provided something was prepared beforehand on Wednesday (the eruv tavshilin); and you may only light from an existing flame, such as a candle left burning for the three days.

The thirty seated blasts

Then come the first thirty sounds: tekiah, shevarim, teruah, tekiah and its combinations, as marked in the mahzor. We blow 100 sounds each day (101 in practice). The custom is to sit for these thirty, because we will stand for the later ones in Musaf. Someone who will not be present for Musaf, or someone for whom the shofar is blown at home, should stand for them if able.

From the blessing until the hundredth sound, after Kaddish Titkabal at the end of Musaf, it is forbidden to talk about anything unrelated to the shofar. Announcing page numbers is fine; idle talk is not. That is why the sermon is given before the shofar, even though the Sefer Torah is still out: once the shofar starts, no one may speak.

Holland Park's custom Among Ashkenazim and in the classic Spanish and Portuguese synagogues such as Bevis Marks and Wembley, the shofar is not blown during the silent Amidah of Musaf: thirty at the blessing, thirty in the repetition, and forty in the final Kaddish. Holland Park is a hybrid of Spanish and Portuguese and Middle Eastern tradition, and follows the Middle Eastern custom: thirty at the blessing, thirty in the silent Amidah, thirty in the repetition and ten in the final Kaddish.

Musaf: three long blessings

Musaf of Rosh Hashanah is the longest Amidah of the year, longer than any on Yom Kippur. After the first three blessings (the third with its theme of Hashem's unity) and "Atah vehartanu," come three long central blessings: Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot. Each has an introduction, ten verses (three from the Torah, three from Ketuvim, three from Neviim, and a concluding tenth) and a closing blessing. - Malkhuyot begins with Alenu. The Rabbi said it is a pity that Alenu, once said only on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, was later added to the end of every service; we have lost our sensitivity to an extraordinary prayer about all the world bowing before the King of kings. Nine verses contain "melekh"; the tenth is Shema Yisrael, because the essence of malkhut is Yihud Hashem. - Zikhronot: Hashem recalls everything, and on this day it is decided which countries will have war or peace, famine or plenty. The verses speak of Hashem remembering Noah and the covenant, ending with "Zakhor ezkerenu od" and a tenth verse on remembering the covenant from Egypt. - Shofarot recalls the shofar at Sinai, adds the whole of Psalm 150 with its instruments, and ends asking Hashem to blow the "great shofar," the shofar of Mashiah.

Birkat Kohanim in Musaf

Asked about the priestly blessing, the Rabbi said Birkat Kohanim belongs in every repetition of the day where it is possible. It is not said at night, or at Minhah, when people may have eaten and drunk. Since Rosh Hashanah has a repetition of Musaf, it should be said there too. He noted that Spanish and Portuguese practice does not, said he would find out the local custom, and that he intended to establish it at Holland Park.

Summary

  • Before the shofar, the service builds drama: "Et shaarei rason" on the Akedah, Saadiah Gaon's reasons, "Alah Elohim biteruah" and the shofar verses.
  • The shofar moves Hashem from the throne of judgment to the throne of mercy, beyond merely sweetening the judgment.
  • The misvah is to hear and truly listen to the shofar; from the blessing until the last sound, idle talk is forbidden.
  • Holland Park follows the Middle Eastern custom of blowing during the silent Amidah of Musaf.
  • Musaf is built on Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot, each with ten verses, and Malkhuyot is crowned by Shema Yisrael.
