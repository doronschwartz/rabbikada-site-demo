Where is the limit?

This was the last class of a series on Torah fundamentals, after classes on Sinai, the Oral Torah and evolution. Tonight's question, the Rabbi said, is "sometimes abused, sometimes overused, sometimes not used": when may we say that something in the Torah is a metaphor or an allegory, and did not happen as written?

He opened with the snake in Bereshit. Was there a real serpent, with sound coming out of its mouth, speaking to Adam and Havah? And if we may say that is a lesson rather than an event, what stops someone saying the Exodus never happened, or the binding of Yishak, or the revelation at Sinai? If the principle has no boundary, the whole Torah becomes a set of lessons.

He took a quick poll. Most in the room believed Bilam's donkey really spoke. Nobody believed that Hashem literally "went down" to see Sodom, or that He has eyes or an outstretched arm. So who gave us the licence to call one verse metaphorical and not the other? Someone mentioned that some writers today suggest the Avot were not historical figures.

He made two points before beginning. First, the class was only about the Five Books of the Torah. How to read the statements of Hazal in the Gemara and Midrash is a separate discussion. Second, he cited a public conversation between Rabbi Jonathan Sacks and Richard Dawkins, in which Dawkins pressed several times on whether the binding of Yishak actually happened. Dawkins complained that theologians decide on the spot what is symbolic and what is literal. The Rabbi's point was that we need clear lines: when yes, and when no.

Torah has many meanings, yet keeps its plain sense

Like a hammer. The Gemara (Sanhedrin 34a) teaches that just as a hammer breaks a rock into many fragments, one verse yields many interpretations.

Torah as poetry. The Netsiv writes that the Torah calls itself a shirah, a song ("write for yourselves this song"), and has the qualities of poetry. In a poem the idea is not laid out plainly, and sometimes what the words allude to is itself the simple meaning. That is not derash; it is how poetry works.

This, he said, is why the first translation of the Torah into Greek is mourned on Asarah BeTevet. In Hebrew, davar means both "word" and "thing": the word carries the essence of what it names. Translate it and you lose its many meanings. That is also why gematria makes sense only in Hebrew.

Ein mikra yose midei peshuto. The Gemara (Shabbat 63a) says a verse never loses its plain meaning. Yet the Gemara itself has cases where the literal reading is set aside: - In yibbum, the child "shall rise on the name of his dead brother" does not mean he must carry that name. - "Do not put a stumbling block before the blind" means do not give misleading advice. The Minhat Hinukh shows from the Rambam that someone who literally trips a blind person has done terrible things, but has not broken this particular prohibition. - "An eye for an eye" means monetary compensation.

The warning on the other side comes from Pirkei Avot (3:11): one who interprets the Torah against the halacha has no share in the World to Come.

Saadiah Gaon's four categories

Saadiah Gaon, in 10th-century Iraq, wrote Emunot VeDeot, the first systematic work of Jewish philosophy, and was the first to address this head-on. He lists four situations in which the Torah may be read non-literally, and says there is no fifth:

  1. The Hakhamim interpreted it. The Torah says forty lashes, and everyone agrees it means thirty-nine. "Do not cook a kid in its mother's milk" forbids all meat with milk. Here the Hakhamim had a tradition, and with it they could work the meaning into the verse in different ways, as with peri es hadar meaning an etrog. This category is closed to us today.
  2. One verse contradicts another. Chapter 1 of Bereshit reads as if man and woman were created together; chapter 2 shows Havah was formed from Adam. The first must be read in light of the second.
  3. It contradicts what we observe. Havah is called "the mother of all life". She is not the mother of lions and insects, so it must mean human life.
  4. It contradicts reason. "Hashem your God is a consuming fire" cannot mean God is fire, and the same applies to God's hand, arm or eyes.

Asked who may use the other categories today, without a Sanhedrin, he said it is not reserved for one great figure, but it needs a real understanding of the whole Torah.

He stressed that for Saadiah the Torah is presumed literal until it falls into one of these categories. Midrash is the reverse: "your starting point has to be: didn't happen, and it's coming to teach you something." Saadiah was strict because he had heard people allegorise the Torah absurdly, for instance reading the ban on lighting fires on Shabbat as a ban on armed battle.

But Saadiah leaves a problem. Every miracle contradicts observation and perhaps reason. Taken alone, his categories could dissolve every miracle in the Torah.

The Rambam's two tests

The Rambam was accused in his lifetime of not believing in the physical resurrection. In his essay on resurrection he wrote that he interprets allegorically only when the plain meaning is impossible, as with God having a body.

In the Moreh Nevuchim (II:25) he explains why he does not accept Aristotle's eternal universe. It is not because the Torah says "In the beginning God created". If it were proven, he would reinterpret those verses, and that would be easier than all the verses describing God physically. He gives two reasons:

  • Proof. That God has no body has been demonstrated, meaning beyond reasonable doubt. The eternity of the universe has only been argued, and an argument is not enough to override the plain meaning of the Torah.
  • Foundations. Reading the bodily descriptions of God as metaphor undermines nothing. Accepting a universe of fixed, unchanging nature would destroy belief in miracles, revelation and the whole Torah.

Applying the tests

  • The snake. There is proof that snakes do not speak, and whether it was a real serpent does not touch any foundation. "I don't have a problem with people saying the serpent wasn't a real serpent," though he added that he does not know.
  • Bilam's donkey. The same reasoning. The Rambam says clearly that the donkey's speech was in Bilam's prophetic vision.
  • Sinai, the Exodus, the splitting of the Sea. These are foundations. We have a festival built on them and mention the Exodus throughout our lives. They may not be allegorised.
  • The Avot. There is no proof they did not exist, and denying them undermines everything. "These are our Avot. We pray about them every day."
  • The binding of Yishak. In his view it does touch a foundation (it is central to Rosh Hashanah and the shofar), so it must be taken as having happened.

Angels. The Rambam (Moreh II:42) holds that every appearance of an angel in Tanakh took place in a prophetic vision. The three visitors who ate with Avraham, and the one who wrestled Yaakov, were seen in prophecy. Why then did Yaakov limp? Some explain that, like waking from a nightmare, the vision affected him physically. For the Rambam, angels eating a meal is as impossible as God having eyes. A malakh, the Rabbi said, is not a winged figure, which is a Christian picture, but part of the chain through which Hashem's will is manifest in the world.

The Ramban strongly objects. If it was all a vision, why tell us that Sarah baked cakes and Avraham prepared a calf? Why would Yaakov fear for his life? And if Lot's visitors were only a vision, how did the people of Sedom surround his house? The Ramban calls the Rambam's words contrary to Scripture, forbidden to hear, let alone believe. The Rabbi said the Rambam is consistent but "has got really strong questions". He himself did not see angels appearing in physical form as so absurd that it must be reinterpreted.

Summary

  • Torah has many layers, but every verse keeps its plain meaning unless there is a real reason to leave it.
  • Saadiah Gaon allows a non-literal reading only for the tradition of the Hakhamim, a contradicting verse, observation or reason.
  • The Rambam adds two tests: proof beyond reasonable doubt, and no harm to a foundation of the faith.
  • A speaking snake or donkey may be read as vision or allegory; Sinai, the Exodus, the Sea and the Avot may not.
  • "We're never going to have conclusive answers to anything, but we can have a framework."
