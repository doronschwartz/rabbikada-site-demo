Topic: Lighting candles too early, who may light, the wording of the blessing, whether to bless before or after lighting, and whether lighting accepts Shabbat

Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263:4-5 and 10, with the Rema and the Mishnah Berurah

Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 3 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (recorded around 2013-15, exact date not stated; the class falls in the Hanukkah season).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review: not too early, not too late

The class reviews the earlier laws of Siman 263. Forgetting to light carries a knas (a penalty), though one opinion holds it applies only if there was no light in the house. Even a poor person must ask for money to buy a candle. Women are more obligated than men, either because they are at home or because Hava extinguished the light of Adam.

Siman 263:4 says not to light too early. Before Plag HaMinhah, the lighting does not look like Shabbat lighting. But someone may light earlier if he accepts Shabbat at that moment, since it is then not a hakdamah (lighting ahead of time). The Biur Halakhah says that if a woman lit before Plag HaMinhah, thinking it was after, she must light again with the blessing. Rav Ovadia notes an opinion that a light already burning in the house is enough, but we do not rule like it, so she lights again.

A woman who must leave the house

A woman who has to go out after Plag HaMinhah, for a misvah event for example, cannot light and accept Shabbat, because she will still be doing melakhah. Yet if she lights early without accepting Shabbat, the lighting is too early. The Kaf HaHayim and other poskim say that in such a case it is better for her husband to light instead. If he is not available, her daughter. Men are also obligated in the misvah, and the preference for women is only an extra one. Only if there is no one else should she light herself, and that is not the ideal.

A woman who has become observant and never lit candles in the past does not have to light an extra candle for all the missed weeks, since the knas applies to someone who knows the importance and forgets, not someone who did not care about the misvah at the time.

The wording and Shehiyanu (Siman 263:5)

The blessing is "lehadlik ner shel Shabbat", whether a man or a woman lights, and whether on Shabbat or Yom Tov. Even if one lights several candles, the wording is in the singular, because the obligation is one candle. On Hanukkah we say "ner shel Hanukkah". The Ben Ish Hai says that on Yom Tov, too, one says "ner shel Yom Tov".

On Yom Tov, a woman does not normally say Shehiyanu at candle lighting. Some have a custom to do so (in Baghdad, for instance), but most poskim say that she should rely on her husband's Shehiyanu at Kiddush. A woman who has the custom can follow it. If she did say it, can she answer Amen to her husband's Shehiyanu at Kiddush? The Rabbi says yes: she is "dragged after" her husband (nigrar), just as women who are not obligated in the Sukkah still sit in it, and no one raises a problem with Amen. She should not answer "Barukh Hu uvarukh Shemo".

On Yom Kippur the Shulhan Arukh mentions that some say not to bless on the candles since we do not eat. The Rabbi says that the Kaf HaHayim records the Sephardi custom to bless, because the candles also prevent people from tripping, and one of the two reasons is enough.

Before or after lighting?

All other misvot are blessed before they are done. The Rema brings two opinions: some bless before lighting, and some bless after lighting, with the candles covered. He then rules that the custom of Ashkenazim is to bless after lighting. The reason is that some hold that the blessing itself accepts Shabbat, and after accepting Shabbat one may no longer light. So the woman lights, covers her eyes with her hand so that she has no benefit from the light yet, and then blesses. When she uncovers her eyes she is making the blessing before the benefit ("over la'asiyatan"). The Ben Ish Hai also mentions this, with a Kabbalistic reason for covering the eyes.

For Sephardim, the Shulhan Arukh says only "when she lights, she blesses", which suggests blessing before. The Rabbi notes that the Shulhan Arukh says "blesses first" explicitly for other misvot, so here it is ambiguous. Rav Ovadia is strongly of the opinion that the blessing is before, as for all misvot, and he brings many Rishonim. The Rema was defending the Ashkenazi custom. Yet the Ben Ish Hai, the Kaf HaHayim and the Hida all follow the Rema, and until Rav Ovadia the Sephardi poskim ruled this way, so many Sephardi women have a custom to bless after.

The Rabbi's conclusion, following Hakham Ben Zion: if you have a custom (for example your mother's), follow it. If you have no custom, follow the simple halakhah and bless before lighting. There is no clever way to satisfy both views.

The Magen Avraham worries that women will confuse Shabbat with Yom Tov if they bless after on Shabbat but before on Yom Tov, and says to bless after on Yom Tov too. The poskim reject this. On Yom Tov lighting is permitted after accepting the day, so the blessing is before, and a woman can remember the two practices.

If the room's electric lights are on, can she bless first, when she is not yet benefiting from the candles? Hakham Ben Zion suggests switching the lights off, blessing, switching them on and then lighting. The Rabbi will return to this with the laws of lighting.

Does lighting accept Shabbat? (Siman 263:10)

If the blessing accepts Shabbat, so too does the lighting itself, according to the Bahag. Then the woman may not continue doing melakhah. The Shulhan Arukh brings the Bahag, then adds that others say there is no acceptance until Barkhu or Mizmor Shir. Among those who accept it through lighting, some say a condition (tenai) removes it and some say it does not. The Rema rules that women accept Shabbat by lighting unless they stipulate otherwise, and that the condition works even if made only in the mind. For Yom Tov, a woman may light after accepting it, since lighting is permitted.

The Rabbi's summary, in practice:

Ashkenazim. She lights, then blesses after (with her eyes covered). Lighting already accepts Shabbat, so she may do no melakhah after, unless she stipulated beforehand. She also puts down the match and does not extinguish it. The Mishnah Berurah says that a man's lighting does not accept Shabbat for him, though it is still good for him to make a tenai.

Sephardim. She blesses before lighting. According to Rav Ovadia and Hakham Ben Zion, a woman accepts Shabbat neither through the blessing nor through lighting, so she may carry on until the printed time on the calendar. Even so, the Rabbi says it is good to make a tenai. Those who follow the Ben Ish Hai bless after lighting. If she forgot the blessing and only lit, she may still do melakhah.

On a Yom Tov, and for a man lighting, or for a woman who has made a tenai, the blessing is before lighting and the tenai works for both. The Mishnah Berurah says to make a tenai only when there is a need, and not every week for no reason.

A Hanukkah thought

To close, the Rabbi continues a theme from the previous week. On the first day of Creation Hashem made a special light, the Or HaGanuz, hidden away. The Greeks represented outward beauty, the physical light, while Shem represented the inner light. The Gemara says Shimon HaSaddik dressed in priestly garments before meeting Alexander the Great, to show that the Jewish people have outward beauty, but also inner. On Shabbat we change our clothes, as the gematria of Shabbat is the same as "yefeh toar" (fine form), and we dress the soul, not only the body. One of the Rishonim links the 36 hours of the Or HaGanuz with the 36 candles of Hanukkah.

Practical summary

  • Do not light before Plag HaMinhah unless you accept Shabbat as you light. If you lit too early by mistake, light again with a blessing.
  • A woman who must go out and do melakhah after Plag HaMinhah should have her husband (or daughter) light instead.
  • The blessing is "lehadlik ner shel Shabbat", even for several candles. Shehiyanu is not said at candle lighting, though some women with a custom say it on Yom Tov.
  • Ashkenazim bless after lighting, with eyes covered. Sephardim without a custom bless before lighting. If you have a custom, keep it.
  • Ashkenazi women accept Shabbat by lighting unless they stipulate otherwise. For Sephardim, following Rav Ovadia and Hakham Ben Zion, a woman does not accept Shabbat by lighting or by the blessing, but a stipulation is still good when she needs to do melakhah after lighting.
