Where we are

The Rabbi began at Seif 14 and said we are coming to the end of the laws of the zonah. In Siman 6 we are seeing the different problems that can come up in a Kohen's marriage. We have already seen the gerushah, and Siman 7 will be the halalah. Last time we saw the definition of a zonah and the laws of a woman who was raped. The second half of the class was one long sugya, the woman who is pregnant and unmarried.

Seif 14: a woman found not to be a virgin

The Shulhan Arukh says that a Kohen who betrothed a woman, and after the marriage found she was not a virgin (petah pasuah), has a doubt. The Rabbi said this is classical yeshivah material from the Gemara in Ketubot and the sugyot are very lomdish, but the law is straightforward.

An Israelite and a Kohen. For an Israelite it is fine, because there are two doubts. Perhaps it happened before the betrothal, and if it happened after, perhaps she was forced. So there is no concern. The exception is a girl betrothed before three, since a young girl's virginity grows back. If she was betrothed under three and is found not to be a virgin, we have to be strict, because we cannot say it happened before. For a Kohen it makes no difference. The doubt is whether she became a zonah after the betrothal, and the Kohen must divorce her.

Why not a hezkat kesherut? Tosafot in Ketubot asks why we do not say that every woman is presumed to be proper (hezkat kesherut), and so assume it happened before the betrothal, which is a lighter matter. Tosafot gives two answers.

  • There is a competing presumption, that things stay as they were until they change. She was a virgin, and we assume that continued, so the change happened just now, after the betrothal. The two presumptions cancel each other.
  • Even if it happened before the betrothal, it is still a wrong. The hezkat kesherut only works when one side is permitted and the other forbidden. When both options are an issur, we do not rely on it. The Rabbi said the grave prohibition of a zonah is far worse than the other, but Tosafot does not use that.

If she admits it. The Helkat Mehokek says that if she says "it was before the betrothal" and is certain, she is believed. If she keeps quiet or denies it, she is not.

An injury (makkat ez). She may say she lost her virginity by an accident, without a relation. Tosafot says this works, and that she is believed, because it is easy to admit and the fact that she did not say it in the first place proves nothing. The Maggid Mishneh holds that an accident is not common (lo shekhiah), so it does not enter the calculation, and she is not believed. The Rabbi said this is a big practical difference. Nowadays. The Rabbi said this seif is irrelevant today. An injury is common, with all the physical activity girls do, and women marry later. The Poskim say the hezkat betulim is no longer relevant.

A rumour about a Kohen's wife

If a rumour goes out that a Kohen's wife is promiscuous, we do not worry about it, unless it reaches a certain level. The Rabbi said this seif needs to be read together with Seif 4. He gave an example. Witnesses saw her doing something inappropriate. The husband begins to divorce her but dies before he can give the get. She is a widow, not a divorcee, so she may marry a Kohen. Even a husband who started the process, and even witnesses who saw her behave badly, do not make her a zonah.

A shem and a kol. The Shulhan Arukh uses shem here and kol in Siman 4. A kol usually means something Beit Din investigated and found to have some truth. A shem is people talking. The Bah explains the difference by saying that in Siman 4 the woman is married and here unmarried, but the Rabbi found this hard to follow. The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel say that here the Shulhan Arukh speaks even of a kol that Beit Din examined, and says it is still not enough on its own.

There are two kinds of kol. A kol delo pasik is a rumour everyone is talking about, non-stop. A kol muhzak is one that Beit Din investigated. For a Kohen's wife, the Rabbi said you need both together. A rumour that comes and goes once in a while, even if investigated, is a concern only according to how serious it is. His summary: if people are talking about a Kohen's wife, it depends how many are talking and whether Beit Din has looked into it. If the whole town is saying it and Beit Din investigated, then it is a real issue and it must be dealt with.

Seif 17: a pregnant woman who does not know the father

This is the big sugya, Seifim 17 and 18 together. The Rambam has his own approach, which is different from the other Rishonim, and the Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam in both seifim.

The case. A single woman is found pregnant and wants to marry a Kohen. She either says the father was a kosher man, or she was raped and does not know by whom. The question is whether we can trust her, and how much rov (majority) we need.

The Mishnah. A woman is seen having relations, or is seen pregnant, and no one knows who the man was. Even if she says he was kosher, it may have been someone who disqualifies her, such as a mamzer. The Gemara rules like Rabban Gamliel, that she is believed.

The Gemara's conditions. Shmuel says we do not follow Rabban Gamliel unless there is a rov kesherim. The Gemara then says that if she says the father was a kosher man (bari), she is believed. If she does not know (shema), the Gemara in another place needs trei rubei, two majorities: the majority of the townspeople and the majority of those passing through must be kosher. The reason is that the one majority is not enough. We rely on a rov only where the man came to her. But if she went to him, that is kavua (fixed) and the majority does not help. Since we cannot tell, we need both.

The Rabbi laid out three ways of explaining this:

Opinion

How they understand the two sugyot

Rashi

One rov is enough. The second sugya follows Rabbi Yehoshua, who never needs two

The Rambam

Both sugyot speak of a woman who is bari. Even then we need two majorities at the outset (lekhathilah). After the fact (bediavad), even if the majority is not kosher, she is fine

The Ramban and the Rashba

A bari needs one rov, and after the fact is fine even without it. A shema needs two rovim even after the fact. Without them she is forbidden

The Maggid Mishneh sees no difference between a bari and a shema in the Rambam. The Beit Yoseph reads him more strictly: with two rovim a bari is fine, but a shema is fine only after the fact.

The Rambam's addition. The Rambam adds a third requirement. It must have happened outside the city, in a field or on the road. If it happened in the city, even with two majorities, it does not work. The Beit Yoseph says it is a gezerah on a gezerah, and then rejects it. The Rabbi's understanding is that anything inside a city counts as kavua, even if the man left his house and went to hers, because that is not "separating" (perishah). Other Rishonim disagree and say that going to her inside the city does count as separating, so the usual rules apply.

The child. The Rabbi added that the Gemara says not only is she kosher, but her daughter is too. Her credibility works for the child as well, which is a hiddush.

Seif 18: a woman who cannot speak

The Shulhan Arukh then deals with a woman who is mute, deaf or mentally unwell, and found pregnant. She cannot say who the father was, so she is a shema. The Shulhan Arukh says she needs two majorities, otherwise she must leave. The Rabbi noted that the Rambam does not mention this case, and that the Beit Yoseph says that with two majorities she may stay.

The Shulhan Arukh brings both views: the Rambam as the main opinion, and the others as "some say". The practical result is that if a woman is pregnant and we do not know the father, the act must have happened outside the city for her to marry a Kohen. The Rabbi noted this could be a problem today, since most such cases happen inside a city. The main points are the disagreement among the Rishonim and the Rambam's two innovations: two majorities even for a bari, and outside the city.

Practical summary

  • A woman found not to be a virgin after betrothal is fine for an Israelite unless she was betrothed under three. For a Kohen it is a real doubt, and we do not use a hezkat kesherut.
  • Nowadays this is largely irrelevant, because injuries are common and women marry later.
  • A rumour about a Kohen's wife matters only if Beit Din investigated it and people keep talking about it. A husband who dies before giving a get leaves her free to marry a Kohen.
  • A single woman who is pregnant and says the father was kosher is believed after the fact, following Rabban Gamliel. Lekhathilah, two majorities are needed.
  • If she does not know who the father was, the Ramban and the Rashba need two majorities even after the fact.
  • According to the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh, the act must have happened outside the city. A woman found pregnant by someone in the same city may not marry a Kohen.
