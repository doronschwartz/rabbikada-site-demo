Topic: The four fasts, the 17th of Tammuz, who is exempt from fasting, and what the Gemara itself forbids in the Nine Days and the week of Tisha BeAv Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Simanim 549-551 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Thursday 18 July 2024, the week before the 17th of Tammuz

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two classes before the summer

With two classes left before the summer break, the Rabbi set out to cover the main laws of the Three Weeks, Simanim 549 to 560, "speedily", pointing out the points most relevant today.

The four fasts (Siman 549)

The Shulhan Arukh says we must fast on the four fasts because of the tragedies that happened on them. The source is the verse in Zekhariah about the fasts of the fourth, fifth, seventh and tenth months, which will one day become days of joy. Yom Kippur is a Torah fast and a different subject. The Fast of Esther is missing because it is not in the Gemara: it dates from the Geonim and is a custom.

The point of the fast. The Rambam writes that these fasts are meant to awaken us to repair our deeds and those of our ancestors. Of Nineveh it says Hashem "saw their deeds", not their fasting. The Mishnah Berurah criticises those who sleep all day or go out to pass the time. That does not mean skipping the fast and "just doing teshuvah", but the day needs some thought and reflection about why we are fasting.

What happened on the 17th of Tammuz

Five things: Moshe broke the tablets; the daily tamid offering ceased during the siege; the walls of Jerusalem were breached in the Second Temple period; Apostomos burned the Torah; and an idol was set up in the Temple. Who set up the idol? The Rambam's wording suggests Apostomos; the Yerushalmi implies King Menasheh. The Mishnah Berurah leaves it vague.

Why not the 9th of Tammuz? In the First Temple period the city was breached on the 9th. We fast on the 17th, the breach of the Second Temple period, which is more relevant to us; fasting on both would be too heavy for the community. The Magen Avraham says a particularly pious person should also fast on the 9th. Other Aharonim disagree, and in practice this is not done.

"Do not breach the fence"

The Shulhan Arukh repeats that everyone must fast and that "it is forbidden to breach the fence". That phrase is used elsewhere for established customs. Why here? The Gemara explains that the obligation of these fasts depended on there being persecution. In ordinary times there is no obligation, which is why the Rambam calls fasting on them a custom. But the custom has been accepted by all Jewry, so it has almost the force of an obligation, hence the warning. Because its root is custom, though, there are leniencies.

Who need not fast

Pregnant and nursing women. The Shulhan Arukh is clear that they do not need to fast on the three minor fasts. The Rema records an Ashkenazi custom that they do fast unless in great pain, though the Rabbi understood that contemporary Ashkenazi rabbis generally tell them not to. For Sefaradim they do not fast, "and they shouldn't even try to start it." Tisha BeAv is different and was left for the next class.

  • Pregnant: the Mishnah Berurah discusses forty days or three months. The poskim say: as soon as she knows she is pregnant.
  • Nursing: Rav Ovadia Yoseph holds that "nursing" in Hazal means within 24 months of giving birth, the time the Gemara says it takes a woman to recover her strength, whether or not she is actually nursing. This matters most when Tisha BeAv is postponed to Sunday, which has the status of a minor fast in this respect. "That's generally how I advise as well."

A woman who feels strong and wishes to fast may do so; it has value, as sharing in a national tragedy.

The elderly. The Kaf HaHayim exempts the very old from the three minor fasts. How old is that? It depends on the person: a fit ninety-year-old who goes to the gym should fast; a frail seventy-year-old should not.

How the minor fasts differ from Tisha BeAv (Siman 550)

On the three minor fasts, washing, anointing, leather shoes and marital relations the night before are permitted, and the fast begins at dawn. The Mishnah Berurah brings Ashkenazi Aharonim who are stricter, avoiding a hot shower or creams. "We Sefaradim are not particular about this."

  • A fast that falls on Shabbat is postponed to Sunday, never brought forward, since one does not bring tragedy forward. The Fast of Esther, which commemorates nothing sad, is brought forward instead.
  • The 10th of Tevet can fall on Friday; one fasts until nightfall.
  • One may marry on a fast day, but the fast must be completed. A hatan and kallah within their seven days of celebration are exempt from a postponed fast. Only the couple, the guests are not.

Announcing the fast. The Shulhan Arukh, following the Abudarham, says the hazan announces the fasts on the Shabbat before, except Av, Kippur and Purim. Ashkenazim announce none. Many Eastern Sefaradim also do not announce the Fast of Gedaliah, since it would come on Rosh Hashanah between the shofar blasts and Musaf. The Spanish and Portuguese do, following the Shulhan Arukh.

The week of Tisha BeAv (Siman 551)

The title of the siman is "the week in which Tisha BeAv falls". There is no siman on the Three Weeks or the Nine Days, because very few restrictions apply from Rosh Hodesh by law; most are customs.

Postpone a court case. The Gemara says a Jew with a case against a non-Jew should avoid this period, "his mazal is bad". The Mishnah Berurah says all of Av; the Ben Ish Hai and the Hida say until the 15th. The Rabbi reminded the class that, as the Ramhal explains in Derekh Hashem, someone living in direct relationship with Hashem is not under mazal. The halacha is written for those who live in that framework.

"We reduce joy"

When Av begins, we reduce joy. Tosafot argue that since the Gemara gives no measure, it must mean cutting out joy entirely. The Rambam quotes the words as they are. The Rabbi's view: "Obviously it means the spirit." How could anyone measure 50 or 75 percent less joy? You do not walk around depressed for nine days; you are more serious and do not do what you normally do for enjoyment.

What the Gemara forbids from Rosh Hodesh

Restriction

Business

Reduce purchases for joy and luxury, such as a new phone or car that can wait. Ordinary work and livelihood continue.

Building

No building for pleasure (a swimming pool, a decorative garden). Normal refurbishment of your home may continue; building for a misvah, such as painting a shul, is permitted.

Weddings

Not during the Nine Days. Engagement is permitted, without a party.

"Those are the only things which are forbidden from the din during the Nine Days, for everyone." Anything else is custom.

Weddings in the Three Weeks. By law they are permitted until Rosh Hodesh Av; the custom not to marry is widespread among Sefaradim too. Where there is real need, a wedding may take place. The Rabbi once officiated at one authorised by the Beit Din. He added that by strict law the restriction concerns those who have already fulfilled the misvah of having children, but in practice weddings are not held then; this is already recorded in the Beit Yoseph.

The week of Tisha BeAv itself

In the week in which Tisha BeAv falls (that year, Sunday to Tuesday), the Gemara forbids haircuts and laundering, even for after Tisha BeAv, and wearing freshly laundered clothes, including tablecloths and towels. The Beit Yoseph says that with modern laundry this is probably forbidden by law.

The trick of wearing a fresh shirt briefly beforehand is "a bit dubious", since a shirt can stay fresh for hours in air conditioning. Better to keep wearing the Shabbat shirt until Tisha BeAv is over. Someone very sensitive may rely on wearing clothes long enough to sweat in them.

The Rema says Ashkenazim stop laundering from Rosh Hodesh and haircuts from the 17th of Tammuz. The Rabbi recalled hearing Rav Ovadia Yoseph say sharply that the Rema's custom on bathing, written in Poland, should not be applied in Israel's heat even by Ashkenazim. Sefaradim keep to the basic law: haircuts and shaving are permitted until the week of Tisha BeAv.

When Tisha BeAv falls on Sunday or is postponed to Sunday, the Shulhan Arukh rules there is no "week of Tisha BeAv" at all. A stricter opinion treats the preceding days as that week; Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul limits it to a postponed Tisha BeAv.

A rising mourning

The Rabbi closed with a mashal of Rav Shimshon Pincus. For a personal loss, mourning decreases: three days, seven, thirty, a year, helping us move on from grief. For the Temple it increases, step by step to Tisha BeAv itself, because this loss is not felt naturally and we must teach ourselves to feel it. For Sefaradim the progression is clear. For Ashkenazim, he noted, almost nothing new begins in the week of Tisha BeAv itself.

Practical summary

  • The four fasts are a universally accepted custom with near-obligatory force; use them for reflection and teshuvah, not just not eating.
  • Sefaradi pregnant women, from when they know, and women within 24 months of birth do not fast on the minor fasts; the frail elderly are exempt.
  • On the minor fasts Sefaradim may wash, shower and wear leather shoes; the fast starts at dawn.
  • From Rosh Hodesh Av the law restricts only luxury purchases, building for pleasure and weddings, and asks us to reduce joy in spirit.
  • In the week of Tisha BeAv: no haircuts, laundering or freshly laundered clothes, towels or tablecloths. Sefaradim may cut hair and shave until then.
  • When Tisha BeAv falls on Sunday, there is no "week of Tisha BeAv" according to the Shulhan Arukh.
