Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 6, Seifim 8-13 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 31 July 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi began at Seif 8. Last time we saw the views of the Rambam and Tosafot on what a zonah is, and today he wanted to see how the Shulhan Arukh rules. He said there is something peculiar in how the Shulhan Arukh brings it. In the second half of the class he moved to the zonah who was forced (ones), and to what happens when a woman says she was raped. The recording does not always give the seif numbers, so the grouping below follows the order of the class.

Seif 8: the Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam

The Shulhan Arukh says: "Who is a zonah? Any woman who is not Jewish-born, or a Jewish woman who had relations with a man she is forbidden to marry, with a prohibition that applies to every man." The Rabbi said this is almost word for word the Rambam. Three conditions matter:

  • The man must be one she can never marry.
  • The prohibition must be shaveh lekhol (it applies to all men). A prohibition that applies only to a Kohen, such as a widow with a Kohen Gadol, makes a halalah and not a zonah.
  • A niddah does not make a zonah. Relations are forbidden, but she may be married once she is pure. Even a woman who is promiscuous (a kedeshah) is not a zonah in this sense, because she could be married.

A puzzle in the Beit Yoseph. The Beit Yoseph understood that according to Tosafot and the Ravad, there is no prohibition at all for a Kohen to marry such women. That cannot be right, because Tosafot only says it does not make her a zonah. It makes her forbidden in some other way. The Rabbi said the real difference between the Rambam and Tosafot is small. It is whether a man who has relations with a convert, a mamzer or an Ammonite receives lashes. For the Rambam they fall under "zonah", and so there are lashes. For Tosafot it is a general prohibition that does not carry lashes. The Bah and the Helkat Mehokek make this point, and the Rabbi said the Shulhan Arukh's wording here is a little strange, because the passage only matters if there are lashes.

So there are two main approaches among the Rishonim:

Approach

What makes a zonah

Tosafot and Rashi

Only cases where there is a karet or death penalty and there is no kiddushin. Other cases, such as a convert, are learned from other verses

The Rambam

Everything falls under "zonah" through the idea of pegam

A promiscuous past, and the convert

The Beit Shemuel brings the Semag, who says we must be careful about a woman with a promiscuous past. The Maharshal seems to follow this, and the Beit Shemuel rules that a Kohen should not marry such a woman. Again, this is not literally a prostitute.

On the convert, the Rabbi recalled the Gemara's four views. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam: any girl born non-Jewish, even if she converted a few weeks later, is forbidden to a Kohen, and under three years old makes no difference. A Kohen who has relations with her receives lashes according to the Rambam, since she is a zonah. Tosafot also includes a convert, though the Ravad and the Rashba say it is a separate prohibition without lashes.

A giyur lehumrah. The same applies when we do a conversion only as a stringency. We write on the certificate that she is forbidden to a Kohen, since a doubtful conversion is treated like a doubtful divorce. Where a person is clearly Jewish but lacks documents and we only immerse for the record, each case is weighed separately. But where there is real doubt and we convert to be strict, a Kohen may not marry her. He gave the example of a child born through surrogacy or egg donation. Our Beit Din follows the source of the egg, while others look to the woman who carried the child. A conversion done in such a case remains a real doubt, and we would not allow a Kohen to marry her.

Seif 9: any kind of relation

The Shulhan Arukh says that any relation makes a zonah: full or not, natural or unnatural, willingly or by force. The woman must be at least three years old and the man at least nine. The Rabbi said the force clause leads to the next topic.

Seifim 10-11: the woman who was forced

The wife of a Kohen who was raped is forbidden to her husband. The wife of an Israelite who was raped may stay with her husband, because she was forced, but she is forbidden to a Kohen if she later becomes free to marry. The source is Rav Sheshet, who says it for the wife of an Israelite. Rabbah and Rava add the wife of a Kohen. The Baal HaMaor reads this as a dispute, so that according to Rav Sheshet a Kohen's wife who was raped may stay with him. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh read it as one law that applies to both.

Forced at the start, willing at the end. A baraita says that if it began by force and she then took part willingly, she is still considered forced. Rava says this is because a person cannot control the desire that follows the act. The Pithei Teshuvah brings an important point. If she did something active, such as drawing him closer, then according to the Maharshal this is no longer force, and she is also forbidden to her husband.

She offers herself to save her life. The Rabbi distinguished two cases. If the attacker says "submit or I will kill you", that is ones. If she proposes it herself because she fears death, then, according to the Beit Yaakov, that is not ones. She becomes a zonah and forbidden to her husband. He added that whether she is even allowed to do this is a dispute among the later authorities. The case of Esther was brought as a proof, but the Rabbi said Esther acted to save all Israel, which is a different matter.

When is she believed? If she says she was forced, in an open place, in a field, where no one could help her, she is always believed. In a city, where there are people nearby, she is believed if she fought back or screamed. A woman who neither cried out nor resisted in the city is not believed.

Seifim 12-13: when she says she was raped

A related question is what happens when a Kohen's wife comes to her husband and says she was raped. The Gemara says that if she says it and her husband believes her, he has to divorce her. If he does not believe her, the Rishonim ask why we do not accept her word, as she normally has neemanut (credibility) about herself.

The answer of the Rishonim. Tosafot and the Ran say that Hazal made a takanah that she is not believed, because she might say it just to get out of the marriage. The Shulhan Arukh rules that she is not believed as far as the husband is concerned. If he believes her, he must divorce her. If he does not, he may stay with her. But for the future, if her husband dies or they divorce, she is forbidden to every other Kohen, because she herself said it.

The Rabbi pointed out that a Kohen's wife is the only case here. For an Israelite's wife, her claim makes no difference to her husband. He also noted that a woman who says she willingly had relations in order to get a divorce is treated the same way: he need not believe her, and if he does, he divorces her without a ketubah.

In practice. The class asked about a Kohen's wife who comes home and says she was raped. If he believes her, the Rabbi said, there is very little to do and it is tragic. The only room is if he truly does not believe her, which is rare.

The Sis Eliezer. The Rabbi then brought a similar question from the Sis Eliezer. A woman who grew up not religious had relations with non-Jews before she married a Kohen. Does she have to tell her husband? The Sis Eliezer says no. The Beit Din does not need to believe her, so it has no duty to tell her to speak. If she tells him and he believes her, that is her own choice. The principle is that when we do not accept her word, there is no issur we must act on. A student asked whether she herself must avoid relations with her husband, since she knows the truth. The Rabbi said no, either because the issur applies only to him, or because we do not believe her. Since it is not clear there is an issur at all, she need not tell him.

He added that the Igrot Moshe would be seen next time.

Practical summary

  • A zonah is a woman born non-Jewish or a Jewish woman who had relations with a man she can never marry, by a prohibition that applies to all men.
  • A niddah does not make a zonah, and neither does a Kohen-only prohibition. That makes a halalah.
  • The difference between the Rambam and Tosafot is mainly whether there are lashes for relations with a convert, a mamzer or an Ammonite.
  • A conversion done only as a stringency still leaves her forbidden to a Kohen.
  • A woman who was raped is forbidden to her Kohen husband but may stay with an Israelite husband. She is forbidden to a Kohen afterwards.
  • If a Kohen's wife says she was raped, she is not believed against her husband if he does not believe her. If he does, he must divorce her. She remains forbidden to other Kohanim.
