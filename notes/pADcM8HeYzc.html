Topic: The order of Yom Kippur night: the opening piyutim, "Al Daat HaMakom," what Kol Nidrei really is, Shehiyanu, Barukh Shem aloud, and the customs of the night Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 619 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 21 September 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why this siman

The Rabbi explained that the previous year the class had studied the laws of forgiveness before Yom Kippur, so this year he chose "to go to the end": Siman 619, "seder leil Yom Kippur," the order of Yom Kippur night, in six paragraphs. "This will give us an insight into the structure of the service."

The opening piyutim: viduy at the start

Before the service, Sefaradi mahzorim open with piyutim such as Lekha Eli Teshukati. The Shulhan Arukh does not mention them, so why are they there?

The Gemara says viduy is said at Minhah on Erev Yom Kippur, before the final meal, in case a person chokes during it. That is the view of most Rishonim. The Ramban held that the real time for viduy is as the fast begins, and the Hakhamim moved it earlier only because people at the end of the meal are rushed and unsettled. The piyut is entirely a viduy. By saying it as Yom Kippur enters, we also satisfy the Ramban.

Someone asked why this was not in the Spanish and Portuguese rite. The Rabbi answered that it is a humra, not required by the letter of the law.

"Al Daat HaMakom": praying with the sinners

The Shulhan Arukh opens with the hazan's declaration: "With the consent of Hashem and the consent of the congregation, in the heavenly court and the court below, we permit praying with the sinners."

The Rabbi said this was composed by the Maharam of Rothenburg, the 13th century German sage and teacher of the Rosh, who famously refused to let his community pay an inflated ransom for him and died in prison. It is an Ashkenazi composition, yet like Lekha Dodi it spread to every community, including the Spanish and Portuguese and the Yemenites.

He was candid: "It does seem quite a strange prayer." It can sound demeaning to the people who have come, and it is "a much later addition, much later than Kol Nidrei," with nothing to do with it. Someone noted that in viduy we include ourselves among the sinners, while here we seem to point at others.

He cited the Gemara in Keritot on the helbenah, the foul-smelling spice among the eleven of the ketoret, which teaches that we need the sinners of Israel with us. The Maharsha comments that this is when there is a core of ten good people; then the sinners are added, and the diversity adds to the beauty.

Kol Nidrei: a legal declaration

"Kol Nidrei is not a prayer. This is a big mistake people make." There are five prayers on Yom Kippur, and Kol Nidrei is not one of them. It is "literally one paragraph," five or six lines, a dry legal declaration. Its author is unknown; it is not from the Talmud, and probably dates from around the sixth or seventh century.

The halachic source. The Gemara in Nedarim says that someone who wants to avoid the serious sin of broken vows should, at the beginning of the year, declare his future vows void. At some point Yom Kippur was chosen, the Rabbi suggested, because that is when everyone comes.

The academic theory. Kol Nidrei arose among Jews forced to convert, who returned secretly once a year and annulled the vows they had made. The Rabbi said he thinks the halachic explanation is more correct, though the haunting tune fits the academic theory well. Someone suggested the tune simply marks the start of the service. "Could be."

He compared it with Hatarat Nedarim, which is far longer only because of the additions. The essential part takes ten seconds. In Jerusalem, he recalled, Sefaradim say it every Friday morning together in about thirty seconds. "The more you add, the more you detract." The same applies to Tashlikh. The Mishnah Berurah writes that those standing beside the hazan for Kol Nidrei should be God-fearing people, since they act like a court.

Two texts

The Rabbi read the text closely:

  • The original text annulled vows "from last Yom Kippur until this one," retroactively.
  • Rabbenu Tam objected that vows cannot be annulled on Yom Kippur, and that a general annulment does not work for vows already made. He changed it to "from this Yom Kippur until the next": a declaration, a modaah, that future vows made without thinking of this declaration will not take effect. That needs no court and can be made on Shabbat or Yom Kippur.

For those who annul past vows, it should be done before nightfall where possible. Between sunset and nightfall it is permitted for a misvah need, and some poskim allow it later, but ideally before nightfall. Ashkenazim, whose text is a declaration, do not have this concern. The Rema still writes to say it while it is day, and to repeat it three times, each louder than the last, continuing until night.

Shehiyanu, and following the custom of the place

Shehiyanu is said at night, since Yom Kippur is a festival and there is no Kiddush. The Mishnah Berurah has each person say it quietly. The Rabbi said customs differ, and the custom he knows is for one person to say it for everyone. Someone praying alone at home must still say it, and one who missed it at night says it the next day, until Neilah.

The Rema mentions saying it over a cup of wine given to a child. The Rabbi said we do not do this, because it is bad hinukh for the child.

The Rema adds that where there are customs of tunes and texts, one follows the custom of the place. "You have to learn: you follow what they do." He had experienced the full gamut, from Kabbalistic Yom Kippur in Israel to Mizrahi services in London to the Spanish and Portuguese, "and you have to learn to just follow." What is needed on Yom Kippur is the five prayers.

Barukh Shem aloud and the customs of the night

Barukh Shem kevod malkhuto leolam vaed is said aloud on Yom Kippur (619:2). The Mishnah Berurah's reason is that on this day we are like the angels. The Rabbi gave a reason he prefers: Barukh Shem speaks of Hashem's unity being revealed in the world. All year our sins deny that, so we say it quietly and cover our eyes for Shema. On Yom Kippur, when we are pure, we can say it openly.

Two people beside the hazan, one on each side, like Aharon and Hur supporting Mosheh's hands against Amalek. They give moral support to the hazan, so they should be people who understand what is happening.

Standing all night (619:5). The Mishnah Berurah clarifies that this means during the service, and gives the reason of resembling the angels. He notes that women do not stand; asked why, the Rabbi said he did not know.

Spending the night in the synagogue (619:6) with Tehillim and song. Those who sleep there should keep away from the Ark, and the hazanim should not stay up, or they will lose their voices for the day.

The four Tehillim after Arvit. The first four psalms contain 310 words, the numerical value of keri, an emission. The Gemara speaks seriously of a man who has an emission on Yom Kippur night, while adding that if he lives through the year it is a sign he is righteous. The Rabbi mentioned the Ben Ish Hai's responsum in Rav Pealim allowing such a person to immerse quietly in the mikveh on Yom Kippur morning, if he is particular about this all year. The custom of the four Tehillim comes from the Arizal. He suggested it survived in Sefaradi mahzorim, after much Kabbalah was removed following the Shabbetai Sevi episode, because Yom Kippur customs are so entrenched, and because it looks like simply saying Tehillim.

He ended by noting how many of the beautiful prayers in our mahzorim come from the Hida, and promised to cover the day of Yom Kippur another time.

Practical summary

  • The opening viduy piyutim satisfy the Ramban, who held that viduy belongs at the start of the fast.
  • Kol Nidrei is a short legal declaration, not one of the five prayers of Yom Kippur.
  • Ashkenazim make a declaration for the coming year; the Spanish and Portuguese annul past vows; Mizrahim do both. Those who annul past vows should ideally say it before nightfall.
  • Shehiyanu is said at night; if missed, it may be said the next day until Neilah.
  • Barukh Shem is said aloud on Yom Kippur.
  • In any community, follow its tunes and customs rather than your own.
