Part A — Gozez (Cutting)

Definition

In the Mishkan, the lowest roof covering was woven from four-ply thread — three strands of wool and one of linen — and the wool came from shearing sheep. Its derivatives are very common: cutting hair, nails, peeling skin around the nails, removing a wart. Gozez = removing any appendage of the body. (Cutting a limb is not Gozez — that is the body itself.)

Combing and brushing hair

Combing isn't cutting — but it is a pesik reisha: an inevitable result is forbidden even if unintended (the Gemara's case: cutting off a chicken's head to give a child a toy — you can't claim you didn't intend to kill it). A brush with strong, thick bristles will inevitably pull out at least one hair — and even one hair is forbidden. So:

  • A hard, thick-bristled brush — forbidden (it shouldn't even be used on Shabbat).
  • A brush with soft bristles on a spongy base — the bristles bend under pressure, so pulling hair is not inevitable; a hair that does come out is unintended and permitted.
  • Even so, brush moderately — to look neat and respectable, not a vigorous, thorough brushing.
  • Ashkenazi custom: a special brush reserved for Shabbat. Sephardim need not — the weekday brush is fine if soft.

Plaiting hair; wigs

  • Plaiting hair is rabbinically forbidden as a form of building: the Torah says Hashem "built" Chava, and the Gemara says He braided her hair before bringing her to Adam. Undoing a plait is likewise forbidden (demolishing).
  • Plaiting a wig is not building (the hair is detached) but is rabbinically forbidden as weaving (oreg).
  • Combing a wig is permitted — the hair is already detached — unless the hair is badly matted (a Makeh Be'Patish issue).

Nails

Cutting nails is Gozez. A woman due at the mikveh on Friday night who forgot to cut her nails: the halacha is that she may ask a non-Jew to cut them. This rests on a recurring principle — shevut deshevut bimkom mitzvah: something twice removed from a Torah prohibition is permitted for a mitzvah.

  1. Asking a non-Jew is itself only rabbinic.
  2. Cutting nails one doesn't intend to use is, according to many, only rabbinic — melacha she'einah tzrichah legufah (in the Mishkan the wool was cut to be used).

A non-Jew may likewise comb her hair for the mikveh: a pesik reisha through a non-Jew is permitted. (Similarly, a non-Jew may open a fridge whose light will inevitably go on.) Q: Can she cut them herself with her teeth or hands? — The ruling is to ask a non-Jew.

A partly detached nail

Permitted to remove when all three conditions are met:

  1. It is painful or bothering you considerably.
  2. The majority of it is already detached (no longer "attached" in Torah terms).
  3. You remove it with your hand or teeth (a shinui) — not a nail clipper.

Alternatively, ask a non-Jew to use clippers. Q: what if someone normally bites their nails? — We follow how most people do things, and most cut their nails with clippers.

Skin on the lips: be very careful not to pull it off with your teeth — that may be the normal way to do it, and the Mishnah Berurah suggests it could even be a Torah prohibition.

Part B — Sove'a (Colouring and Dyeing)

Definition

The threads of the Mishkan's covering were dyed blue, purple and scarlet — so dyeing is a Torah melacha. It includes enhancing an existing colour, not only changing it: repainting a white wall white, or polishing black shoes (one reason shoe polish is forbidden). Creating a picture is not dyeing but writing (Kotev).

Food and drink — ein tzevi'ah be'ochlin

  • Combining foods that happen to change colour — fine by all.
  • Even mixing foods to create a colour — e.g. jam into a toddler's white yoghurt to make it pink and appetising — is permitted: there is no dyeing with food, which is transient (and food wasn't dyed in the Mishkan). Accepted by Sephardim and most Ashkenazim.
  • Only for eating or drinking. Colouring food for business display, or colouring water as an ornament — forbidden by the Torah.
  • Food colouring may be used (Q from the floor) — it is colouring food.
  • Some (Ashkenazi stringency) reverse the usual order — e.g. cordial/syrup first, then water. Good to do, but strictly it is permitted.

Blood and stains on cloth

Wrapping a bleeding finger in a towel, or wiping red strawberry or pomegranate juice off your hands onto a towel, inevitably "dyes" it — but it is a pesik reisha of a destructive act you get no benefit from (pesik reisha delo nicha lei in a rabbinic matter), so it is permitted. The poskim add: if a tap is nearby, rinse first. A cut finger isn't an emergency — but there's no need to delay dressing a wound.

Make-up

  • Make-up on the skin is not permanent, so it is rabbinic — but all regular make-up and lipstick are forbidden.
  • Regular make-up is oil- or cream-based and adheres to the skin. Pure powder — no oil or cream — merely sits on the surface and is very transient. Most poskim — Chacham Ovadia, Rav Moshe Feinstein — permit it ("Shabbat make-up"); Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach was not happy with it.
  • Apply it only to a clean face, not over cream-based make-up from before Shabbat (which would rub the powder in).
  • Removing make-up is permitted.

Sunbathing to tan

Not a walk on a hot day — but deliberately sitting on a balcony for hours to tan. The room was split. The point: you are not doing anything; the sun acts on you — gerama (indirect).

  • A handful of poskim forbid it: dyeing is inherently a gradual process (like leaving cloth in a dye vat), just as cooking in an oven isn't "indirect".
  • The majority (Chacham Ovadia among them) permit sunbathing even with intent to tan.
  • But not if it becomes uncomfortable — sitting in discomfort just to show off a tan contradicts oneg Shabbat. And consider the spirit of Shabbat: half an hour reading in the sun, yes; a whole afternoon soaking up rays, no.
  • For vitamin D: what ordinary people do for enjoyment isn't forbidden as "medicine" (like prune juice for mild constipation), so it is permitted even for health.

Toilet colouring blocks

  • A block in the cistern — permitted by all: the new water is coloured only as it refills, an inevitable but indirect result (pesik reisha be'gerama) — like pouring water onto concrete that trickles onto grass.
  • A block in the bowl — flushing sends water straight through it. Debated: some call flushing indirect (you only lift a valve and gravity does the rest), or say the colour is too transient. Remove it before Shabbat if you can; if you find one at someone's home, there is plenty to rely on to use the toilet.

Q: forming shapes (with food) — a Kotev (writing) question, to be discussed later.

Summary

  • Gozez: no cutting hair, nails or skin. Only a soft, sponge-based brush, used moderately. No plaiting hair or wigs; combing a wig is fine.
  • For the mikveh, a non-Jew may cut a woman's nails and comb her hair. A painful, mostly-detached nail may be removed by hand or teeth. Don't bite skin off your lips.
  • Sove'a: no dyeing or enhancing colour (shoe polish). No dyeing with food and drink — colouring for eating is fine. Wiping blood or juice on a towel is permitted.
  • Regular make-up forbidden; pure powder on a clean face permitted by most. Sunbathing permitted in moderation and comfort. Cistern toilet blocks are fine; remove in-bowl ones before Shabbat if possible.
