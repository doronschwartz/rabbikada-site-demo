Closing the circle

The series had begun with Rav Uziel, then Hakham Gagin of England and Rav Messas of Morocco and Algeria. It ended with Rav Haim David HaLevi, "a very nice way to finish", because in many ways he continued Rav Uziel's path. Much of the material came from Rabbi Marc Angel's book on him, which describes him as a gentle scholar and courageous thinker.

His life

He was born in Jerusalem in 1923. His father, Moshe HaLevi, came from Turkey as a young man and was the hazan of the Greek-Turkish synagogue in Jerusalem; unlike the fathers of the earlier hakhamim, he was not a rabbi or dayan. He had one brother, Nissim.

  • He studied at Porat Yoseph, where Rav Uziel was the official head but the day-to-day teachers were the Syrian hakhamim Rav Ezra Attiah and Rav Yoseph Tawil, who did not always share Rav Uziel's views on Zionism or philosophy. He later wrote that he felt torn between these camps, but Rav Uziel won him over.
  • His contemporaries there included Rav Ovadia Yoseph and others who became great hakhamim in Israel and abroad.
  • He fought in the War of Independence and married Miriam Vaknin soon after.
  • After a year as rabbi of Romema, a Jerusalem neighbourhood, he spent two years as personal secretary to Rav Uziel, which he called the best years of his life.
  • He was then Chief Rabbi of Rishon LeSion for about 20 years, and from 1973, when Rav Ovadia Yoseph became Chief Rabbi of Israel, Chief Rabbi of Tel Aviv until his death in 1998. He was close to the Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Tel Aviv, Rav Yisrael Meir Lau.

He wrote the responsa Aseh Lekha Rav (eight volumes) and Mekor Hayim, and a clear, readable Kissur Shulhan Arukh for Sepharadim. Bar-Ilan University gave him an honorary degree, though he never attended university, and he won the Rav Kook Prize. He was a strong candidate for Sepharadi Chief Rabbi of Israel, the Rabbi said, but he did not want to be part of party politics, preferring a broader approach, as Rav Uziel had, and he was not appointed.

Rav Uziel's heir

He saw Rav Uziel as the model rav: intellect and knowledge, leadership and communication, and above all love and compassion, relating halacha to each person's life. He called him a continuation of the school of Bet Hillel, and in his acceptance speech in Rishon LeSion said he would follow Rav Uziel's ways. As secretary he saw that Rav Uziel answered every enquiry personally, never raised his voice, and kept his home open. "More than learning Torah," the Rabbi said, is seeing Torah practised by great hakhamim.

Where he agreed with Rav Uziel: - A mixed community's minhag. In the new state, communities from many places prayed together. Follow the majority's tradition; where there is no clear majority, follow the custom of Jerusalem. - A switch on Shabbat. In Salonika the power failed on Shabbat, and the warden wanted to turn the switch so the lights would not come back on uselessly. Rav Uziel allowed it, and Rav HaLevi agreed. - One wife, and halisah. When the state was founded, Rav Uziel agreed with Rav Herzog that Israeli law should follow the Ashkenazi ban on marrying two wives, and that halisah should take precedence over yibum. Rav Ovadia Yoseph strongly disagreed on both. Rav HaLevi sided with his teacher, for the sake of unity and because two wives are no longer accepted in society, though strictly by halacha it is permitted.

Where he disagreed: - Women being elected. He agreed women may vote, but was unsure they may be elected, and disagreed respectfully. - Tahanun on a bar misvah. Celebrating a bar misvah is relatively recent, and Rav Uziel held Tahanun is omitted. Rav HaLevi held there is no source to omit it. The Rabbi noted that today, with photographers and a meal, many places do omit it. - Shaving on Hol HaMoed. The Hakhamim forbade haircuts on Hol HaMoed so people would not leave them until then. Rav Uziel held shaving is included. Rav HaLevi, who had a beard himself, wrote that he could neither say it is permitted nor that it is forbidden. He never told anyone off for shaving and did not teach it in public: if it is hard for you, rely on the minority who permit it. "A good rav will give the tools to a person to make their own decision."

Rulings for a changing world

  • Men's fragrance. The Gemara, and the Rambam, say a man should not go out perfumed, but he allowed it, since custom has changed; synagogues even sprinkle cologne at a bar misvah.
  • Lost money. Money has no identifying mark, so the finder may assume the owner has given up hope. But with police and lost property offices, he wrote, people no longer give up on large sums, such as cash found in a taxi, so they must be handed in; small amounts may be kept. The Rabbi wondered whether people today would really expect a lost £50 to be handed in: it depends on the society.
  • A husband at the birth. Many poskim say a husband should leave the room once his wife is in labour. He wrote the opposite: his presence can be almost a matter of saving life, just as one may travel with her on Shabbat.
  • Women learning Torah. Women study every discipline today; why should Torah be different?
  • Shehiyanu for a daughter. The earlier custom not to say it reflected disappointment at a girl. Today parents rejoice equally, so it should be said. The Rabbi said he did so for his daughters.
  • A solar eclipse. The Gemara in Sukkah calls an eclipse a bad sign, so there is no berakhah. (The rainbow's berakhah is for the covenant, not the rainbow.) Today we know an eclipse is natural, so it deserves praise, but he was afraid to institute a new berakhah. Instead, read verses that praise God as maker of creation, intending them as a berakhah.

Sensitivity to others

At a wedding the rabbi says the betrothal berakhah for the hatan, so the hatan drinks; customarily the rabbi drinks too. Rav HaLevi once saw a hatan reluctant to drink after the rabbi, so he never drank himself, out of sensitivity, even if only one in a thousand minds. The Rabbi said he does drink first, and in 20 or so weddings no one has declined, but the story shows his sensitivity.

At a time when religious taxi drivers marked their cars with a sign showing they rested on Shabbat, he gave a speech on Shabbat observance, then got into a taxi with no sign. His student protested. He answered that his speech was to the public; he does not judge individuals, and perhaps being with this Jew would have some influence.

The Rabbi ended on the importance of loving one another. The series would resume in September, after a summer break.

Summary

  • Rav Haim David HaLevi rose from a hazan's son to Chief Rabbi of Rishon LeSion and Tel Aviv, and was Rav Uziel's spiritual heir.
  • He followed Rav Uziel on unity and openness, but disagreed with him on several rulings.
  • His responsa adapted halacha to changing realities: lost property, childbirth, fragrance, women's learning and eclipses.
  • Above all, he cared about the feelings of every person in front of him.
