Topic: How the Rambam reads Gemarot that seem to speak of bad luck, evil spirits, demons, charms and the evil eye Source: Gemara Yevamot 64b and Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer; Teshuvot HaRambam; Mishnah Shabbat 2 (Bameh Madlikin); Gemara Makkot and Rambam, Hilchot Sanhedrin; Rambam, Hilchot Tefillin UMezuzah VeSefer Torah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 22 July 2020, Rosh Hodesh Av

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The question

Invited to speak to a community on Rosh Hodesh Av, the Rabbi was asked for something philosophical. He chose the Rambam's approach to things we would normally call superstitious. Through five examples he shows how the Rambam takes a Gemara that on the surface means one thing and turns it around to give a completely different reading.

1. The isha katlanit

The Gemara in Yevamot discusses a woman whose husband died, who then remarried, and whose second husband also died. Rabbi says she may not marry a third husband. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says she may marry a third, but not a fourth. Normally a hazakah, a presumption, needs three times; here two are enough because it is a matter of life and death.

The Gemara gives two reasons: something in her body causes it, or, according to Rav Ashi, she has bad mazal. The Shulhan Arukh in Even HaEzer codifies it: she may not marry a third husband, but if she did, she need not divorce.

This raises hard questions. What does it mean that her bad mazal kills her husbands? And if a man's two wives die one after the other, nobody stops him marrying a third, fourth or fifth time. If her mazal is truly dangerous, why the difference? In those days it was also common: a girl married at 13 might lose one husband to war and another to illness, and at 20 she could never marry again.

The Rambam's teshuvah

The Rambam was asked about such a woman whose second husband died without children and left a brother. Does the misvah of yibbum override the concern about marrying her?

The Rambam answers that not only may the brother marry her, he should. He also expresses great astonishment that great rabbis who study Torah all day could ask such a question. There are three categories: what the Torah forbids (cooking meat and milk), what the Hakhamim forbid (eating meat and milk), and a third category that is only advice, not a prohibition at all.

In his words, this whole matter is superstition and imagination, not reality. Some people who are psychologically weak fall for it. If such a man feels unwell, he will start blaming his wife and think he is next. For him, better not to marry her. For someone who does not believe in this, there is no problem.

The Rambam goes further. We whisper in the woman's ear: quickly and discreetly make kiddushin with the man you want to marry, before any two witnesses, then come to the beit din and we will authorise the wedding. He says he and many others did this. On the Rambam's view she could even marry a fourth time.

The Rosh and others read the Gemara simply, as a real prohibition. The Rambam must have been criticised for his approach, but this is his attitude again and again: a Gemara that is hard to accept rationally is given a whole new reading.

2. Ruah raah

Every Friday night we read in Bameh Madlikin that one who extinguishes a lamp out of fear of non-Jews, robbers or ruah raah is exempt. A simple reading is an evil spirit.

The Rambam does not accept that there are spirits running around trying to harm people. He explains ruah raah as melancholy, depression. A person is alone and upset on Friday night, cannot sleep, and puts out the light. That is not the same liability as one who extinguishes in order to rekindle.

3. A warning from a demon

Capital punishment requires two witnesses and a warning, hatraah. The Gemara in Makkot says the warning counts even if the person warns himself in the witnesses' hearing ("I am not allowed to kill, but I hate him so much"), and "even from the mouth of a shed", a demon.

How would two witnesses hear a demon warn someone? The Rambam in Hilchot Sanhedrin explains: if the killer heard the voice of the one warning him but did not see him, it still counts. A shed here is a real person behind a wall, heard but not seen.

4. Names inside the mezuzah

The Rambam rules that writing God's name on the outside of a mezuzah is a common custom, and there is no problem with it. But those who write names of angels, holy names, verses or shapes inside have no portion in the World to Come. These fools nullify the misvah and turn a great misvah, which expresses the Yihud HaShem, into a talisman for their own benefit.

The purpose of the misvot is to recognise the oneness of God, not personal gain. One of the Rishonim (the Rabbi thought it was the Rosh) writes that a mezuzah put up for protection gives none. It protects when it does its job: reminding us each time we go in and out that HaKadosh Barukh Hu is with us.

5. Ayin hara

Hezek reiyah is real damage by looking, such as constantly peering over the fence while your neighbour is in his garden. Some wanted to treat staring at a neighbour's produce, counting the apples on his tree, the same way. The Rambam says not to equate them. Intruding on privacy is real damage. Staring at his apples is only ayin hara, and not to be taken seriously.

For the Rambam, putting one's hope in demons, spirits, red strings, special names and intermediaries is Avodah Zarah. They are crutches, and they distract us from a direct connection with HaKadosh Barukh Hu. The Rabbi noted that there are other approaches, but this was the Rambam's.

Questions and answers

Where did these beliefs come from? We were heavily influenced by the cultures around us. The hamsa against ayin hara is mentioned by the Ben Ish Hai and the Hida, but its origins are not Jewish, and the Rambam would never have approved. People should take their minds off these things and go straight to God.

Is ayin hara a form of jealousy? The Rabbi offered the most rational explanation possible: when one person envies another's success, it may cause Heaven to reassess whether he deserves those blessings. Nothing magical happens through a look, and he doubted the Rambam would accept even this.

What about Gemarot that seem to say the opposite? A participant raised the Gemara that says almost everyone in a cemetery died through ayin hara. The Rabbi agreed it is very difficult, and said the Rambam would not have accepted it. The Rambam also wrote a letter rejecting astrology as a fake science (unlike astronomy), even though some Gemarot speak of the mazalot, such as being born under Maadim. Some of these can be explained: a Gemara in Berakhot about harm in crowded gatherings fits germs spreading, as with Corona. Others are harder to explain.

A participant summed up: our lives are in God's hands, and pure emunah needs none of these. The Rabbi agreed, adding that our environment makes it difficult.

Summary

  • The Rambam reads "superstitious" Gemarot rationally: bad mazal as psychology, ruah raah as depression, a shed as an unseen person.
  • A twice-widowed woman may remarry; the Rambam calls the concern superstition and imagination, and tells her to make kiddushin quietly before two witnesses.
  • Names of angels inside a mezuzah turn a misvah into a talisman; a mezuzah protects only when it reminds us of God.
  • Charms, red strings and fear of ayin hara are crutches that pull us away from a direct connection with HaKadosh Barukh Hu.
