Where we are

Last time we covered the arusah and the penuyah who become pregnant, and a woman known to be promiscuous. Today we reached the end of Siman 4 and began the law of the asufi, the foundling, which takes four seifim. The rest of the Siman, on marriages between Mamzerim and a woman giving birth in a delivery room, is for later.

A reminder about a shetuki. We know the mother, who is before us, but not the father. The child is a safek Mamzer unless the mother says "lekosher nivalti" ("I had relations with a kosher man"), and then he is kosher. If she keeps quiet or dies, he stays a safek Mamzer.

Seif 31: what is an asufi?

An asufi is a child found abandoned, where we know neither the father nor the mother. He seems worse than a shetuki, though we will see that in some ways he is better. By default he is a safek Mamzer, because he was thrown away.

But there is no asufi if there is a hakhashah, a sign that he was not abandoned to die. The Rabbi gave the signs:

  • On the child's body: he was circumcised, or was cared for, anointed with oil, with eye makeup.
  • In the place: he was left somewhere that someone would find and look after him: a public thoroughfare, a tree near the city out of reach of animals, a synagogue near the city. The Gra points out that a synagogue by itself is not enough, it must be close to the city.

If there are such signs, he is not an asufi, and he is kosher.

Why is he not a shetuki? The Rabbi asked this himself. The child is still of an unknown father. The Nodah BiYehuda deals with this, as we will see.

Seif 32: when parents come forward

If the child was in a non-public place, without these signs, and someone comes forward and says "this is my child, I regret what I did", he is believed. If the father comes forward, the child becomes kosher. If only the mother comes forward, the child becomes a shetuki, because now we know who the mother is.

But if he was already picked up from the market and the name "asufi" has been established, the parents are no longer believed, and neither is a stranger who says "he is ours". The Gemara has one exception: a time of famine, or another clear reason (umdena) why the child was abandoned. Then the parents are believed even afterwards. The Rabbi said there is no mahloket on this seif: it comes straight from the Gemara in Kiddushin.

The Nodah BiYehuda: asufi against shetuki

The Rabbi presented the Nodah BiYehuda's comparison, which explains the Gemara.

Where the asufi is worse. Once the name asufi has been fixed on him, it stays, and even the parents cannot change it (except in a famine). A shetuki can always be fixed when the mother speaks: "lekosher nivalti" and she is believed. This is a small difference, the Rabbi said, but it is the one that is generally given. He asked why an asufi needs a separate category at all, and said it is only because of this.

Where the asufi is better. The Nodah BiYehuda goes further and says an asufi with a hakhashah is actually better than a shetuki. With a shetuki there is a problem with the mother: she did something improper, seemingly, until she speaks, since there is no man before us. So we are concerned he is a Mamzer. An asufi with a hakhashah was not thrown away, so we assume he is from a kosher couple, and he is kosher. The Rabbi said that when we learn Kiddushin it does not sound like this, since the usual order is Mamzer, then asufi, then shetuki.

A further chiddush. If Beit Din did not know about the birth, or had no chance to question the mother before she died, the child is now like an asufi, and since his mother cared for him he was not abandoned to die, so he is kosher. If Beit Din did ask her and she stayed silent, and then she died, he stays a shetuki and a safek Mamzer. The Rabbi said some ask on this, but it seems to be the only way to explain the difference between the two.

Seif 33: the Mishnah in Machshirin and the Rashi and Rambam

A child is found in a city. If the majority of the city is gentile, he is treated as a gentile, and if the majority is Jewish, as a Jew. Rav says this applies only to gabei aher (other matters), not to yuhasin. So what does it mean? The Rabbi explained that this is the basis of a big mahloket between Rashi and the Rambam.

  • Rashi: The whole discussion applies only to the question of Kehunah (whether he may be a Kohen or marry a Kohen). Rashi sees it as applying even to an asufi with a hakhashah. In a city with a gentile majority, he is a gentile, and a kiddushin he performs is nothing.
  • The Rambam: The discussion applies to everything, and it concerns an asufi with no hakhashah. Even in a gentile-majority city, we must always be concerned that he may be a Jew.

The Shulhan Arukh quotes the Rambam almost word for word and does not bring Rashi. The Rabbi noted a question the class raised: the beginning of the Shulhan Arukh's seif can speak of any asufi, even with a hakhashah, but the end can only speak about one with no hakhashah. If he had a hakhashah, there would be no reason to make him a safek Mamzer once he converts. The Rabbi said we must read the two parts as speaking of different cases.

Seif 34: practical rules for a person of doubtful status

The Shulhan Arukh lists how we treat such a person depending on the majority of the place where he was found. All of these rules are for matters other than yuhasin.

In a city with a gentile majority:

  • We may give him neveilah (non-kosher meat).
  • There is no duty of hashavat aveidah, returning his lost property as for a brother.
  • If his ox gores the ox of a Jew, he pays nezek shalem (full damages) rather than half damages. The Rabbi asked why he cannot say "perhaps I am a Jew, bring me proof". The answer is that the dispute is not about the money: the majority gives us an assumption about his status, and that status decides how much he pays. Also, he himself has no idea what he is.

In a city with a Jewish majority:

  • We must return his lost property. The Rabbi asked why we cannot say "bring proof that you are a Jew", since he is claiming the property. The answer is that the one holding the item found it, so he has no proper hazakah against him.
  • We must feed him kosher food.
  • If a Jew gores his animal, there is a question of how much is paid. The Rishonim say that the Jew pays nothing, but Tosafot disagree. The Rabbi noted that this person ends up badly off either way: he may be paid nothing, and he may have to pay in full.

Half and half (mehsah al mehsah): he is treated strictly in both directions. We return lost property, do not feed him neveilah, and for damages the rules are the doubtful ones, so sometimes he has to pay and sometimes he does not. The Rabbi noted that the Rambam's text reads "kekhol", which makes better sense than some versions of the Shulhan Arukh.

Shabbat and saving life. The last two points, the misvah to save him and desecrating Shabbat for him when he is under a collapsed building, are the two points on which the Rema argues. According to the Shulhan Arukh, in a gentile-majority city we do not desecrate Shabbat for him, and only in a half-and-half place do we do so. This is based on Shmuel's rule that we do not follow the majority in a fixed group. The Rambam and the Tur differ. The Rabbi raised a difficulty: in Hilchot Shabbat the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema have the same law with no such difference, so why do they differ here? He gave the idea that the Rema follows the Rambam, and that here there is an extra doubt about whether the person is a Jew. The Rabbi did not give a final answer, and said he would send a summary sheet of all the laws in these seifim.

Practical summary

  • An asufi is a child of unknown father and mother, found abandoned. A sign that he was not abandoned to die (a brit, care, a safe place) means he is not an asufi at all and is kosher.
  • Parents who come forward are believed only if there was no fixed name of asufi, or in a famine.
  • A shetuki can be fixed when the mother speaks. An asufi cannot, which is why he is in some ways worse.
  • Rashi limits the majority rule to Kehunah. The Rambam, followed by the Shulhan Arukh, applies it to everything: kiddushin needs a get, and a convert stays a safek Mamzer.
  • In a gentile-majority city we treat him as a gentile for food, lost property and damages, but not for yuhasin. In a Jewish-majority city we treat him as a Jew.
  • The question of Shabbat and saving life was left for a summary sheet.
