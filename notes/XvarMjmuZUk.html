Siman 676: The Order of the Berachot and the Lighting

Seif 1: The three berachot

On the first night: להדליק נר (של) חנוכה, שעשה נסים לאבותינו, שהחיינו. If Sheheḥeyanu was missed on the first night, say it on the second, third, even the eighth night.

Recap and a new point: "נר חנוכה" vs. "נר של חנוכה"

  • The original text (Gemara, Rishonim, Rambam) is להדליק נר של חנוכה. That is what Ashkenazim and the Spanish & Portuguese say, and what the original Sephardim said.
  • Most Sephardi siddurim today say להדליק נר חנוכה, because of the Arizal: the initials ל-נ-ח spell נחל, a stream, which draws down a stream of shefa (Divine abundance).

The Ḥida's question: if so, why does everyone, including the women lighting Shabbat candles, say להדליק נר של שבת?

The answer: Shabbat has many aspects: the candles, the food, the dress. So "the light of Shabbat" is one aspect among many. Hanukah's whole essence is the light. נר and חנוכה are synonymous, so נר חנוכה fits.

Middle Eastern Sephardim say נר חנוכה, but the original Sephardi custom was נר של חנוכה. "Those should follow the minhag, whatever it is."

"בזמן הזה" or "ובזמן הזה"?

  • Ashkenazim and almost all Sephardim: בימים ההם בזמן הזה: miracles for our ancestors in those days, thousands of years ago, at this season, on 25 Kislev.
  • Spanish & Portuguese and some older texts: בימים ההם ובזמן הזה, "in those days and at this time," which can mean that we pray and hope for miracles now as well.

Follow your custom. Absent one, the general practice is בזמן הזה.

Forgot Sheheḥeyanu all eight nights: can you say it on the eighth day?

On the eighth morning someone realises they never said Sheheḥeyanu. Too late?

What is Sheheḥeyanu on: the festival, or the mitzvah of lighting? - On the festivals, the Gemara is clear: Sheheḥeyanu is on the day itself. On Yom Kippur we say it in shul, not over a candle. On Yom Tov, if there was no Kiddush, you'd still say it. On Sukkot, if you forgot until Ḥol HaMoed, you say it then and there, walking down the street. - Some poskim applied this to Hanukah: the berachah is on the special time, merely attached to the lighting, so you could say it on the eighth day. - We do not hold this. A ḥag has kedushah: it is from the Torah, a regel, with tosefet (adding to its sanctity). Hanukah is not a ḥag. It is a holy time in our calendar, but the Sheheḥeyanu is on the new mitzvah, which comes only once a year.

The main halacha: no Sheheḥeyanu on the eighth day without lighting, though one who did say it "would have what to rely on."

"Ḥag Ḥanukah Same'aḥ"? An aside

"What really bothers me: people who wish each other 'Ḥag Ḥanukah Same'aḥ'... It's a complete misuse of the word ḥag. It's not a ḥag."

A ḥag is one of the three regalim: Pesaḥ, Shavuot, Sukkot. Even Rosh HaShanah is imprecise: it is not a ḥag, nor a time of simḥah. Say "a good Yom Tov," "Shabbat Shalom," or anything else. When the Rabbi calls Hanukah a "festival," he means it "in a very loose sense: a special time in our calendar."

Seif 2: Finish all the berachot, then light

On the following nights you say two berachot. Say all the berachot before beginning to light.

Why does this need saying? Because there is an opinion that you should start lighting immediately after the first berachah, "to light," and say שעשה נסים while lighting. Its logic: שעשה נסים is not intrinsic to the act of lighting. It relates to seeing the lights (we will see that someone who only sees a menorah says it).

  • The custom of Beit El and the mekubalim, brought by the Kaf HaḤayim and the Ḥida, and followed by many Sephardim, was to start lighting straight after the first berachah.
  • The Rabbi: "Unless you are a kabbalist... it seems better halachically to wait to complete the berachot." While saying שעשה נסים and שהחיינו your mind should be focused, not "mucking around with the wicks."
  • The main halacha for both Ashkenazim and Sephardim: finish the berachot, then light.

Forgot the berachah until after lighting the first light?

Fourth night: you lit the first light and realise you never said להדליק. Normally, once a mitzvah is done, it's too late for its berachah.

A class member: the first light is the night's mitzvah, and it's done. The others are only hiddur. The Rabbi: but hiddur is still part of it. You may still say the berachah, because as long as you are engaged in the mitzvah, even its extra parts, you can bless.

Parallels: - Arba minim: you're told to hold the etrog upside down for the berachah, but people forget and pick everything up together. The mitzvah is technically done, yet you still bless, on the waving and the circuits, all part of one big mitzvah. - Tefillin: the mitzvah is tying the shel yad. You should bless first, but if you tied it and then blessed, it's fine; the remaining straps are still part of the mitzvah.

An aside on tefillin: the Shulḥan Arukh rules explicitly that you tie the shel yad, immediately put on the shel rosh without interruption, and only then wind the seven straps, which are not intrinsic to the mitzvah. "In practice, I don't know any community which does this. Even the Spanish and Portuguese." The Arizal said to wind the seven straps first, and that spread.

Seif 3: Birkat HaRo'eh, the berachah on seeing

Someone who has not lit and has no one lighting for them, and sees a Hanukah menorah, says שעשה נסים (and on the first night שהחיינו), but not להדליק, since they are not lighting.

Who qualifies?

The Rambam

The Rashba, the Ran (the halacha)

The condition

Simply not lighting yourself that night

Not lighting AND no one lighting for your home

Example

A Sephardi yeshivah student whose parents light at home

A traveller in a hotel that forbids lighting, single, with no household lighting for them

On yeshivah students: a Sephardi student financially supported by his parents (they pay for the yeshivah, food and plane tickets) is part of their household and does not light. "When I was in yeshivah, I didn't light a menorah. I relied on my parents." By the Rambam, seeing Ashkenazi friends light, he would say the berachah of seeing. The Rashba and Ran disagree: if your home is lit for you, it is as if you have lit.

The Beit Yosef writes that the Rambam reads the Gemara more precisely, but rules like the Rashba and Ran. So the situation is rare: you would need to have no way to light and no household lighting for you.

Continuing the case: that traveller said שעשה נסים and שהחיינו on seeing a menorah on the first night. On the second night, back home, he lights his own and does not repeat שהחיינו. Earlier we concluded you cannot say Sheheḥeyanu on the day itself without lighting, but in association with seeing the lights you can, and he has.

The husband who comes home at 1 a.m. on the first night

The first night is a big work event; he gets home at midnight or 1 a.m. As discussed, if he'll be home after 8 or 9 pm, his wife or children light at the proper time and he fulfils the mitzvah through them. On the second night he comes home in time and lights himself for the first time. Does he say Sheheḥeyanu?

Ruling

Mishnah Berurah (Seif Katan 16)

No. His wife did it on his behalf the night before

Ḥacham Ovadia

Yes, on the basis of a triple doubt (ספק ספקא)

Ḥacham Ovadia's reasoning: 1. Some poskim hold that since he did not actively perform the mitzvah, he says Sheheḥeyanu the next night. 2. A further opinion supports saying it. (The Rabbi was not entirely clear on this point.) 3. A general principle worth knowing: ספק ברכות להקל does not really apply to Sheheḥeyanu. It is a berachah of personal thanksgiving and happiness, so we are more lenient in saying it.

Ruling (Ḥacham Ovadia): say Sheheḥeyanu on the second night in this case.

Seif 4: הנרות הללו

After lighting, we say הנרות הללו אנו מדליקין על התשועות ועל הנסים ועל הנפלאות...

  • The Gemara does not mention it. It comes from Massechet Soferim (8th–9th century), "not part of the Talmud... a much later addition," printed at the end of one of the tractates.
  • Two texts exist. The Arizal required exactly 36 words, corresponding to the 36 lights of Hanukah (excluding shamashim), and changed the text accordingly. The old Spanish & Portuguese siddur and many old Sephardi siddurim (Syria, Iraq) have the earlier text.

The Syrian tune: "When I was younger and used to be particular about these things," the Rabbi would try to fit the Arizal's 36 words into the special Syrian melody, but it never worked. "Then I realised, actually, it's the wrong thing to do. You follow the minhag, and just say what your father said, even if it's not in line with the Arizal."

A participant noted that their siddur cites the Maharam of Rothenburg as the source of the alternative version. The Rabbi: that is the Ashkenazi version (with הנרות הללו itself, more than 36 words). The point is that before the Arizal there were various texts, and the Arizal specified 36.

Timing matters: say הנרות הללו only after the first light has been lit. "What I sometimes see, and you should be careful with: as soon as people finish the berachot they start saying הנרות הללו. That's wrong." The berachah must take effect on at least one light first. הנרות הללו is a lovely post-Talmudic text, not part of the berachot, and it would be an interruption between the berachah and lighting the first light.

Seif 5: Which direction?

Picture your menorah. On the first night you light the light on the right. Each night you add a light to its left.

Sephardim (Shulḥan Arukh), all Sephardim

The Vilna Gaon (some Ashkenazim)

Start with

The new light, the leftmost of the night

The same first light every night, the one nearest the doorway

Direction

Left to right

Right to left

Reasoning

1) Every turn should be toward the right. 2) Bless on the new light: a new night, a new miracle

Why bless on a hiddur light rather than the main one? And the berachah should be on the light nearest the doorway (סמוך לפתח), the more important mitzvah. Moving right is a principle, but a greater principle is to bless on the main light

Kenneth confirmed that some Ashkenazim always begin with the same light each night.

Siman 678: When Money Is Short

נר ביתו (נר של שבת) קודם לנר חנוכה, משום שלום בית. The Shabbat light takes precedence over the Hanukah light, because of peace in the home.

If you cannot afford, or cannot find, fuel for both, buy Shabbat candles.

Why not one candle for both? (A participant suggested אין עושין מצוות חבילות חבילות, not bundling mitzvot together. True, "but much simpler.") Their purposes are opposite. The Shabbat light is for benefit: you enjoy it and eat by it, which was its whole purpose before electricity. The Hanukah light may not be used.

Obviously: if you can afford one Shabbat candle and one Hanukah light, or two Shabbat candles, light one of each. Two Shabbat candles are nice, but one fulfils the mitzvah.

The Mishnah Berurah's puzzle (work it out yourself)

You have one Shabbat candle and one Hanukah light, and money for one more. It's the second or third night. A second Shabbat candle, or an extra Hanukah light?

Mark: the extra Hanukah light. The Shabbat candle already provides shalom bayit, and the extra Hanukah light is hiddur.

Exactly. The hiddur of Hanukah is in the Gemara; nowhere does it say to light two Shabbat candles. That is a much later custom. Paradoxically: one Shabbat candle comes before one Hanukah light, but after that, Hanukah lights come before a second Shabbat candle.

  • "But Shabbat is more frequent (tadir)!" Tadir decides only which to do first when you do both. Here you are doing both mitzvot.
  • A good point raised: even if you can afford only a second light on the eighth night, there is still value in lighting extra lights, even short of the full number.

Shabbat candles or Kiddush wine? (from Hilchot Shabbat, two years earlier)

Maria suggested Kiddush, since it is d'oraita. The Rabbi: "Your logic is sound, but don't forget: you can make Kiddush on bread." So buy Shabbat candles and make Kiddush on the ḥallah.

Hanukah or Kiddush wine?

יקנה לנר חנוכה משום פרסומי ניסא. Having secured Shabbat candles, spend the rest on Hanukah lights, not wine; make Kiddush on bread. The Rema: Hanukah lights also come before Havdalah wine.

The full hierarchy: 1. Shabbat candle 2. Hanukah light 3. Wine for Kiddush 4. Wine for Havdalah

"Even though of all these, Kiddush is the most important, Kiddush has an alternative." The Mishnah Berurah's caveat: all this assumes you have bread. With no bread for Kiddush, spend the money on grape juice.

Siman 679: Friday Afternoon: Which Candles First?

בערב שבת מדליקין נר חנוכה תחלה, ואחר כך נר שבת. On Friday, light the Hanukah lights first, then the Shabbat candles.

"Everyone does this, but it's actually not simple at all."

Behind it: the Ba'al Halachot Gedolot (Behag, about 1,200 years ago), the source of the custom for women to light Shabbat candles and then say the berachah: - Shabbat begins at sunset, or from pelag haminḥah whenever a person explicitly accepts it; after that, melachah is forbidden (at least rabbinically, possibly biblically; even then only a negative prohibition, not the full Shabbat penalty). - The Behag: lighting Shabbat candles is itself an acceptance of Shabbat. So light Hanukah first, or you would have accepted Shabbat and could no longer light.

Position

Ashkenazim

Follow the Behag: lighting Shabbat candles accepts Shabbat

Sephardim (Shulḥan Arukh)

Do not. Lighting Shabbat candles does not necessarily accept Shabbat, especially if one has in mind not to

The Rashba

Rejects the Behag, and says Shabbat candles first, because they are more frequent (tadir)

So why does the Shulḥan Arukh rule Hanukah first? The Ba'ḥ explains with a principle worth remembering:

When there is a dispute in halacha, even if you don't hold like that opinion, if it's easy to satisfy it and it makes no significant difference, do it. Why enter into a dispute?

The Rashba's tadir argument is counterbalanced (many disagreed with him, since there are considerations on both sides, such as pirsumei nisa), which leaves the Behag. We don't follow the Behag, but what's wrong with lighting Hanukah first?

If you lit Shabbat candles first by mistake (Shabbat has not yet begun):

Ruling

Sephardim, men or women

Light the Hanukah lights afterwards. Absolutely fine

Ashkenazim, a man

May still light

Ashkenazim, a woman

Should not light (her husband, or someone else, can). If she is alone with no one to light for her, she does not light

Enough fuel, and Minḥah

  • The berachah is said even in daylight, since it is from pelag haminḥah. The fuel must last until half an hour after nightfall, about an hour and a half, which is a problem with ordinary small candles. At least one light should last that long.
  • The Mishnah Berurah (Seif Katan 2): "some Aḥaronim" say to pray Minḥah early, then light Hanukah and Shabbat candles, then go to shul for Kabbalat Shabbat. The first to say this was the Pri Megadim, about 300 years ago. In the Rishonim's time the prevalent custom was to light at home, come to shul, light there and pray Minḥah.
  • The Pri Megadim's concern: praying the previous day's Minḥah after lighting for the following night looks like a contradiction. Some went so far as to say it is better to pray Minḥah alone than after lighting.
  • The Rabbi: very hard to accept. Minyan is a far older concept, and this idea is "300 years old at most." It is a good thing to pray early when you can (that year, with Boxing Day on the Thursday, perhaps many would be off work on Friday), but in a normal year it is fine to light at home and then pray Minḥah. It isn't really a contradiction: "I'm lighting it now in preparation for the night which is coming in half an hour's time," only because I can't light on Shabbat.

Siman 680: Protect the Lights on Friday Night

צריך ליזהר... שיניח דבר מפסיק בין הנרות לפתח: put something between the lights and the door, so that opening and closing it won't inevitably blow them out.

  • This applies on weekdays too (don't leave a menorah where it will certainly go out), but on Shabbat it is more severe, since extinguishing is a Shabbat prohibition.
  • It concerns a פסיק רישא, where extinguishing is inevitable or very likely. An unexpected gust when you open the door carries no responsibility from either the Hanukah or the Shabbat perspective.
  • "Use your common sense."

The old door-holder: some used to set the light in a holder on the door itself. On Shabbat that is impossible: each time the door moves, the oil moves toward or away from the flame, raising muktzeh and extinguishing or kindling issues. (There is an opinion based on פסיק רישא דלא ניחא ליה, but "it's not really relevant.")

Siman 681: Motza'ei Shabbat: Havdalah or Menorah First?

אין מבדילין בנר חנוכה, שאין נהנים לאורו. One may not use the Hanukah light for Havdalah, since one may not benefit from it.

Why? (from Hilchot Havdalah, learned about a year before) בורא מאורי האש is a berachah of praise, commemorating the benefit of fire given to Adam HaRishon. You may only say it when you are benefiting from the light. The Hanukah light may not be used, so it can't serve for Havdalah.

In the synagogue: menorah first

Light the menorah, then Havdalah. Normally we try not to do melachah until real Havdalah (even after אתה חוננתנו), and Havdalah is more frequent. But because of pirsumei nisa, and because after Havdalah everyone leaves, the custom is to light first. This is the universal custom in synagogues.

At home: both are acceptable

For Havdalah first

For the menorah first

Tadir: Havdalah is more frequent

Pirsumei nisa: "a beautiful mitzvah, a special mitzvah"

Better not to do melachah before Havdalah

At home it isn't public

"There's no correct answer here." דעביד כמר עביד, ודעביד כמר עביד: whichever you follow is fine. The general Sephardi practice, in the Rabbi's experience: Havdalah first at home, then the menorah.

Summary

  1. 676:1: three berachot. The original text is נר של חנוכה. The Arizal's נר חנוכה spells נחל, and differs from נר של שבת because Hanukah's whole essence is the light. בזמן הזה (most) vs. ובזמן הזה (S&P).
  2. No Sheheḥeyanu on the eighth day without lighting: it is on the mitzvah, and Hanukah is not a ḥag. (Please don't say "Ḥag Ḥanukah Same'aḥ.")
  3. 676:2: finish the berachot, then light (not the kabbalistic custom). Forgot the berachah until after the first light? You can still say it while engaged in the mitzvah (compare arba minim and tefillin).
  4. 676:3: the berachah on seeing applies only to someone with no way to light and no household lighting for them (Rashba, Ran, Beit Yosef). A husband whose family lit for him on the first night says Sheheḥeyanu on the second (Ḥacham Ovadia's triple doubt; Sheheḥeyanu is personal joy).
  5. 676:4: הנרות הללו is from Massechet Soferim. Say it only after the first light is lit. Follow your family's text, 36 words (Arizal) or not.
  6. 676:5: Sephardim begin with the new light and go left to right. The Gra: always begin with the light nearest the door.
  7. 678: Shabbat candle → Hanukah light → Kiddush wine → Havdalah wine. After one Shabbat candle and one Hanukah light, extra Hanukah lights beat a second Shabbat candle.
  8. 679: Hanukah lights before Shabbat candles, to avoid the Behag's dispute "when it's easy." Lit Shabbat candles first? Sephardim: still light Hanukah. Ashkenazi women: not. Pray Minḥah early if possible, but lighting before Minḥah is fine.
  9. 680: keep the lights from being inevitably blown out by the door on Shabbat.
  10. 681: no Havdalah on the Hanukah light. In shul: menorah, then Havdalah. At home: either, and Sephardim usually Havdalah first.
