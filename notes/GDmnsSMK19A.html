Recap

Chapter 5 was begun last week, but it is long and important, so the Rabbi starts it again. First, a quick review of the Introduction so far.

Chapter 1: Rav Saadiah thanks Hashem for the ability to write the book.

Chapter 2: why people make mistakes. Either they do not know what they are looking for, or they know but are too lazy to find it. There are four kinds of people: those who have reached the truth and are content with it; those who have reached it but doubt it; those who think they have reached the truth but have reached falsehood; and those who flip-flop from one belief to the next. That last group is what prompted him to write the book.

Chapter 3: why did God leave us with doubts at all? Because we are created beings, and everything in this world takes time, including the intellect. To demand instant understanding of Hashem is to demand equality with Him. Only Hashem knows things instantaneously; we have a process to go through. "Wise people are patient."

Chapter 4: the definition of emunah. A belief is something you have contemplated and analysed, and then set aside as something to refer back to. If you have never gone through the process, "then you can't say you believe or don't believe in anything."

"Your opinion, however strong you may assert it, cannot change reality. Wise people want to search for reality and then form their opinions around reality, whereas foolish people form reality around their opinion."

That is the difference between a hakham and a kesil, a wise person and a fool.

Part 1: Three Sources of Knowledge

Before he finishes his Introduction, Rav Saadiah says we must know the sources of truth, because the whole book depends on them. There are three:

Source

What it means

Example

1. Sense perception

Whatever we know through the five senses: sight, hearing, smell, touch and taste

I see smoke

2. Reason

Self-evident truths that arise in the mind

Truth is good and lying is bad

3. Inference

A conclusion forced on us by the first two

Smoke means there is a fire

The test of an inference

An inference is knowledge we must accept because the alternative would mean denying something we sense or something self-evident. You can see smoke; you know smoke only comes from fire; so there must be a fire underneath.

This is very strict reasoning. To make an inference, you must rule out every other possibility. If there is any other way to explain what you see, you have no inference.

Rav Saadiah's example is the soul. We cannot see it, but we must accept that a person has consciousness, "because without consciousness we would be pieces of flesh." All living beings have some kind of nefesh. We cannot see it, but we can infer it, "because otherwise it wouldn't make sense."

Who denies what

There have been people who denied each of these sources.

  • A very few deny the senses. How can anyone deny what they see? Some philosophers did, because of illusions such as reflections in water or your image in a mirror. Rav Saadiah will deal with them later in this chapter and in Essay 1. Whoever denies the senses must also deny the other two sources, since they are built on the first.
  • Many accept the senses but deny reason and inference.
  • Most deniers deny inference. The second source is less palpable than the first, and the third less than the second. "It's far harder to deny the senses than it is to deny inferential knowledge."

Arguing over what counts as an inference

There is another group: people who accept inference in principle but argue over what has really been proved. Each side "proves" what the other disproves.

The Rabbi explains (he says he had to do some research for this) that Rav Saadiah is alluding to two early Greek philosophers who were contemporaries and rivals. Parmenides taught that nothing truly changes and that all claims of change are illogical. Heraclitus taught the opposite, the doctrine of flux: everything is constantly changing and nothing is fixed.

Along the way he gives a warning: later in the book there will be deep geometry and astronomy. "If at any point I'm saying something which you know about, something which is not Torah, in terms of science, please feel free to intervene."

Part 2: The Fourth Source: Reliable Tradition

Now comes something important. "But we, the congregation who affirm the unity of Hashem," says Rav Saadiah, accept these three sources, and we add a fourth: authentic, verified, reliable tradition.

If your father tells you something that his father told him, and the tradition is authentic, that is a source of knowledge. "I haven't seen it, it's not my intellect, it's not something I can infer. I've just been told by my father. That in itself is a source of knowledge."

This fourth source is itself built on the first three, as Rav Saadiah will explain in Essay 3, where he devotes a whole discussion to what makes a tradition authentic.

Is tradition really like the others?

Someone pointed out that the first three are natural, while the fourth is of a completely different nature. The Rabbi called it a fair point, and said it is exactly why Rav Saadiah introduces it with "we, the congregation of believers," and why he insists on reliable tradition. He will address it directly later.

He added that reliable tradition is not only about Torah. Something your grandfather tells you, nothing to do with religion, also counts, if it meets the criteria Rav Saadiah sets out later. Here, though, he mainly means the Tanakh.

Part 3: The Tradition Confirms the Other Three

Rav Saadiah now takes a short detour. He uses the fourth source, the books of the prophets, to show that the first three are recognised in the Torah.

The senses

The Rabbi asked the class to think of a verse "we say on a regular basis" that counts the senses the idols lack. After some guessing, the answer: Hallel. "They have a mouth but cannot speak, eyes but cannot see, ears but cannot hear" (Tehillim 115).

The verse lists the five senses and adds two more abilities. These are not extra senses; they are subcategories of the five:

  • Movement: "they have feet but cannot walk." Through movement we also know what is heavy and what is light. Some philosophers wanted to make the sense of weight a sixth sense. Rav Saadiah includes it within movement.
  • Speech: "they make no sound in their throat." This is the ability to speak and to combine words, premises and proofs.

So Rav Saadiah holds there are only five senses. Others counted more, including, the Rabbi notes, Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi in the Kuzari, while the Rambam followed Aristotle's five.

Reason

The verse in Mishlei (8:7): כי אמת יהגה חכי ורשע תועבת שפתי, "for my palate speaks truth, and wickedness is an abomination to my lips." That truth is good and falsehood is bad is a self-evident axiom.

Inference

Iyov (18:4): הלמענך תעזב ארץ ויעתק צור ממקמו, "shall the earth be forsaken for your sake, or the rock be moved from its place?" Some things are as fixed as the earth and the rocks. You must accept them, even if they are not to your liking.

All knowledge begins with the senses

Iyov again (34:3): כי אזן מלין תבחן וחך יטעם לאכל, "the ear tests words as the palate tastes food." The verse compares knowledge to taste, telling us that all the sciences are rooted in what we can sense.

Tradition

And the verse affirms reliable tradition (Iyov 15:17-18): "I will tell you, listen to me... what the wise have told, and have not hidden, from their fathers."

A question: senses without intellect

Someone asked how the Rambam sees empirical knowledge, and whether the senses alone are enough. The Rabbi agreed that the senses are necessary but not sufficient. "Senses in and of themselves are nothing. If you have no ability intellectually to process it, then the sensual things you're feeling mean nothing." You can touch things in your sleep, or sleepwalk, and know nothing.

Part 4: Knowing Through the Senses

Having named the sources, Rav Saadiah explains how we actually learn from each one. "You'd think it's easy. One second."

Whenever an object makes an impression on a healthy sense organ through contact, we should accept it as true. There is one condition: we must be careful not to be trapped by illusions.

Rav Saadiah gives examples. Some people thought the figure seen in a mirror was a real form, created at that moment. "Don't forget, he's writing this 1,100 years ago." In truth, polished surfaces have a nature that shows things reversed.

Others thought the reflection seen in water was a real thing. Rav Saadiah explains the reflection by the depth of the water compared with the height of the thing reflected. If you do not understand how reflections work, you fall into an illusion. He also cites the verse "and Moav saw the water opposite them, red as blood" (II Melakhim 3:22).

The rule for the senses: accept what you sense, but guard against illusions.

Part 5: Knowing Through Reason

Anything that arises in the mind, free of defect, is true, but only once we know how to analyse and have completed the analysis.

The Rabbi asked what Rav Saadiah is referring to. The answer is Chapter 3, where he showed that finding the truth means eliminating every false option. "Not doing things half-baked, when you're left with half-baked theories."

Beware of dreams

We must also guard against dreams. There was a thinker who held that whatever you see, even in a dream, is true. The commentary names him as a Muslim theologian, a student of al-Nazzam. His example: if you go to sleep in Andalusia and dream you are standing in China, you should know that God took you to China in the night and brought you back by morning.

Rav Saadiah rejects this and lists the real causes of dreams:

  1. The day's events: what we thought about during the day.
  2. Food: hot or cold foods, too much or too little. As Yeshayahu says (29:8), the hungry man dreams he is eating and the thirsty man dreams he is drinking.
  3. Temperament: moist and hot temperaments tend to happy dreams, dry ones to sad dreams. Iyov says (7:13-14): "I thought my bed would comfort me... but You frighten me with dreams." "He wasn't just afflicted during the daytime. He was afflicted during his dreams as well."
  4. Sometimes something spiritual, very slightly mixed in.

"The point is, a dream is a dream." Intellectual knowledge means analysing, completing the process, and not falling into intellectual illusions such as treating dreams as real.

Is it harder today?

Someone observed that today videos can be doctored, and what we see on social media looks true at first glance. Working out what is real is much harder than in Rav Saadiah's simpler world.

The Rabbi agreed. He said that this is why it is so hard nowadays to be a well-rounded intellectual. The great Torah scholars of the past were also mathematicians and astronomers. "Saadiah, it's unbelievable what he knows. He wrote things on the calendar." Part of the reason is that there was less to grapple with. If Rav Saadiah were writing today, his list of illusions would include doctored videos and stage magic.

The answer, he suggested, is to lean on the fourth source: reliable experts in each field. That is why courts today use expert evidence. A judge cannot eliminate every possibility himself. "Sherlock Holmes: you have to eliminate everything."

Someone asked where the shape of the earth fits. Today we can see from satellites that the earth is round, yet some people still say the pictures are fake. That is the same problem: there is far more to eliminate nowadays, and so far more conspiracy theories than there must have been in the past.

Part 6: Knowing Through Inference

"Now this gets interesting." (A practical note: the paragraph breaks in the online text are inconsistent, so do not draw conclusions from them.)

When we sense something, and we can only accept it by also accepting other propositions, few or many, we must accept them. Otherwise we would have to deny our senses or our intellect.

One proposition

  • We see smoke, so we must accept there is fire. One cannot exist without the other.
  • We hear a human voice behind a wall, so we must accept there is a person there. The Rabbi noted that today a machine can imitate a human voice. "Clearly a lot of these things are outdated, but the idea is the same."

Four propositions

We see an animal or a person eat, and later excrete. To accept what our eyes have seen, we must accept at least four propositions. The creature must have the ability:

  1. to draw nourishment into its body;
  2. to retain the food until it softens;
  3. to digest it;
  4. to expel what it does not need.

We see the food go in and the waste come out, and we see that the creature lives on, so we must accept all four.

A whole science

The next few paragraphs go into astronomy, which the Rabbi asked the class to read ahead. Rav Saadiah's point is that from what we see of the moon, we are forced to infer not just one proposition or four, but a whole science. Inference can give us an entire discipline.

After that, Rav Saadiah ends Chapter 5 by warning of seven mistakes people make when they infer without care. "I told you at the beginning how he loves categories. Everything is so well categorised and boxed up."

Summary

  1. Three natural sources of knowledge: the senses, self-evident reason, and inference, which is what the first two force us to accept.
  2. Inference is strict: every other possibility must be ruled out. Of the three, it is the easiest to deny, and most deniers deny it.
  3. A fourth source for the believer: authentic, reliable tradition, itself built on the first three.
  4. The Tanakh confirms all three: the senses in Hallel, reason in Mishlei, inference and tradition in Iyov.
  5. How to use each source: trust the senses but beware illusions; trust reason once the analysis is complete, but beware dreams; accept whatever the senses and reason force on us, even a whole science.
  6. Today it is harder: with so much to eliminate, we rely more on reliable experts, the fourth source.
