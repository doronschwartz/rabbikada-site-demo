Topic: Resting and fasting on the tenth of Tishrei: work, the five afflictions and adding to the fast Source: The Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Shevitat Asor, Chapter 1 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at his Thursday-night halacha class, Thursday 17 September 2026, three days before Yom Kippur These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The laws of resting on the tenth

In past years the class learnt Yom Kippur from the Shulhan Arukh; this year the Rabbi turned to the Rambam, with Sukkot to follow the next week. The first thing to notice is the name. The Talmud calls the day Yoma. Yet the Rambam does not call this section Hilchot Yom Kippur but Hilchot Shevitat Asor, "the laws of resting on the tenth". The reason, the Rabbi explained, is that the atonement of the day was already covered at length in Hilchot Teshuvah, which the class has learnt in the past. Here the Rambam deals with the day itself: resting and fasting on the tenth. In his introduction the Rambam lists four misvot in the section's three chapters: a positive and a negative command about work (to rest, and not to do work), and a positive and a negative command about affliction (to afflict oneself, and not to fail to do so). Three passages in the Torah. Before opening the Rambam, the Rabbi went through the only three places the Torah speaks of Yom Kippur: - Parashat Aharei Mot (Vayikra 16), read on Yom Kippur morning. - Parashat Emor (Vayikra 23), read on the first days of Pesah and Sukkot. - Parashat Pinehas (Bamidbar 29), read as the maftir on Yom Kippur. Across these passages "you shall afflict your souls" appears five times, alongside the command to rest ("Shabbat Shabbaton") and the prohibition of work, both carrying karet. That number five will matter later.

Yom Kippur is like Shabbat (halachot 1-3)

Halacha 1. Resting from work on the tenth of Tishrei is a positive misvah, and doing work also breaks a negative one. Done deliberately, the punishment is karet; done by mistake, in Temple times, one brought a fixed sin offering. He also raised something from life. Many traditional Jews who drive on Shabbat would never drive on Yom Kippur. Yet by the letter of the law Shabbat is the more severe: a deliberate melakhah on Shabbat carries stoning, while on Yom Kippur it carries karet. He promised an explanation. Halacha 2. Every melakhah that carries a penalty on Shabbat is forbidden on Yom Kippur. Everything forbidden on Shabbat without that penalty, such as walking beyond the tehum, is forbidden on Yom Kippur too, and so is everything forbidden on Shabbat in action or in speech, like business talk or asking a non-Jew. "There's no difference between Shabbat and Yom Kippur," except in the punishment. The Rambam makes this comparison six times in one halacha. Five times he calls the day Yom HaKippurim, but in the first sentence, about deliberate work, he calls it "the tenth". The Or Sameah, Rav Meir Simhah of Dvinsk, about a hundred years ago, explained why. Someone who slips, or breaks only a rabbinic law, still has Yom Kippur and its atonement. But "if you consciously and willingly break Yom Kippur, it's not Yom Kippur for you." For him it is only the tenth day of the seventh month. This, the Rabbi suggested, may explain the instinct of the traditional Jew who keeps Yom Kippur above all. Of course it is better to do no melakhah at all, he stressed; but to break Yom Kippur deliberately is to lose the day itself. Halacha 3. The Gemara records one difference. On a weekday Yom Kippur, in the afternoon, one could prepare vegetables, crack nuts and open pomegranates, so that people would not be distressed about the meal after the fast. This never applied when Yom Kippur fell on Shabbat, and already in the Gemara's time the custom in Babylonia and the West was not to do it. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh rule that Yom Kippur is treated exactly like Shabbat. How could removing withered leaves ever be allowed, when it looks like borer? Tosafot say the permission was only to wash the leaves. For the Rambam, who reads it as removing them, the Maggid Mishneh says withered leaves are not truly waste; others say it is all one kind. The Rabbi preferred the explanation of Rav Kafih and others: the bad outer leaves are plainly visible, so this is not a mixture, the condition for borer that the class learnt a few months earlier. Cracking nuts, the commentators explain, means crushing them without separating them out.

The affliction: eating, drinking and four more (halachot 4-5)

Halacha 4. The positive misvah of affliction means not eating or drinking. The Gemara in Yoma considers whether it might mean sitting in the sun; the Rambam explains in his Commentary on the Mishnah that our life depends on eating and drinking, so the soul is afflicted by going without. Eating or drinking deliberately carries karet; by mistake, a sin offering. Halacha 5. The Rambam adds that "from the tradition" we learn that washing, anointing, wearing shoes and marital relations are also forbidden, citing "Shabbaton", a rest from these too. Yet the punishment is makkat mardut, rabbinic lashes, with no karet and no sin offering. So are these four from the Torah or from the rabbis? Many Rishonim, among them the Rosh and the Tur, say they are rabbinic: the Sages built them on the five repetitions of "afflict your souls". The Rambam himself wrote in his Commentary on the Mishnah, in his twenties, that they are rabbinic and the verse is only a support, and in Sefer HaMisvot (196) he counts only eating and drinking. But here he says "from the tradition" and quotes verses, which sounds like Torah law. The Ran read the Rambam as holding they are from the Torah; others disagree. Someone asked how either reading fits the text. The Rabbi agreed: "both questions are valid." It is a real tension in the Rambam. For consistency, he leaned towards reading them as rabbinic. What is clear is that eating and drinking are forbidden by the Torah with karet.

Adding to the fast (halachot 6-7)

Halacha 6. Like work, the afflictions apply by night as well as by day. Then the Rambam adds: one must add from the weekday to the holy, at the start and at the end, as the verse says, "you shall afflict your souls on the ninth of the month in the evening". What is unique, the Rabbi pointed out, is that the Rambam applies this adding (tosefet) only to the fast. He reads "on the ninth in the evening" as going on the last phrase, the affliction. So for the Rambam there is no tosefet for melakhah: not on Yom Kippur, not on Shabbat, not on Yom Tov. "There is no such concept as Tosefet Shabbat," in his view. Other Rishonim learn tosefet from "Shabbat Shabbaton" too, and the Shulhan Arukh rules like them. | | The Rambam | Other Rishonim and the Shulhan Arukh | |---|---|---| | Adding to the fast | Yes, from the Torah | Yes | | Adding to the rest from work | No, on Yom Kippur or Shabbat | Yes, every Shabbat and Yom Kippur | How long? There is no fixed measure. The poskim give a minimum of about two minutes; London calendars bring the fast in about fifteen minutes before sunset, so with sunset at about 7.04 on Sunday the fast begins at 6.49. On the Rambam's view one would have to stop eating a couple of minutes before sunset, yet could still do work until sunset itself. Asked whether the adding applies to washing and shoes too, he said it does, rabbinically. Halacha 7. Women who eat and drink "until dark", not knowing they must add, are not rebuked: better they act unknowingly than deliberately. In Talmudic times, the Rabbi noted, there were no printed calendars to tell everyone the time. Two questions follow. First, a Gemara says this principle does not apply to Torah law, and tosefet is from the Torah. The Maggid Mishneh answers that it applies only to what is explicit in the Torah; tosefet is derived, not explicit. The Rema rules that where something is explicit, one must protest. Second, "until dark" means nightfall, so these women eat past sunset, possibly into Yom Kippur itself. The Mishnah Berurah reads it as until sunset. The Rambam meant nightfall, the Rabbi explained, because he holds that being strict in a doubt about Torah law is itself only rabbinic. The time between sunset and nightfall is a doubt, so from the Torah one may be lenient. "Fascinating."

What comes next

Chapter 2 covers how much eating counts and the laws for the ill; chapter 3 covers the other four restrictions, including when one may immerse in a mikveh on Yom Kippur. The Rabbi left these for another year and encouraged everyone to learn them over Yom Kippur.

Practical summary

  • Yom Kippur is treated exactly like Shabbat for every melakhah and every rabbinic restriction; the old afternoon permissions are no longer practised.
  • Deliberately breaking Yom Kippur means losing the day itself; in the Or Sameah's reading it becomes just "the tenth".
  • Eating and drinking are forbidden by the Torah; whether washing, anointing, shoes and relations are biblical or rabbinic is debated, even in the Rambam.
  • Stop eating before sunset; London calendars bring the fast in about fifteen minutes early.
  • The Rambam limits adding before and after to the fast alone, but the Shulhan Arukh rules that it applies to work and to every Shabbat as well.
