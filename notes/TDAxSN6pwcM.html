Topic: The laws, customs and ideas of Shavuot: Kiddush on the first night, preparing for the second night, staying up, dairy, greenery, Ruth, and two ideas about Sinai Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 12 May 2021, the last class of a four-week Shavuot series

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Kiddush on the first night

The series had covered Pirkei Avot, how much of the Torah was given at Sinai, and Ruth and conversion. This final class was practical. That year in London, Shavuot began on Sunday night, with sunset at about 8:45 and nightfall at about 9:45.

Every Friday night we add time to Shabbat, and many bring it in an hour early. You would expect the same on Shavuot. Until the sixteenth century, no one mentions otherwise. Then Rabbi Avraham HaLevi Horowitz of Prague, father of the Shelah HaKadosh, wrote in Emek Berakhah that bringing in Shavuot early shortens the forty-ninth day of the Omer, while the Torah demands seven complete weeks. So he required waiting until nightfall for Kiddush, though he allowed praying Arvit earlier. A century later the Taz (Rabbi David HaLevi Segal) required waiting for Arvit too. This became the widespread Ashkenazi practice. The Rabbi recalled four Shavuot nights in Gateshead, where Arvit began around eleven and dawn came very early.

Even among Ashkenazim there was dissent. Rabbi Yoseph Yuspa Hahn of Frankfurt wrote that the sages there prayed early, ate, and returned to learn, and that one who wishes to be careful should at least wait for sunset. Since counting the Omer today is rabbinic, from sunset one may treat it as night.

Among Sephardim, the Rabbi said, there was no such custom. Rabbi Efrayim Laniado, the Hakham of Aleppo in the eighteenth century, wrote that he had never heard of it, and Rabbi Hayim Palagi was lenient too. It spread among Sephardim mainly through the Ben Ish Hai, who was strict. Rav Ovadia Yoseph concludes there is no need to wait for nightfall. His summary of common Sephardi practice: pray Minhah and Arvit before sunset and make Kiddush after sunset. For elderly people or others for whom that is hard, strictly speaking one may make it even earlier, though it is better to wait for sunset.

Preparing for the second night

The second night has no such issue, since it is clearly the fifty-first day. In fact it is better to pray early. One may not prepare on the first day of Yom Tov for the second: heating food or setting the table on Monday afternoon for a meal after nightfall is forbidden. But if the meal begins on Monday evening before sunset, it is still the first day, so the preparation is for the same day, and the candles, the food and the table can all be prepared in the afternoon.

Someone asked whether one must wait for plag haminhah, about 7:15. Yes: candles and the meal must be after plag haminhah for it to count as the second night's meal. So one might pray Minhah around 6:30, Arvit after plag, and start the meal around 8.

Shehehiyanu at candle lighting

A woman lighting candles on Shavuot night can say Shehehiyanu then, since she brings in the festival. The problem comes at Kiddush. If a man then says Shehehiyanu in Kiddush, her "amen" may be an interruption, since she has already said it. So most authorities say that if she will hear Kiddush from a man, it is better to wait and fulfil Shehehiyanu through his Kiddush. If women are eating alone, they say it at candle lighting. The Ben Ish Hai records the custom, kept by some Iraqis, of saying it at the candles; but if there is no custom, waiting is halachically preferable.

The customs and their sources

Staying up all night. The earliest source is the Zohar, which speaks of the early hasidim learning through the night. It entered halachic works with the Magen Avraham about four hundred years ago and later the Mishnah Berurah, and from then it spread widely. Someone suggested it spread with coffee; the Rabbi thought the halachic codification was the real reason. Two caveats. First, it is a beautiful custom but not a requirement, and it must not come at the expense of Shahrit, Hallel and Musaf, or of spending the day asleep. One of his teachers stayed up as a boy, slept until six in the evening, and resolved never to stay up again, preferring to sleep and learn through the day. Second, the original practice was to read the set Tikkun: portions of Torah, Neviim, Ketuvim and Midrash, and then learn as one wishes.

In the morning, one who stayed up says all the morning berakhot except al netilat yadayim, and Asher Yasar only if he has been to the bathroom.

Dairy. This custom goes back to the hakhmei Ashkenaz of the thirteenth century and is recorded by the Rema, not the Shulhan Arukh. The main reason is the shetei halehem, the two loaves brought on Shavuot. Bread that has been on a meat table may not be used at a dairy meal, so eating dairy and then meat requires two separate loaves, recalling the two loaves. That shows, he said, that its founders never intended a fully dairy Yom Tov. On the festivals there is a misvah of simhah with meat and wine for those who enjoy them. His advice: have cheesecake after morning Kiddush, take a break, then wash for a meat meal, unless someone does not eat meat.

Greenery. The Maharil (fourteenth century) records greenery in the synagogue, recalling that Sinai bloomed for the Revelation, or the judgment on fruit trees at Shavuot. Flowers are always fine. But the Vilna Gaon strongly opposed bringing trees into synagogues because it resembles a Christian practice, and many Ashkenazi synagogues follow him. Sephardim generally rely on the view that it is permitted when done for a different reason, here for Shavuot and beauty.

The Azharot. Rabbi Shelomo Ibn Gabirol's poem lists the 613 misvot, but in poetic order, jumping between unrelated misvot for the rhyme, and his count is not the halachic one. The Rabbi suggested that studying the Rambam's Sefer HaMisvot, with its clear structure, would serve the purpose better.

Ruth. The story is set at the wheat harvest. But he shared a deeper reason. The Torah forbids a Moabite to enter the community. Shaul and Doeg used this to question David's lineage. What saved him was the Oral Torah's reading, "a Moabite man, not a Moabite woman." Without the Oral Torah, David would not be Jewish. Reading Ruth on Shavuot reminds us that the written and oral Torah are inseparable. The best custom of all, he said, is to learn Torah.

Let them climb the mountain

The people were forbidden to touch Sinai, yet the Torah says that as soon as the shofar sounds long, "they may go up the mountain." The Meshekh Hokhmah asks why. His answer: places and objects have no holiness of their own apart from Hashem. Sinai was holy because the Torah was given there, not the reverse; once the Revelation ended, it was an ordinary mountain. The same, the Rabbi said, is why Moshe's burial place is hidden. He warned against treating segulot like a vending machine, or a red string, or shrines, as if they work independently of Hashem: "That's a denial of Yihud Hashem."

The mountain overhead

The Gemara in Shabbat says Hashem held the mountain over Israel like a barrel: accept the Torah or be buried there. The Mekhilta reads the same words, "they stood beneath the mountain," as protection: the people were terrified, and Hashem held the mountain over them as a shelter. The Rabbi suggested the two complement each other, like a parent who insists out of love. We can experience misvot as a mountain hanging over us, or as Hashem's hand around us. That, he said, is what we must pass to our children: Torah is not a burden.

Summary

  • Ashkenazim wait for nightfall to make Kiddush on Shavuot night; common Sephardi practice is to pray before sunset and make Kiddush after it.
  • On the second night, a meal begun before sunset allows preparing the food and table in the afternoon.
  • Staying up, dairy and greenery are customs with sources; keep them in proportion and do not let them displace the main misvot.
  • Ruth shows that the written Torah depends on the Oral Torah.
  • Sinai had no holiness of its own, and the mountain over our heads is a shelter, not a threat.
