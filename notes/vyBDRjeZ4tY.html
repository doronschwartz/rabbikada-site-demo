Topic: Whether there is a duty to rescue a person who endangered himself, and who comes first when only one can be saved Sources: Sanhedrin 73a; the Rambam, Hilchot Gezelah VaAvedah 11:11; Yoma 35b; Ketubot 67b; the Minhat Hinukh; the Shevut Yaakov; the Hida; Rav Moshe Feinstein Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Monday 23 March 2020, his first lockdown shiur, joining his Wembley community with Tiferet Eyal and others

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The first lockdown shiur

This was the Rabbi's first shiur on Zoom, on the evening the first national lockdown was announced. For the first time, he said, he had brought together his Wembley community and his friends from Hendon, Tiferet Eyal and elsewhere. "Imagine this happening ten, fifteen, twenty years ago," before Zoom and Skype. "When God brings a difficulty to the world, He always sends the medicine," or at least a way to keep in touch.

He set out the week: contemporary halacha on Monday, the Rambam's Thirteen Principles on Tuesday (the ninth, that the Torah will not change), the laws of Pesah on Wednesday for the many people making Pesah at home for the first time, and the Haggadah on Thursday. He also asked who would want a virtual daily minyan, which would not allow Kaddish or the repetition of the Amidah but would keep a routine of praying together.

Three thoughts for the time

Before the shiur itself, he shared three thoughts.

Our precautions are a misvah. The Torah says "vahai bahem", live by the misvot, so we must protect our health. Staying away from synagogue may feel like losing misvot, but it is itself a great misvah. Most of us rarely have a chance to save a life unless we work in medicine. Young people who are unlikely to become seriously ill but still isolate are doing it purely to save others. "This is really an opportunity we have every single day now, each and every one of us."

Emunah and bitahon. He quoted the Hazon Ish: emunah is the theoretical understanding of God, bitahon is the practical test of it, actual trust. These are times that test our trust. The purpose of science is to bring us closer to God, not to replace Him. He told a story from the Ben Ish Hai in Ben Yehoyada: in a plague, the rabbi of a town agreed with the angel of death that five thousand would die. When fifteen thousand died, the angel said he had kept his word: five thousand died of the plague and ten thousand of fear of the plague. We must be vigilant, he said, but not let anxiety make us forget that there is a Creator in control.

Why the minyan was taken away. Perhaps because we have turned into a communal religion, when our relationship with God is fundamentally individual. He quoted Rav Shimshon Raphael Hirsch, who wrote that if he could, he would close the synagogues in Germany for fifty years, so that people would learn that religion is not confined to the synagogue. Now we have a chance to pray alone for as long as we want and build our own connection.

The question

The Rabbi then turned to a hard topic.

Suppose a person who has put himself in danger becomes critically ill, and there are not enough ventilators for everyone. Does a person who put himself in danger have the same claim to be saved? More generally: if someone recklessly endangers himself, on Shabbat for example, must we still break Shabbat to save him?

The case for no duty

The Gemara in Sanhedrin learns the duty to save a life from the misvah of returning lost property: if you must restore a person's money, all the more so his life. But the Rambam rules that if someone deliberately abandons his property, leaving his cow in an open shed or throwing his wallet into the street, there is no duty to return it. Likewise, the misvah to help someone unload applies only if he helps too. We need not care about a person's property more than he does.

The Minhat Hinukh, Rav Yoseph Babad of Poland, draws the conclusion: since saving a life is derived from lost property, someone who carelessly endangers himself is like one who throws his property away, and there is no duty to save him, and so no permission to break Shabbat for him.

The Shevut Yaakov, Rav Yaakov Reischer of Austria, brings a second proof. The Gemara tells how Hillel, too poor to pay for entry, listened to the shiur through the skylight on a winter Shabbat, fell asleep, and was found frozen. The sages said, "This man is worthy that we desecrate Shabbat for him." Why only for Hillel? Because he had put himself in danger for Torah. Anyone else who endangered himself would not be saved. On this view, endangering oneself for a misvah is different.

A third proof was brought from a rich miser who starves rather than spend his money: the halacha, following the Hakhamim, is that we need not give him charity. The Rabbi asked the class to reject it. His answer: the miser can rescue himself at any moment by buying food. The man drowning after swimming against warnings can no longer help himself.

The case for a duty

There are proofs to the contrary. The halacha is that a Jew who sold himself to non-Jews, or was captured for debt, is ransomed twice but not a third time, yet if his life is in danger he must be ransomed however many times it has happened.

The Hida was asked about a girl who drank poison on a Friday night. A kabbalist in the town broke Shabbat to write an amulet for her, and soon after she vomited it up and lived. The Hida ruled he was wrong, because Shabbat may be broken to save a life only by natural means, not supernatural ones. But clearly he held there was a duty to save her by natural means, though she had endangered herself.

Rav Moshe Feinstein ruled clearly that we must save anyone who endangers himself: a person who attempts suicide on Shabbat, or a fourteen-year-old who goes driving without knowing how. The proof from property fails, he explains, because your money is yours to throw away, but your body is not. You are only its guardian, its shomer. If someone entrusted with a watch is reckless with it, others must still rescue it. The Rabbi linked this to cremation: one of the main halachic objections is that the body is not ours to burn. That very afternoon, he noted, a law had been passed so that the government's emergency powers would not allow cremation against people's religious beliefs.

The halacha

Since this is a dispute about life, we must be stringent: we save the person, and break Shabbat if necessary, even if he endangered himself. Today, the Rabbi added, anyone who attempts suicide is regarded as not in control of their actions, so there is a duty to save them on every view.

However, because of the dispute, if a doctor can save only one of two people who both need immediate care, he should choose the one who acted prudently over the one who acted recklessly. "If it's going to take away from someone else's life," the careful person takes precedence. If the choice is between a reckless person in danger of death and a responsible person who is ill but not in danger, the one in danger comes first.

Asked about uncertain cases, such as a person on a rooftop who might be acting under duress, he was emphatic: "Of course we must save the life... There's no question about that." The rule of precedence applies only where we truly know what happened, which will rarely be the case. He ended with a plea to keep to the guidelines: every one of us can save lives by acting carefully.

Practical summary

  • Following the guidelines in a pandemic fulfils "vahai bahem" and the misvah of saving lives.
  • The Minhat Hinukh and the Shevut Yaakov suggest there is no duty to save someone who endangered himself, the latter except for a misvah.
  • The Hida and Rav Moshe Feinstein hold there is a duty, since our bodies are entrusted to us, not owned by us.
  • In practice we must save him, even on Shabbat; a person who attempts suicide is treated as not in control and must always be saved.
  • Where only one can be saved and the facts are known, the person who acted carefully takes precedence over the one who was reckless.
