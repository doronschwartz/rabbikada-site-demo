A question close to home

The series had covered the hostages, self-defence, collective punishment and civilian casualties, and honey traps. Those were fascinating, Dayan Kada said, but tonight's question is one that touches us directly. Parents of soldiers in the IDF are anxious every Shabbat. But the same question arises for anyone with a sick or elderly relative they cannot walk to: may they keep a phone on to hear if something goes wrong? And on 7 October, which was Simhat Torah in Israel and Shabbat abroad, Jews outside Israel heard nothing for a day and a half. (Where one can actually help, for example a doctor, there is nothing to discuss: one must.)

He dedicated the shiur to the memory of a beloved friend and pillar of the community, laid to rest the day before, who loved Torah and shiurim.

To answer the question he had to go back to a subject he had never analysed in a shiur: why is electricity forbidden on Shabbat at all? He warned that some of what he would say might be surprising or sound controversial, but none of it was his own opinion; all of it is in the literature.

Incandescent bulbs: a Torah prohibition

The light bulb was invented in 1879, and poskim in Europe soon discussed it. The Gemara teaches that heating metal until it glows red is forbidden by the Torah. The Rambam treats this as part of kindling a fire; the Raavad as part of cooking. Either way, the great majority of poskim ruled that switching on an incandescent bulb, which heats a filament until it glows, is a Torah prohibition. The same applies to turning it up.

The Maharsham disagreed, holding that the Torah prohibition applies only when one intends to affect the metal, so a bulb is only rabbinic. The majority does not follow him, though the distinction matters, for example when asking a non-Jew.

Rabbi David Hoffmann, the German posek, ruled that turning off or dimming such a bulb is only rabbinic, since extinguishing is a Torah prohibition only when done in order to reuse what was extinguished. So someone who is unwell in bed may ask a non-Jew to switch off the light.

LEDs and phones: five reasons, all rejected

Most of what we use today, LED lights, computers and phones, involves nothing red-hot. Why are they forbidden? Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach, the leading Ashkenazi posek in Israel in the second half of the twentieth century and an expert in technology, examined every reason offered, and rejected each one.

  1. Electrons are fire. An early argument based on primitive science. We know there is no flame.
  2. Building (the Hazon Ish). Completing a circuit turns useless wires into a working appliance. But anything made to be opened and closed is not building, just as closing your front door does not "complete" the house.
  3. Molid, creating something new. Hazal forbade creating a new scent (so one may not spray perfume on clothes on Shabbat, though one may on the body), and some extended this to creating a current. But a switch is routinely turned on and off, and we may not invent new rabbinic decrees.
  4. Sparks. Even if a switch sometimes sparks, it is unintended and not inevitable, so permitted.
  5. More fuel burned at the power station. Outside Israel this is indirect action by non-Jews; even in Israel, someone else may be switching off at the same moment.

He then read Rav Auerbach's conclusion verbatim: "In my opinion there is no prohibition from the Torah or from the Hakhamim to use electricity on Shabbat or Yom Tov... However, I am afraid that the masses will err and turn on incandescent lights on Shabbat, and thus I do not permit electricity absent a great need. The matter requires further analysis."

He stopped to be very clear: "I am, God forbid, not suggesting that it's permissible." He noted that Rav Ovadia Yoseph used to send his own teshuvot on technology to Rav Auerbach for comment. He did question whether the fear of incandescent bulbs carries the same weight today, when most of our lighting is LED.

Rav Asher Weiss: start from what is creative

Usually a posek assumes something is permitted until he finds a source forbidding it. Rav Asher Weiss argues that Shabbat works differently. The Yerushalmi tells that Rabbi Yohanan and Resh Lakish spent three and a half years on the laws of Shabbat and found thirty-nine derivatives for each melakhah; whatever they could not place, they put under makkeh bepatish, the final hammer blow.

So the method is reversed: first ask whether an activity is significant and creative, melekhet mahshevet, and then find where it belongs. Nothing is more creative or central in our lives than electricity, so it is forbidden by the Torah under makkeh bepatish. Even Rav Weiss limits this to significant use: the phone's reception changing as you walk does not count, and automatic doors would need thought.

The phone on Shabbat

A teshuvah from Eres Hemdah, where he trained as a dayan, dealt with an Israeli posted abroad who is connected to the army and might be urgently recalled. It allowed him to carry his phone within an eruv, and outside an eruv to use a smartwatch. (Carrying without an eruv is a far bigger issue than electricity.) On 7 October itself, in Israel, everyone connected to the army turned on their phones, and that is clear.

  • A sick or elderly parent. If you agree in advance that a call from that number means something urgent, and you could go to help, there is a strong argument to keep the phone on at home with the ringer loud, and to answer if that number calls.
  • Anxious for news of a loved one. This is far harder. Allowing it would be a big breach, and there is a beauty in being cut off on Shabbat and living in the moment. Anyone who truly struggles every Shabbat should ask an individual question.

He ended firmly: electricity has been forbidden for 140 years, and "if we make any breaches in this, it will ruin our Shabbat, not just for us but for our children and grandchildren."

Questions from the class

  • Why read the news abroad? It does not help anyone; an emergency alert where life is at stake is different.
  • Someone working for the CST? There would be strong grounds to allow them to stay informed, within an eruv or with a smartwatch.
  • Outside an eruv but in a karmelit? More complicated, since that is a stronger rabbinic prohibition.

He mentioned that Yom Tov is a separate discussion which he did not reach.

Practical summary

  • Switching on an incandescent bulb is, according to most poskim, a Torah prohibition; dimming or switching it off is rabbinic.
  • For non-incandescent electricity, Rav Auerbach found no clear prohibition but did not permit it without great need; most poskim treat it as rabbinic; Rav Asher Weiss as a Torah prohibition when the use is significant.
  • Someone with an ill or elderly relative may keep a phone on at home and answer a pre-arranged number, when they could go and help.
  • Reservists and those with a real role may, per Eres Hemdah, carry a phone in an eruv, or use a smartwatch outside one.
  • Keeping a phone on only for news of loved ones is much harder to allow; ask your own rav.
