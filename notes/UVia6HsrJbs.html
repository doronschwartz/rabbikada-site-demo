Topic: The wedding ring in kiddushin: why a ring, why a plain one, and whether a borrowed ring works Source: Gemara Kiddushin 2a and 7b; Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer 27-28 with the Rema; Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh and the Ran Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Torah Way's "Start Your Day with Torah", North West London, Tuesday 3 March 2020 (7 Adar)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why a ring?

The week's parashah, Tesaveh, is full of rings: the rings that hold the hoshen and the efod. That gave the Rabbi his theme, the ring in kiddushin.

The opening Mishnah of Kiddushin says a woman is betrothed in three ways: with money, a document or relations. Today kiddushin is done with money (kesef), and anything worth a perutah counts, whether coins, a dress or any object. But the Rema (quoting the Kolbo, and in the Darkhei Moshe) records the ancient custom, going back to the Rishonim and perhaps earlier, of using a ring. Today this is the practice throughout Am Yisrael.

Why a plain ring?

The mesader kiddushin always checks that the ring has no stones. Surely a diamond ring is worth more?

The source is the Gemara in Kiddushin (7b). A man betrothed a woman with a silk garment. Rabbah held it did not need valuing first; Rav Yoseph held it did, because a woman cannot judge its worth and relies on what the groom tells her. The Gemara concludes: "the halacha is that silk does not need valuing."

The Rishonim ask why the Gemara needed to say this, and why it says "silk" specifically. Rabbenu Tam answers: silk is different. Anyone can see roughly what a piece of silk is worth. Precious stones and pearls are another matter. A diamond can be worth ten pounds or a hundred thousand, and a bride might consent only because she thinks the ring is worth far more. Weeks later a friend in the diamond trade tells her what it is really worth, and the kiddushin is in doubt.

The Rosh and the Ran disagree about how far this goes:

The Shulhan Arukh brings both and is concerned for the Ran. That is why, for many centuries, the custom has been to use a ring with no stones, even though today no one states the value under the huppah.

Someone asked: could a bride mistake silver for white gold or platinum? The Rabbi was not sure there is a large enough gap in value between plain rings, and left it as an interesting question.

A borrowed ring

Now suppose the groom, or the best man, has left the ring at home. People assume a borrowed ring cannot be used. That is not so simple.

If he borrows a ring without telling the lender why, the Rosh lists four conditions for it to work: the ring is lent for a fixed period; the lender allows him to lend it on; the groom tells the bride it is borrowed; and he betroths her with the use of it for that period. The Baal HaItur objects that a gift that must be returned is not a real gift, and so the Shulhan Arukh rules that in such a case the kiddushin is only a doubt.

But if the lender knows the ring is being borrowed for kiddushin, the Rosh says it certainly works, and no one disputes it. The lender gives it with an open mind, in whatever way the halacha needs, as a loan or as a gift, so that this couple can marry. Afterwards they can settle between them what is owed.

Borrowing from a married woman

Most often the ring at hand belongs to a married woman. The Rema raises a doubt here: what a wife has may belong to her husband, and she may not be able to give it. Someone asked whether the bride or lender would even know these laws. The Rabbi answered that the Rosh's point is that the lender gives it "however it needs to work".

Coin or borrowed ring?

The Aharonim are a little hesitant, since the Rosh's ruling is his own reasoning rather than an explicit Gemara. They say that lekhathilah the groom should use his own ring. So, standing under the huppah with no ring, which is better: a two-pound coin from his pocket, or a borrowed ring?

Both would work. But the conclusion is that it is better to use the coin, which is his own money, and give up the custom of the ring, which is only a custom. If there is no money, a borrowed ring, lent knowing what it is for, also works. In practice, when someone under a huppah asks to borrow a ring, it is obvious what it is for.

Practical summary

  • Kiddushin works with any money or object worth a perutah; the custom is to use a ring.
  • The ring should be plain, without stones, because a bride may misjudge the value of a jewelled ring.
  • A ring borrowed without telling the lender why may leave the kiddushin in doubt.
  • A ring borrowed with the lender knowing it is for kiddushin works, according to all.
  • If the ring is missing, it is best to use the groom's own money; a borrowed ring is the second option.
