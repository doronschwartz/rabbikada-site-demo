"More precious to me than everything I teach"

In the Letter on Resurrection the Rambam describes great scholars, steeped in halachah from youth, who don't know the basic philosophical principles of the Torah. One even doubted whether Hashem has a body. So whenever his halachic writing gives an opening, he digresses into faith. At the end of his commentary on Berachot he explains:

ואין זה מקום להביא שם הדבר הזה, לולי אשר כוונתי כשיבוא זכר דבר בעניין האמונה לבאר בו מעט, כי יקר בעיני ללמד עיקר מעיקרי הדת ואמונה יותר מכל אשר אלמדהו.

This isn't the place for it, but my purpose, whenever something touches on faith, is to explain a little, because teaching one principle of faith is more precious to me than anything else I teach.

The same habit shows in the Mishneh Torah, which often ends a section with an idea to think about.

The plan: the Introduction to the Mishnah over May, June and early July; then the short Introduction to the Mishneh Torah, which links neatly; then, after Sukkot, through the winter, Perek Helek and the principles of faith.

Why this matters so much

The Introduction to the Mishnah answers the questions we usually take for granted:

- How do we know there is an Oral Torah?

- Why should we trust what the Sages said?

- What is the status of the Talmud?

- Why do the Sages argue so much, all the way through the Mishnah and Talmud? (Intellectual, halachic disputes, "not the political kind")

- What are aggadah and midrash?

- What are a Mishnah, baraita, Tosefta, Talmud?

"I promise you, if we study this properly, you'll come out with such a clear understanding of the Oral Law, of rabbinic arguments, of midrash. If I had ten things my son must do before he goes to yeshivah, this would be one of them. You can't go to yeshivah and not know this. Unfortunately I only learned it much later in life, and so many questions I had in yeshivah would have been answered."

About the text

  • The Commentary on the Mishnah was the Rambam's first Jewish work, written between about 20 and 23, and corrected throughout his life (we see this in the manuscripts). "Remarkable, when you think what he writes here, at such a young age."
  • Written in Arabic, so the masses could understand it; translated into Hebrew by different people for different parts. This introduction was translated by Rav Yehudah al-Harizi, the text used in class. ("Not the best text. Next week we'll use a different one.")

Then the people teach one another, write the mitzvah itself in scrolls, and go around until everyone can read it, and learn its explanation, which is the tradition (kabbalah).

Why the Rambam spells this out

He is building a picture of a yeshivah: the rosh yeshivah gives a shiur, the students analyse it, discuss it, go back and ask. Teaching passes from one to the next, and people discuss and pass it on.

"Four times": where have we seen that?

In the Letter on Martyrdom, mocking the rabbi who answered without thinking: anything said in public should be examined at least four times, and anything written, "theoretically a thousand." For the Rambam, you haven't properly learned something until you've learned it four times.

Written scroll by scroll

The Gemara debates whether the Torah was written as one book at the end of forty years or מגילה מגילה, scroll by scroll. The Rambam clearly holds scroll by scroll: each mitzvah was written when given, discussed, and then the next came.

"The whole Torah was given at Sinai": it's "not precise" to imagine Moshe received all 613 mitzvot and their explanation on Shavuot. On Shavuot he received the Ten Commandments; over the next forty days, Mishpatim; then he taught, went back for more. Pesach Sheni, the daughters of Tzelofchad, the laws in Bamidbar came later. "A process of forty years." (The Rabbi has a separate pre-Shavuot shiur on what "given at Sinai" means, with three or four explanations.)

Part 2: The Example of Sukkah

The Baraita (Sifra, Behar):

מה ענין שמיטה אצל הר סיני? והלא כל התורה כולה נאמרה מסיני! אלא מה שמיטה נאמרה כללותיה ופרטותיה ודקדוקיה מסיני, אף כולן נאמרו כללותיהן ודקדוקיהן מסיני.

Why mention Sinai with Shemittah, when the whole Torah was said at Sinai? To teach that just as Shemittah's principles, details and fine points came from Sinai, so did all the mitzvot.

("By the end of this introduction you'll know exactly what a Mishnah, baraita, Tosefta and Talmud are.")

What the verse says, and what Moshe was told

בסכת תשבו שבעת ימים: "In sukkot you shall dwell seven days." Four words.

What is a sukkah? What does "dwell" mean: sit, live, sleep, build my bathroom there? What does "seven days" mean? Minimum size, maximum, height, materials?

Told to Moshe orally

It applies to men, not women

The sick and travellers are exempt

The covering (sechach) must be something that grows from the ground

Not wool, not silk

Not utensils, even if from plants: pillows, cushions, garments

Eating, drinking and sleeping there is the whole obligation

Minimum 7 × 7 tefachim

At least 10 tefachim high

And so with all 613 mitzvot: the mitzvot in writing, their explanation by mouth.

You can't read the Torah without it

  • וקשרתם לאות על ידך והיו לטטפת בין עיניך: tie what, where on the hand ("the hand's a big place"), what are totafot?
  • לא תעשה כל מלאכה: "Your definition of work is different from mine. Some people will tell you going to the gym is melachah." Moshe was told there are 39 forbidden labours, and taught it.

Part 3: The Last Thirty-Seven Days

In the fortieth year, on Rosh Chodesh Shevat, Moshe gathered the people: "My time to die has come. Whoever heard a halachah and forgot it, let him come and I'll repeat it. Whoever has a question, let him come and ask."

הואיל משה באר את התורה הזאת. The Sifrei: כל מי ששכח הלכה אחת יבוא וישנה, וכל מי שצריך פירוש יבוא ויתפרש.

From Rosh Chodesh Shevat to 7 Adar, 37 days, Moshe did nothing but review and clarify. Then, at midday on 7 Adar (בעצם היום הזה), he went up the mountain.

His death was "for us": we lost him. משה רבנו לא מת אלא עלה ומשמש במרום: Moshe didn't die; he ascended and serves above. "These matters are very long, and not needed here."

Why stress all this? The Rambam is painting an intact transmission: forty years of mitzvot, each taught four times, discussed and written, then a final chance to review everything.

Part 4: Where Do Disputes Come From?

After Moshe died, Yehoshua and his generation studied what he had left.

כל מה שקבל משה... לא תהיה עליו מחלוקת.

Whatever was received from Moshe: there is no dispute about it.

Whatever they had not heard from the Prophet, the branches of those roots, they derived by reasoning, through the thirteen principles given at Sinai. Some of these rulings were agreed; on some a dispute arose: this one says so, that one says so. When a dispute arose, they followed the majority: אחרי רבים להטות.

The question

If the Torah was handed down with such care, taught four times and reviewed at the end, why is there so much argument?

The Rambam's answer, "so crucial, and different from how others understood it"

Don't make the terrible mistake of thinking disputes arose from "Chinese whispers": that as time passed, people garbled what was transmitted. "He goes very strongly against this." (More in a couple of weeks.)

Roots: given by Hashem to Moshe

Branches: derived later

Transmission

Moshe → Yehoshua → elders → prophets… (Avot 1:1)

Worked out by the Sages, and long before the Talmud, even in the prophets' time

Dispute?

Never

Possible

Sukkah

Eat, drink and sleep in it; the sick are exempt. "No one argues about that" (sleeping in the cold is "a medieval argument," much later)

E.g. how high a sukkah may be, if that wasn't given

Four species

פרי עץ הדר = etrog. "No argument"

How the branches are derived: the thirteen principles of Rabbi Yishmael (בשלש עשרה מדות התורה נדרשת), said every morning in Shacharit, "because the whole Oral Law is based on this." (Strictly there are 32; these are the main 13.) Different principles can lead to different conclusions, and that's where dispute comes from. "We'll see why it intensified later."

Organic growth. "Branches grow organically from the roots. An extrapolation can't contradict the roots." Recent movements that "took the roots and reinterpreted them": "it doesn't work like that. The roots, given from Hashem to Moshe, cannot be changed."

Coming later: if everything goes back to Sinai, what is the special category הלכה למשה מסיני? "We'll see."

Question from the class

If Israel hadn't sinned with the Golden Calf and the spies, they'd have entered the Land after a few months. Wouldn't Moshe then have received everything at Sinai?

"No. Moshe would simply have continued: he'd have entered the Land, and teaching would have gone on for years more. It's 100% clear Moshe didn't get the whole Torah on Shavuot. Pesach Sheni, Tzelofchad, Rosh Chodesh: just look at the Torah. It was an ongoing process."

Part 5: A Prophet Has No Edge in Interpretation

The Rambam now takes issue with Rav Saadia Gaon ("those on Monday nights know his Emunot veDeot").

ודע שהנבואה אינה מועילה בפירוש התורה ובהוצאת ענפי המצות בשלש עשרה מדות.

Prophecy gives no advantage in interpreting the Torah or deriving the branches through the thirteen principles.

What Yehoshua and Pinchas could do by study and reasoning, Ravina and Rav Ashi can do.

"Ravina and Rav Ashi, a thousand or more years later, far below Yehoshua and Pinchas in prophecy: in extrapolation they're equal. Don't tell me I'm a prophet, so my interpretation is better."

So what is a prophet's advantage? "חי נפשי, by my life, so much depends on this principle." But first he must explain the kinds of prophecy.

Miracles don't prove a prophet

Most people get this wrong, even some important ones, imagining that prophecy is established only by a wondrous sign: like Moshe's, like Eliyahu reviving the widow's son, like Elisha's miracles.

That's Rav Saadia Gaon (Emunot veDeot, essay 3): if he can't do miracles, he's not a prophet.

The Rambam: "No, no, no, no. It's not true." Eliyahu and Elisha didn't perform miracles to prove their prophecy, which was already established. They did them because the situation needed it (to revive a child), and Hashem fulfils the wishes of the righteous. Prophecy is proven by something else, which depends on dividing prophecy into two kinds: next week.

Summary

  1. The series: the Rambam's introductions, starting with the Introduction to the Mishnah: the Oral Law, its transmission, the Sages' authority, why they argue, aggadah and midrash. "One of ten things before yeshivah."
  2. The Rambam digresses into faith wherever he can: "more precious to me than anything else I teach." His four great digressions: Avot, Perek Helek, Mishneh Torah, Mishnah.
  3. Every mitzvah came with its explanation. Moshe taught each one so that everyone heard it four times; they discussed it, wrote it scroll by scroll, and passed it on. Sukkah shows how little the verse says alone.
  4. The Torah came over forty years, not in one day at Sinai, and in the last 37 days Moshe reviewed everything.
  5. What came from Sinai is never disputed. Disputes arise only in the branches, derived through the 13 principles, and were settled by majority. Not "Chinese whispers." Branches must grow from the roots, never against them.
  6. Prophecy gives no edge in interpretation: Ravina and Rav Ashi = Yehoshua and Pinchas. Miracles don't prove a prophet, against Rav Saadia Gaon.
