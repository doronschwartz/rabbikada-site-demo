Topic: Living in the Land of Israel, leaving it, and loving it Sources: The Ramban on the Rambam's Sefer HaMisvot; Ketubot 110b-112b; the Rambam, Hilchot Melakhim 5:9-10 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, in person and on Zoom, Wednesday 15 January 2025, the first class of a three-part community series

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why this topic

Dayan Kada explained that the community's education committee had asked for this subject. The word Zionism is used a great deal, he said, and many people who do not live in Israel call themselves Zionists, but not everyone knows what the Torah and Hazal say about our relationship with the land. He divided the class into three parts: living in Israel, leaving Israel, and loving Israel. He opened with a prayer for the country at a hard time.

Part one: is there a misvah to live in Israel?

The Ramban. Hazal tell us there are 613 misvot but do not list them. The Rambam set out fourteen principles for counting them, and the Ramban, in his glosses, argued with him where he thought something had been left out or wrongly included. One misvah the Ramban says the Rambam should have counted is to possess and settle the Land of Israel: "You shall possess the land and dwell in it, for to you I have given the land." The Ramban holds that this applies in every generation.

He brings proofs from the Gemara and the Sifrei:

  • If one spouse wants to move to Israel and the other does not, the Gemara rules that the one who wants to go can compel the other. If the wife refuses, he may divorce her without paying her ketubah; if the husband refuses, he must divorce her and pay it. "The one who wants to make aliyah always has the upper hand." Why compel anyone, unless there is a misvah?
  • The Sifrei tells of great Tannaim who reached the border on their way out of the land, looked back, wept and tore their clothes, quoting this verse. They were crying because they were losing the misvah.

Why did the Rambam leave it out? Rabbi Yishak de Leon, in his Megillat Esther, defends the Rambam in two ways. First, the Gemara relates that Rabbi Zeira avoided his teacher Rav Yehudah because he wanted to move to Israel, and Rav Yehudah held that going up from Babylon transgresses the verse "To Babylon they shall be brought, and there they shall be until the day I remember them." Second, and mainly, the Gemara's three oaths, learned from the three oaths in Shir HaShirim: that Israel will not go up "as a wall," en masse; that they will not rebel against the nations; and that the nations will not oppress Israel excessively. If there is an oath not to return en masse, the misvah cannot apply in every generation, and the Rambam counts only misvot that apply in every generation.

Dayan Kada noted that these same sources are the basis of the groups who oppose a Jewish state, a position he said we reject.

Answering the three oaths

He gave three answers.

  1. It is aggadah. This Gemara is a homiletic passage, and the general principle is that we do not derive halacha from aggadah.
  2. The other side broke the oath. In the laws of vows, if two parties swear to an agreement and one breaks it, the other is released. The Or Sameah, Rabbi Meir Simhah of Dvinsk, argued that the nations have oppressed Israel far beyond measure, so Israel's side of the oath no longer binds.
  3. With the nations' permission. The prohibition on going up in force applies only when it is not accepted by the nations. The Or Sameah wrote that after the San Remo conference of 1920, which recognised the Jewish people's right to return, the oath no longer applied. Dayan Kada added that this recognition was wider still after the Holocaust and the United Nations resolution, though the situation today is very different.

Someone asked why a Jewish-majority government is not considered sovereignty. He explained that the Rambam, following the Gemara, writes that the only difference between this world and the days of Mashiah is "subjugation to the kingdoms." We still do not have that full sovereignty, nor the Beit HaMikdash or the Sanhedrin, so we are still in some form of exile.

A different approach. Rabbi Eliezer Melamed suggests that the Rambam may agree there is a misvah but did not count it, because his fourth principle excludes commands that encompass the whole Torah. The Ramban himself writes that the main purpose of keeping misvot outside the land is so they will not be strange to us when we return, "for the essence of all the misvot is for those who dwell in the land of Hashem." In cold countries we only eat in the sukkah, though "you shall dwell in booths" means living there. "The Torah was given for Eres Yisrael."

The conclusion. The consensus of the poskim follows the Ramban. Rav Moshe Feinstein writes that most authorities hold there is a misvah to live in Israel, though not an obligation that would make living in the diaspora forbidden. He adds that someone considering the move should first consider whether he will be able to keep the misvot connected with the land. Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi expressed the feeling of the Rishonim in his poem "My heart is in the east and I am at the edge of the west." He left a full life in Spain to settle in the land and died soon after arriving.

Part two: leaving Israel

The Rambam rules that it is forbidden to leave the Land of Israel for the diaspora except for three reasons: to study Torah, to marry, or for a livelihood when it is hard to earn one in Israel. He explained that there were times when the centre of Torah was outside the land, which is why we follow the Babylonian Talmud.

What about a holiday? Dayan Kada said that according to the Rambam's words it seems one may not leave even for that. He mentioned the report that the Lubavitcher Rebbe never visited Israel because he would then not have been allowed to leave. Rav Ovadia Yoseph, normally lenient, writes in his responsa that one may not leave Israel purely for a holiday.

Others are more lenient. Rav Wosner, in Shevet HaLevi, argues that the prohibition is leaving in order to settle elsewhere, and not a temporary trip with a return ticket. Rabbi Eliezer Melamed adds that in earlier times leaving meant years away, while today one can return the next day.

What everyone accepts is that leaving permanently, without one of the three reasons and with no intention to return, is forbidden.

Someone asked about rabbis who travelled to raise funds for the land. Dayan Kada mentioned the Hida, who spent years travelling the world collecting for its inhabitants and kept a diary of his journeys, Maagal Tov. That is clearly permitted, as it was for Torah or livelihood.

Part three: loving the land

The Torah praises "a good land, a land of streams of water... a land of wheat and barley." The Rambam writes that the great sages would kiss the borders of the land, kiss its stones and roll in its dust. Dayan Kada stressed that the Mishneh Torah is a book of law, not inspiration, and this teaches that there is a duty to love and revere the land, even though there is no obligation to roll in its dust.

The Gemara tells that Rabbi Abba kissed the stones of Akko, and Rabbi Hanina repaired its roads. Rashi explains that he did so out of love, so that no one would speak badly of the land. Rav Kook wrote that even secular pioneers building roads had a spark of Hashem within them and were doing His work without knowing it.

He raised a question. Lashon hara is about people. Calling a table or a building ugly is not lashon hara. Yet the spies were punished for speaking badly about the land. Rashi links their sin to Miriam, who was punished for speaking about Moshe just before. The answer is that this is not lashon hara in the strict legal sense. It belongs to the misvah of loving the land: speaking ill of it means rejecting what Hashem gave us. As Tehillim says, "they despised the desirable land."

He made clear that he was speaking about the land and the nation as a whole, not about political criticism of individuals, which is a different subject.

Finally, he pointed out that the first covenant Hashem made with a person in the Torah, with Avraham, long before Sinai, was about the land. Torah and the Land of Israel go hand in hand.

Summary

  • The Ramban counts settling the Land of Israel as a misvah for all generations; the poskim follow him, though Rav Moshe Feinstein calls it a voluntary misvah rather than an obligation.
  • The Rambam's omission is explained by the three oaths, which can be answered as aggadah, as broken by the nations, or as void once the nations recognised Israel's return; or he may have treated it as a principle covering the whole Torah.
  • Leaving the land is permitted for Torah, marriage or livelihood. Poskim differ about holidays, but leaving permanently for no such reason is forbidden.
  • Loving the land is a duty: praise it, care for it, and never speak ill of it.
  • Even in the diaspora we can keep the land in our hearts as an aspiration and do what we can to promote it.
