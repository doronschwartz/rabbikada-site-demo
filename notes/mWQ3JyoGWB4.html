Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 271, seifim 16-17; 272, seifim 1-9 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 9 November 2023

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seif 16: when the table has to wait

A recap from last week: once one person, preferably the one making Kiddush, has drunk a cheekful, nobody else needs to drink. Everyone drinking is a beautiful extra (misvah min hamuvhar), not a requirement. The Rabbi remembered being frustrated as a boy at Ashkenazi kiddushim, when everyone went straight to the hallah and cake without any wine. That is fine; you can go straight to the food.

But if you do the extra, do it properly. Seif 16 says those at the table should not drink before the one making Kiddush drinks, if they need him. Why would they need him? Because their own cups hold pagum wine, and he has to pour from the Kiddush cup into theirs to fix it. If their cups are fine, they may drink straight away. With bread it is different, as we will see: you wait for the one who broke the bread.

So why is it not pagum when a family drinks one after another from the same cup? The Shaar HaSiyun explains that those who listened to Kiddush are an extension of the one who made it, so it is like one long drinking. Pouring from one cup into another is a new act, and that makes it pagum.

Seif 17: four ways to share the wine

  1. Pass the cup round. The old Sefaradi way; in the Rabbi's family it went by age. Nothing is pagum, but it is the least hygienic.
  2. Pour first, then drink. Before he drinks, he pours from the Kiddush cup into everyone's cups, then drinks what is left. That is part of the Kiddush, not an interruption.
  3. Drink, top up, pass round. This relies on topping up to fix pagum wine.
  4. Everyone has their own cup poured beforehand. He drinks, and everyone drinks from their own cup.

The Rabbi leans towards the fourth. The Shulhan Arukh says clearly in seif 17 that there is no need to drink from the Kiddush cup itself unless you have no other wine. He knows of no halachic significance in drinking wine from the cup the berakhah was said over. Those silver fountains that pour into little cups only make sense if they are used before he drinks; otherwise they add nothing.

Siman 272, seif 1: bad smell, or left open overnight

Siman 272 covers which wine may be used.

A bad smell. Wine that smells bad may not be used even if it still tastes like wine. It is still wine, but you do not do a misvah with something second-rate. The Gemara uses a phrase from the prophets, "offer it to your governor": would he accept it?

Wine left uncovered. The Gemara forbids drinking anything left uncovered overnight, for fear of snakes. Tosafot say this no longer applies, and the Shulhan Arukh in Yoreh Deah rules that way. But the Mishnah Berurah says it is still not fit for Kiddush, for the same reason: it is not a respectful way to do a misvah. The classic case is the cup of Eliyahu at the Seder, left full all night. If it was not covered, it may not be used for Kiddush the next day.

How long counts as uncovered? The Seder is no proof either way, since the cups are being used the whole time. The Rabbi understood the Ben Ish Hai to mean that only a full night counts. The Mishnah Berurah's wording suggests a shorter time. So if you pour the cup on Shabbat morning and then go next door for an hour or two, "it may be a problem."

Seifim 2-5: new wine, sediments, white wine, strong wine

  • New wine. Wine straight from the press is fine, even though wine for the altar had to wait 40 days. You may squeeze a cluster of grapes before Kiddush and make Kiddush on the juice. That is grape juice.
  • Particles and sediment. Wine with particles floating on top is fine (the Rema says strain it first), and so is wine full of sediment from the bottom of the bottle.
  • Dark, sweet, or with a hint of vinegar. All fine, as long as it tastes like wine. "Sweet" here means wine from sweet grapes, not added sugar, which comes later. It is still a misvah to use the best wine.
  • White wine. Fine. If you can, use red. The Mishnah Berurah says that if the choice is between a really good white wine and a cheap red, you may use the white. Pesah is different.
  • Strong wine. Undiluted wine is fine, even if it is very strong. It is better to dilute it to a drinkable strength, but our wines are already diluted and need nothing added.

Seifim 6-8: raisin wine, water, mevushal and sugar

Raisin wine. Raisins soaked in water until they ferment make wine, and Kiddush may be made on it.

Too much water. On wine made from sediment or skins with water added, the limit is about six parts water to one part wine. Beyond that it is no longer wine and does not take Borei Peri HaGefen. Because our wine is already diluted, the Rabbi warned against pouring in water for mezigah. Rav Ovadia Yoseph warned about this: if you want to do it, literally a few drops.

Mevushal and added sugar. The Shulhan Arukh permits both. Some forbid them, the Rambam among them, so the Shulhan Arukh writes "some say" rather than naming one authority.

The Rabbi's practical point: "What drives me mad" is people who drink mevushal wine every Friday night and then refuse it on Seder night. If you use it every week, you can use it on Pesah. But if you are able to use non-mevushal wine every Friday night, you should. Saying "I like sweet wine, it enhances my Shabbat" only works because the main opinion permits it anyway.

He added that a sparkling white grape juice mixed with other fruit raises two of these questions at once: white wine and an added ingredient.

Seif 9: when there is no wine

Some say Kiddush may be made on the drink of the country (hamar medina), such as beer; others say not. The Shulhan Arukh says that at night one makes Kiddush on bread. The Rosh explains: at night the Kiddush has its own long berakhah, so bread works. Shabbat morning Kiddush is only a verse and one berakhah, so HaMosi alone would look like an ordinary meal. In the morning, make Kiddush on beer. "That's the minhag which we do when you're stuck."

Practical summary

  • Nobody besides the one making Kiddush has to drink; if they do, they need not wait for him unless their cup needs topping up from his.
  • Simplest: give everyone their own cup, poured in advance.
  • Do not use wine that smells bad or was left uncovered overnight.
  • Red is preferred, but a good white wine is fine (not on Pesah).
  • Do not add water to modern wine beyond a few drops.
  • Mevushal and sweetened wine are permitted; if you can easily use non-mevushal, do.
  • With no wine: Kiddush on bread at night, on beer or a similar drink in the morning.
