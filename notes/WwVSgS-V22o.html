Topic: Hashgahah peratit, whether and how God pays attention to the details of our lives Sources: The Rambam, Moreh Nevuchim 3:17-18; the Ramhal, Derech Hashem Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 20 May 2024, the fifth class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The series so far, and tonight's question

Dayan Kada reviewed the series: what God is and is not; why God created the universe; the World to Come; and, before Pesah, reincarnation, where the conclusion was that there is no strong source for it. Still to come were two classes on why the righteous suffer and a final class on Jews and non-Jews.

Tonight's subject was hashgahah peratit. Hashgahah comes from the root "to look" and peratit means specific. Does God pay attention to the specifics of this world?

He said he would present the approach of the Rambam and the Ramhal. Hasidic thought, including Chabad, takes a different approach, and he made clear that he was not following it.

Animals: general providence

He began with the animal kingdom. Why did God create creatures we never see or use? People suggested that they are part of an ecosystem, or that creation is ultimately for man.

The creation of Adam on the sixth day does not rule out earlier human forms; Adam is the human we are familiar with.

Then he raised a harder question. As a child he loved the nature documentaries where cheetahs stalk and tear apart their prey. We believe in a just and good God. How is that fair? When a human harms a human, that is free will, and the person will bear the consequences. But animals have no free choice.

His answer was an analogy. You own a business with a hundred similar black cabs. At the end of the year you need only eighty, so twenty are sold for scrap. Nobody asks why these twenty and not the others. Animals do have feelings, he said, but this is the best way to explain it: God does not watch over whether this sheep lives or that one dies. There is only hashgahah kelalit, general providence. God wants there to be a certain number of lions, elephants and sheep. Which one is caught is mikreh, chance. "Random things happen in this world. And that's just nature."

One exception: an animal connected to a human, such as a farmer's sheep that provides his livelihood, falls under the human system.

Humans: specific providence, and its condition

For human beings there is specific providence. Our actions are seen and have consequences. He compared a teacher choosing any five children from a class of thirty for a task (general) with choosing the five who did something special (specific).

But here was the caveat "you may be very surprised to hear." The degree of providence depends on the person. The Rambam writes in the Guide that providence is proportional to the divine influence a person has attained: the greater a person's perfection, the greater the providence over him. And those who are empty of it "rank equal to that of irrational beings."

In Dayan Kada's words: "You want to treat life as random? Life will be random for you." Things may still go well for such a person, but it is nature, like an animal that happens to do well. It works the other way too. The more a person brings God into his life, the more what happens to him has meaning, and the more meaning he finds, the closer the relationship becomes: a virtuous cycle rather than a vicious one.

He linked this to Amalek, whose whole outlook is that everything is chance. The book of Vayikra begins with a small aleph. With it, vayikra means "He called." Without it, vayikar means "He chanced upon." It is our choice whether to see life's events as accidents or as callings. He cited Viktor Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning, which makes the same distinction.

The prophets lived at the far end of this scale. The Talmud says that even reaching into your pocket for one coin and pulling out another can carry a message. For most of us that would mean nothing, which is fine. The point is how far one can go.

Where was God in the Holocaust?

Someone asked: the Jews of Poland kept Shabbat and were deeply religious, and they ended up in Auschwitz. Where was God?

He answered by quoting Rabbi Jonathan Sacks: when people asked where God was in the Holocaust, he replied, "where was man?" When people harm people, that comes from human free will, and God does not stop it. On Rosh Hashanah a person may be decreed a good year, yet another person can still choose to harm him. If everything done to us by others were decreed, those people would have no agency, and Torah and misvot would be pointless. "The question isn't on God. The question is on humans."

Asked why God did not simply stop the wicked, he said honestly that this is a different question and he did not have the answer. His point was to separate harm done by people from natural events. With natural events we have a choice how to see them.

Someone suggested that sometimes a whole nation moves from specific to general providence, as when the Shekhinah withdrew at the destruction of the Temple. He agreed that there are national decrees, a separate scheme of things, and the opposite is found in Egypt, where the blood on the doorposts was needed to take a home out of a general decree. Whether a particular national tragedy is of this kind, "no one can tell you." His own focus was on the events of our individual lives.

Superstition takes us out of providence

He then warned against superstitions such as red strings, blue strings and amulets. A person who relies on them is saying that his fortune depends on the object, and so takes himself out of God's providence and places himself under luck. The Torah repeatedly tells us to keep away from soothsayers and those who predict the future.

Someone asked about ayin hara, the evil eye. It is not in the Torah, he said, but it is in the Talmud, and the question is what it means. The Rambam explains it as not being able to see the good in others or rejoice in their success. Someone asked about flaunting blessings in front of someone who lacks them. He agreed that showing off in a way that causes others pain may lead Hashem to ask whether you deserve what you have been given, but going about life naturally needs no hiding. Yaakov told his sons not to enter Egypt by one gate; one can explain this as ayin hara, or simply as the sensible caution of not drawing attention to eleven strong men. The custom not to call a father and son to the Torah one after the other is advisory, not a law of the Talmud, which is why Rav Ovadia Yosef allows it when needed, for example for twins celebrating their bar misvah together.

The same danger applies to real religious symbols. The Israelites made the golden calf because they wanted an intermediary in place of Moshe. A beautiful explanation of why Moshe broke the Tablets is that a people who could worship a calf would have worshipped the Tablets too. We do this with the mezuzah when we think of it as an amulet that protects us. The mezuzah is there to make us look at it and think of God. "That needs conscious thought. We humans don't like doing that."

Living with meaning

Someone asked: if there is a meaning but we cannot know it, why search? He answered that the purpose of meaning is a calling to do something. Hazal teach that when something happens to you, examine your actions.

He gave an example. You fall off your bicycle in the wind. You learn to check your bike and ride more carefully; that is already finding meaning. Then, riding perfectly, a branch falls and knocks you off. One response is "it's random." The other is introspection: what have I done this week, did I cause anyone pain? "I wouldn't say it's a punishment. Maybe it's a punishment. Maybe it's a calling."

He ended with the difference between living as a victim and living with purpose. He told of someone who suffered abuse when young and went on to set up an organisation to help others who had been abused. Something has happened to me; what can I do with it?

Asked whether prayer can change providence, he said of course it can, and that the new prayer series starting that week would take up this question.

Summary

  • The rest of creation is under general providence: God ensures species exist, but which individual animal lives or dies is chance.
  • Human beings are under specific providence, and its degree depends on each person's relationship with God, as the Rambam explains in the Guide.
  • Harm done by people comes from their free will, which God does not override; natural events can be seen as accidents or as callings.
  • Superstitions and amulets take a person out of God's providence. Religious symbols are only meant to direct us to God.
  • See life's events as a calling: look for the meaning, examine your actions and turn hardship into purpose.
