Making Pesah for the first time

It was Rosh Hodesh Nisan, two weeks before Pesah, and the first lockdown had just begun. Many people would be making their own Pesah for the first time instead of going to parents or parents-in-law, and the Rabbi had received many questions. He planned six topics: shemurah massah, new utensils, cleaning, buying food, kitniyot and Zoom on Pesah. There was time for the first four. The next night's shiur was on the Haggadah, and the laws of the Seder night itself were planned for the following week.

He began with a reminder: from Rosh Hodesh Nisan one may say Birkat HaIlanot, the blessing over blossoming fruit trees, even at night. If you see two blossoming fruit trees on your daily walk for exercise, say the berakhah yourself; there is no need for a minyan.

Shemurah massah

People ordering online from supermarkets were finding ordinary Pesah massot but not shemurah massah, and hand-baked shemurah costs "literally about ten times the price". How essential is it?

The Gemara in Pesahim says that massah baked from dough bought from a non-Jew may be eaten during Pesah (if there is no concern of hames) but cannot be used for the misvah on Seder night. The verse "and you shall guard the massot" teaches that the massot of the misvah, on the first night in Israel and both nights outside it, must be specially guarded for the purpose of Pesah.

From when must the guarding begin? There are three stages: harvesting, grinding and kneading. - The Rosh: as long as a Jew kneads the flour for the sake of the misvah, it is valid, even if non-Jews harvested and ground it. He adds that the custom is to guard from grinding. - The Rif and the Rambam: for the Seder night, the wheat must be guarded from harvest.

The Shulhan Arukh (Siman 453) rules that it is good to guard the wheat from harvest, at least from grinding, and in pressing circumstances one may even buy flour from the market, relying on guarding only from kneading.

The point he wanted people to know: any massot made for Pesah with a hekhsher, even the cheap boxes not labelled "shemurah", are already guarded from grinding. Because of a separate issue (wetting grain before grinding, which is not done), all Pesah massot are protected from water from the grinding stage. So: 1. Crackers or "massot" from an ordinary shop without a hekhsher cannot be used at all on Pesah, since "the thing which is easiest to become hames is massah". 2. Ordinary massot made for Pesah with a hekhsher are fine for all of Pesah. They are not ideal for Seder night, but according to many opinions they fulfil the misvah of shemurah massah then too. 3. Hand-baked shemurah massah, guarded from harvest, is the best for the Seder, following the Rif and the Rambam, if you can get it and afford it.

Someone isolated, such as an elderly couple relying entirely on online orders, may use ordinary Pesah massot for the Seder. Machine-made shemurah from harvest is better than nothing, but for Sefaradim, hand-baked shemurah from harvest is best. Anyone going out to the shops anyway should have no reason not to buy shemurah.

New utensils with the mikveh closed

The men's mikvaot and the mikvaot for utensils had closed. There is an obligation from the Torah to immerse new utensils before use. This year it was not advisable to kasher utensils, as the usual facilities would not be running and kashering needs knowledge of the halachot and safety precautions, so most people would buy new ones. How can they be used without immersion?

  • If your daily walk passes a river, such as the River Brent, you can immerse them there.
  • Otherwise, give the utensils as a gift to a non-Jewish neighbour and borrow them back. The obligation applies only to utensils that belong to a Jew, so a borrowed utensil needs no immersion. When things return to normal, take them back and immerse them.

Someone mentioned that the Federation offers a service for transferring utensils online. That works too, he said, but the neighbour is easiest.

Cleaning: you do not need to clean the whole house

"This is probably one of the biggest misconceptions of Pesah." Many women spend the whole month cleaning and fall asleep at the Seder, "which is such a shame". He quoted Rav Moshe Feinstein as saying that if he were to clean the house for Pesah, he would spend three or four hours on it. The Hakhamim gave us one night, the night of the search, and in earlier times that was all people did.

The principle: - From the Torah, it is enough to nullify one's hames (bitul). No search is required. - The Hakhamim required us to search and remove it for two reasons. If you nullify the expensive whisky sitting in your cupboard, have you really nullified it? And if it stays in sight, by the seventh day, "when massot are coming out of your ears", you may be tempted to eat it.

So the search is rabbinic, and it applies only where hames is brought and where there could be a piece the size of a kezayit, about 28 grams, roughly a matchbox. There is no need to check under beds or behind fridges: hames is unlikely to be there, and anything that is would be unfit even for a dog. No one is tempted by it, and no one still wants it.

Rooms where hames is never brought need no checking. Where a sandwich was occasionally eaten, look for big pieces, behind the curtains or beside the beds. "You don't have to start moving furniture around." The kitchen and dining room need a thorough clean. "If you want to spring clean, by all means... But don't come to Seder night and blame Pesah for it."

Buying food without a Pesah hekhsher

The KLBD had just published a list for these circumstances. He explained why the Sefaradi approach is far easier.

Normally a forbidden food that falls into permitted food is nullified in sixty times its amount. On Pesah itself hames is not nullified even in a thousand, because it will become permitted after Pesah. But that stringency applies only on Pesah. Before Pesah, even on Erev Pesah afternoon, a trace of hames is nullified in sixty.

In countries with strict food labelling, any unlisted ingredient is only a trace, far less than one sixtieth. So a pure product bought before Pesah is fine even if a trace of hames fell in during manufacture.

Pure products bought before Pesah

Sefaradim

Do not need a Pesah hekhsher. We do not hold that the hames "reawakens" on Pesah.

Ashkenazim

Hold that hames nullified before Pesah "reawakens" once Pesah begins, so they look for a hekhsher. They should ask their own rabbis.

Examples he gave as fine for Sefaradim, if pure and bought before Pesah: salt, sugar, pure tea and coffee, oil, 100% juices without sweeteners, natural yoghurts, pure butter, pure spices, pure dried fruit (citing Rav Ovadia Yoseph), and whole or chopped nuts. These need care: mayonnaise (sometimes made with grain vinegar), jams, and ground almonds, where you need to know how they were ground. Anything with additives needs a hekhsher.

Practical summary

  • Use only massot made for Pesah with a hekhsher. Hand-baked shemurah from harvest is best for the Seder; if you cannot get out, ordinary Pesah massot fulfil the misvah according to many opinions.
  • New utensils: immerse them in a river, or give them to a non-Jewish neighbour and borrow them back until the mikvaot reopen.
  • Searching for hames is rabbinic: check only where hames is brought, for pieces the size of a matchbox. Clean the kitchen and dining room properly; do not move furniture.
  • For Sefaradim, pure products with no additives, bought before Pesah, do not need a Pesah hekhsher. Ashkenazim should ask their own rabbis.
