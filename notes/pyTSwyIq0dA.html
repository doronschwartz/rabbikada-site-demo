Topic: Monarchy, transcendence and ritual: why we need the shofar and the formality of the festivals Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Lauderdale Road Synagogue, Monday 26 September 2022 (first day of Rosh Hashanah 5783)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A year that felt almost normal

This was the first Rosh Hashanah since 2019 that felt slightly normal, the Rabbi said, though we dare not be complacent. The year had been complex: the invasion of Ukraine, rising costs of living and energy, continuing climate problems and, for the British and for many across the world, the passing of Queen Elizabeth II two and a half weeks earlier. "All of this was decided on Rosh Hashanah last year."

What monarchy can teach us

For two and a half weeks our minds had been occupied with monarchy. In a democratic age it seems archaic, yet the whole world was watching, and the Rabbi believes it still teaches us about Judaism and about God. The Hakhamim instituted a blessing on seeing a monarch, thanking God who has apportioned some of His glory to human beings. The idea is that the kingdom on earth mirrors the kingdom in heaven, so what we see on earth helps us understand true monarchy. Rosh Hashanah is about proclaiming God's sovereignty and control over the world, and we blow the shofar like a fanfare to greet Him and reintroduce Him into our daily lives. Even the greatest and most benevolent monarch is a pale reflection of the King of Kings.

Transcendence and self-esteem

Watching those events, we all felt stillness, awe and a silent sense of purpose, which could be called transcendence. It contributes to our emotional and spiritual well-being: we feel there is something bigger than ourselves and that we are part of a picture. The Rabbi called this the polar opposite of low self-esteem, which is being absorbed in our own world, our own identity and what others think of us. Self-confidence is recognising who we are in relation to the world around us. Nature, community and having God in our lives give us this too: I am not always in control, there is something greater than me, and I am part of a project. It is hard to feel this in a mundane year, which is why we have these days. Yamim Noraim does not mean "awesome" in the casual sense but days of awe, when we feel there is a purpose to life that we forget during the year. That is what the shofar reminds us of.

Why we need ritual

Why do we need the shofar and all the rituals? Why not simply set aside time on our own? What stood out in those weeks, he said, was the importance of ceremony and formality in a world getting used to casualness. Casualness is convenient, but when we ritualise and formalise something we give it weight and it becomes more meaningful. That is the value of our traditions. The festivals are called Moadim, designated times, and over the coming weeks we have designated times to reach that feeling of transcendence and to recognise that there is more to life than myself and my family: we have a community, a world and a God. We stand, sit, blow and listen, just as people stood, sat and kneeled in the royal ceremonies.

Hearing and listening

When we hear the shofar, let us not only hear it but listen, and internalise its call to awaken, to recognise our community, our nation and the humanity we share, and to bring God into our lives, not just today but all year. Setting aside times in our calendar is a good start, and our children and grandchildren will follow. He closed with a blessing for a year of a better world, meaning, purpose and spiritual and emotional health.

Summary

  • Monarchy, even in a democratic age, points to God's kingship, the theme of Rosh Hashanah.
  • The awe of those weeks is transcendence: being part of something bigger than ourselves.
  • That sense is the opposite of low self-esteem and is good for our well-being.
  • Ritual and formality give moments weight, and the Moadim are designated times for it.
  • Listen to the shofar, not just hear it, and set aside regular time for God.
