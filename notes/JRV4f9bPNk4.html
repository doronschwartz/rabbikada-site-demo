Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 301, seifim 3-8 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 23 May 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Where we are

Simanim 242-300 covered the positive side of Shabbat, the zakhor: preparations, Kiddush, the meals and Havdalah. From Siman 301 to 344 the Shulhan Arukh covers the shamor, the 39 Melakhot. It begins with carrying, the last of them, because it is the Melakhah people take least seriously. "They will carry on Shabbat in a place without an eruv because they think, what am I doing? I'm just carrying something." Last class covered running and jumping: permitted for a misvah or for enjoyment.

Seifim 3-6: Walking through water

Seif 3: if you come to a puddle or stream you may jump over it, even if it needs both feet, and it is better to jump than to go the long way round, since we do not make you walk further on Shabbat. But you may not walk through it, because your clothes will get wet and you may come to wring them out (sehitah), which is part of laundering.

Why then may we go out in heavy rain and come home soaked? Because walking in the rain is the normal way of walking, and the rabbis did not legislate against that. They forbade the less common act of walking through water. In the same way, you may not jump into a pool in your clothes on a hot day and let them dry on you.

Seif 4: for a misvah. One going to a misvah may walk through water. The Gemara's example is going to visit one's rav, or to learn from someone greater in wisdom. Since he is busy with a misvah, the rabbis were not concerned that he would forget. He should keep his hands in, for example in his pockets, as a reminder not to wring his clothes, and should not wear sandals that come off easily, since they may fall and he will carry them. The Rabbi said today's equivalent is slippers, not our tight modern sandals.

The misvah of visiting one's rav. The source is the haftarah of Vayera: when the Shunamit woman's child dies and she sets out to Elisha, she is asked why she is going to him today, when it is "neither Rosh Hodesh nor Shabbat". The Gemara infers she used to visit him then, and so there is a misvah to visit one's rav on Yom Tov, Shabbat and Rosh Hodesh. (Sefaradim end the haftarah on that verse, in the middle of the story.) The idea is that time spent with hakhamim rubs off on you. Greeting the rabbi in shul also fulfils it.

Seif 5: having gone through water to visit your rav, you may come back the same way, even though there is no misvah on the way back. Otherwise people would not go in the first place.

Seif 6: going to guard your produce, you may cross water on the way there but not on the way back. Looking after one's property is a kind of misvah, and one may walk to check on a field or empty property on Shabbat, provided one does not speak about business, which is forbidden by "vedaber davar". Thinking about it is not forbidden, though it is better to rest. On the way back there is no permission, because people care about their money and will go anyway. As an illustration, he imagined someone who could swim across the Thames to reach shul: he may not, but to visit his rav he could, and come back the same way.

Asked about crossing water to get home in time for a seudah, the Rabbi said that is not a reason, unless going round would mean missing the meal before sunset.

The principles of carrying

From here the siman deals with going out wearing or carrying things. The principles: - Malbush or takhshit is permitted. Anything worn as clothing, or as an adornment, may be worn on Shabbat from the Torah. - Anything else is a masui, a burden. The Gemara's example is a sword. A watch, for example, will need discussion: it is neither clothing nor obviously an adornment. - Rabbinic restrictions apply even to clothing and adornments: items that easily fall off (like loose sandals), items one commonly takes off to show people, and items people will laugh at.

Hayav, patur, mutar. Now that we are in the Melakhot, these terms matter. Hayav means a Torah prohibition: if deliberate and with witnesses and warning, stoning in the time of the Sanhedrin; if unintentional, a sin offering. Patur almost always means exempt from Torah punishment but rabbinically forbidden. Mutar means permitted. Asked about lashes, the Rabbi noted that walking beyond the tehum could be an exception, since it is a negative commandment and not one of the 39 Melakhot.

Reshut HaRabbim. A Torah prohibition applies only in a true public domain. A busy street like Oxford Street may be one; a quiet cul-de-sac is a karmelit, where carrying is rabbinic.

Seif 7: Jewellery, weapons and armour

Seif 7: going out with something that is neither clothing nor adornment, carried in the usual way, is a Torah prohibition. A loose adornment that easily falls off is forbidden, though not from the Torah. A woman may not go out with jewellery that women commonly take off to show others. The class discussed whether women today take off rings to show them; some said yes.

A man may not go out with a sword, bow, shield or spear, since these are neither clothing nor adornment; if he did, he is liable. Armour, a helmet and metal leg guards are different: they are worn and are clothing, so the Torah does not forbid them. The rabbis forbade them because people would think he was going out to fight.

Why do women wear jewellery today? Later (in Siman 303) the Shulhan Arukh notes that women do go out with jewellery and justifies it: our streets are not a full Reshut HaRabbim of 600,000 people, and the rabbis did not make a decree to prevent carrying that would itself only be rabbinic (gezerah ligzerah). Asked whether that contradicts the Shulhan Arukh's own view of Reshut HaRabbim, the Rabbi agreed it is a known difficulty and said they would return to it in Siman 303. It follows that a very busy road like Oxford Street or the North Circular might be more questionable.

Tefillin. The Shulhan Arukh says not to go out in tefillin on Shabbat because one will take them off, for example to enter a toilet, and carry them. Read alone, that suggests this is the only problem. Tosafot point out that tefillin are an ot, a sign, and Shabbat is itself a sign, so we do not need tefillin then. The question is whether one may still wear them; elsewhere the Shulhan Arukh implies one should not. In practice tefillin are not worn on Shabbat and are treated as a mild Mukseh: they may be moved for any need. The Rabbi once found tefillin left on the bimah on Friday night and simply moved them. Tefillin found lying in a field are a separate halacha, discussed later in the siman.

Seif 8: Big shoes

A child should not go out in shoes several sizes too big, bought so he can grow into them, because they slip off and he will carry them.

Questions held over. Many cases were raised for coming weeks: watches, sunglasses, hats without a strap, medals, a necklace worn under the shirt for sentimental value, the "Bring Them Home" hostage necklaces, an ornamental walking stick, a jacket worn over the shoulders, and military insignia. The Rabbi said they first needed to define what counts as a takhshit. An umbrella was mentioned as clearly not permitted; a long plastic raincoat is clothing.

Practical summary

  • Jump over a puddle rather than walking through it; do not walk through water except for a misvah such as visiting your rav.
  • Walking in the rain and getting wet is fine.
  • Clothing and adornments may be worn without an eruv; anything else is carrying.
  • Items that easily fall off or are commonly taken off are rabbinically forbidden.
  • Women may wear jewellery today in ordinary streets, relying on the Shulhan Arukh's explanation in Siman 303.
  • Tefillin are not worn on Shabbat but may be moved when needed.
