The halacha

  • After Korekh comes Shulhan Orekh, the festive meal, a misvah every Yom Tov night. The egg recalls the Korban Hagigah brought every festival besides the Korban Pesah.
  • The Afikoman (the Syrian pronunciation is Afikomin) is the last food of the night. The section is called Safun, hidden, as it was wrapped and put aside at Yahas. It recalls the Korban Pesah.
  • It is the leader's fourth kazayit of massah and everyone else's third. Some eat two kezeitim, for the Korban Pesah and the massah eaten with it, a nice humrah; strictly three suffice, with the leader's extra one at Mosi Massah.
  • It is eaten on a full stomach. Reasons in the Rishonim: the Korban Pesah was eaten when full so people would not break its bones, which the Torah forbids; or to show it is eaten for the misvah, not for enjoyment.
  • The Gemara debates whether the Korban Pesah could be eaten until dawn or only until hasot, and the Shulhan Arukh rules hasot. So aim to reach Safun by hasot (about 1:05 a.m. in London).
  • Some poskim say one engaged in misvot all night need not rush, and there is truth to that, but do not waste time: divrei Torah can continue after the Afikoman. One who missed hasot may still eat it until morning.
  • "Afikoman" is Greek for dessert; others explain it as Aramaic for removing all other foods. After it one eats nothing, drinking only the third and fourth cups, water or a plain drink such as tea.
  • It is eaten reclining, recalling the happy misvah of the Korban Pesah.

Sources quoted

  • The Torah: no bone of the Korban Pesah may be broken.
  • The Gemara: until dawn or until hasot.
  • Shulhan Arukh: the Afikoman before hasot.

In one line

The Afikoman recalls the Korban Pesah: eat it reclining, on a full stomach, before hasot, and eat nothing after it.
