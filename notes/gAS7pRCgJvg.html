Berakhot after a night of learning

The second class on the one chapter of Shavuot began with a review. A berakhah made on food or drink early in the night covers the whole night of learning, even if the next cake or coffee comes two hours later, because it is all one long sitting. The exception is if you had decided you were finished: "This is all I'm in the mood for." If an hour later you are tempted again, you make a new berakhah.

In the morning, a Sefaradi who has not slept says all the morning berakhot except the washing of the hands and Asher Yasar (unless he has been to the bathroom). Those two relate to sleep. All the others are said because we bless "on the custom of the world", for what people do each morning, even if it has not happened to us. That is why we still bless God for giving the rooster understanding, though few of us hear a rooster. The berakhot on the Torah are also said.

Dozing is not sleeping

Someone asked about a person who sleeps for an hour and then comes to learn at half past one in the morning. The Rabbi distinguished between two kinds of sleep.

  • Dozing off for twenty minutes, half an hour or even an hour, without meaning to, without lying down in bed, does not count. It changes nothing.
  • A fixed sleep (sheinat keva), where you go to bed, perhaps change into pyjamas and sleep properly for about an hour or more, is different. When you wake, it is a new day for you.

After a real sleep you say all the berakhot: washing the hands, the berakhot on the Torah, and the morning berakhot, even though it is still night. He compared it to the winter, when the class met at 5:45 in the morning, well before dawn, and everyone said all the berakhot first, "because that's the new day for you."

The readings and the chariot

The Ten Commandments on Shavuot are read in the taam elyon, the special "upper" cantillation. The Rabbi had hoped to compare it with the regular version in the Humash but ran out of time.

The haftarah of the first day is the chariot of Yehezkel, the most esoteric part of Tanakh. Hazal say the chariot may not be expounded in public, and in many places the custom is for the rabbi, or someone wise, to read it. Someone asked how we can read it publicly at all. The Rabbi answered that what is forbidden is analysing and dissecting it, not reading the verses. In the same way, the Gemara says not to expound the forbidden relationships in public, yet we read the whole list in the Torah.

No fasting after the festival

The Shulhan Arukh says it is forbidden to fast on the day after Shavuot. In 2022 Shavuot ended on Monday, so the Tuesday was included. Why only one day, and why Shavuot?

The first reason goes back to the Temple. Before the fixed calendar, Shavuot could fall on Shabbat; in our calendar its first day never does. On Pesah and Sukkot, if the first day was Shabbat, the festival offerings could be brought on any of the seven days. Shavuot has only one day. When it fell on Shabbat, the offerings were brought the next day, the yom tevoah, which became a day of celebration. That reason applies only to Shavuot.

The Mishnah Berurah brings others who say the reason is simply the joy of the festival spilling over. That applies after all three festivals, and that is the halacha: one should not fast on the day after Pesah, Shavuot or Sukkot. The Rabbi added that someone who fasted on such a day, even for a bad dream, would need to make up for it after Shavuot, but after Pesah and Sukkot could rely on the view that the rule is only for Shavuot.

On Tahanun, the Rema records that Ashkenazim do not say it from Rosh Hodesh Sivan until the eighth of the month. The Sefaradi custom is to omit it for the seven days after Shavuot as well.

Greenery and trees: two customs

The Rema writes that it is customary to spread grass and flowers in the synagogue and the home, to recall the joy of the giving of the Torah. The Rabbi stressed that there are two separate customs here, which people confuse.

Greenery. Grass, small flowers and myrtle, in the synagogue and at home. There was grass at Sinai, since Moshe was told that the flocks must not graze before the mountain, and the Midrash says each word God spoke was like blossoms to the Bnei Israel. The Mishnah Berurah allows it to be spread on Yom Tov itself, even if it is mukseh, because it was set aside for this. If fragrant myrtle is handed out, it should not be during Pesukei DeZimra, from Barukh SheAmar on, when one may not interrupt to make the berakhah. "No one has a problem with that. That's a beautiful minhag."

Trees. The second custom is to put up trees or large displays, as some do in Israel, to recall that on Shavuot the world is judged for the fruit of the tree. The Vilna Gaon abolished this, because putting up trees for a festival had become the way of the non-Jews.

We do not follow that ruling, because of how hukkot hagoyim is defined. The prohibition comes in Parashat Aharei Mot, right after the forbidden relationships. It covers what the non-Jews do in the context of sin, or customs of theirs that have no apparent reason. It does not mean that anything non-Jews do is forbidden. Something with a clear reason is not a problem, which is why a synagogue could put up trees if it wanted to, though some synagogues do not, following the Vilna Gaon.

The class discussed where the customs came from. The greenery custom is first recorded by the Maharil in fifteenth century Germany, an Ashkenazi custom that spread to the Sefaradim. "There's lots of times, you know, we see goyim doing things and we try to give a Jewish spin to it."

Dairy on the first day

The Rema writes that in many places people eat dairy foods on the first day of Shavuot. The Rabbi noted that it says the first day, "not Shabbat before, not second day," whatever the three days of cheesecake might suggest.

The reason given in the Rema is this. On Pesah night the plate holds two foods, the egg and the bone, recalling two offerings. The offering special to Shavuot was the two loaves. So we eat a dairy course with bread, and then a meat course. Bread that has been on the table at a dairy meal may not be eaten with meat, even if no cheese touched it, so the meat course needs a new loaf. Two loaves.

"At its core, this minhag was a minhag about having one course of dairy." It was never meant to turn the Yom Tov meals into dairy meals, since there is a misvah to eat meat on Yom Tov. But if three days of meat are too much for someone, that too affects his joy, and it is fine to have one meal of meat and one of dairy on each day.

On waiting between them: after meat, six hours, with more room to be lenient for chicken. After dairy, one cleans the mouth and washes the hands. The Zohar and the Kabbalists forbid having dairy and meat in the same meal at all, which is why they dislike this custom; but the Rema evidently ate them in one meal. In practice the Rabbi suggested Kiddush with the dairy foods, a break, and then the meal.

Finally, the custom must never become a reason to relax kashrut. One Hasidic rebbe, Rebbe Elimelekh of Lizhensk, is reported to have said that on Shavuot one need not wait the full six hours after meat before dairy. Rav Ovadia Yosef rejected this firmly. "There's no misvah to have dairy food. It's a custom, it's a nice thing." It should not come at the expense of the laws of kashrut or of the joy of Yom Tov.

Practical summary

  • One berakhah on refreshments covers a night of learning, unless you decided you had finished. After a sleepless night, Sefaradim say all the morning berakhot except the washing of the hands and Asher Yasar.
  • Dozing does not count as sleep; a real sleep in bed of about an hour or more makes it a new day for you, even before dawn.
  • Do not fast on the day after Shavuot, or after Pesah and Sukkot.
  • Greenery in the synagogue and home is a beautiful custom; trees are permitted by our practice, though the Vilna Gaon forbade them.
  • Dairy on the first day means one dairy course, then meat with a fresh loaf. It is a custom, not a misvah, and no laws of kashrut are relaxed for it.
