Tonight

Written in 1177, when the Rambam was about 40. Some of it overlaps the Introduction to the Mishnah, "which is why it's so appropriate to do it now"; some is new.

Part 1: An Opening Verse, and a Nod to His Critics

אז לא אבוש בהביטי אל כל מצותיך (Tehillim 119)

Then I shall not be ashamed, when I gaze at all Your commandments.

Why open his halachic magnum opus (the Moreh is his philosophical one) with this verse? "Perhaps he recognised this book wouldn't go down well with everyone. And indeed it did not."

  • Some of the Rambam's books were burned, because Rabbeinu Yonah of Gerona thought some views (in the Moreh) heretical. "Later he recognised he was wrong."
  • The Mishneh Torah itself was criticised: by Rav Pinchas HaDayan (whose letter we learned this winter), and by the Ra'avad: how can you write a book without sources? "And yet the Ra'avad spent an enormous amount of time writing a gloss on the entire Mishneh Torah. So he clearly thought it significant."

"There's no halachic work like it, in brevity, clarity and system." So the verse is "like a nod to those who will criticise him: I'm not embarrassed. I'm confident I'm doing the right thing. Here I am."

Part 2: The Written and the Oral Torah

כל המצוות שנתנו לו למשה בסיני, בפירושן נתנו. Every mitzvah given at Sinai came with its explanation.

ואתנה לך את לחת האבן והתורה והמצוה (Ki Tisa):

התורה = the written Torah; המצוה = its explanation, the Oral Torah. We're commanded to keep the Torah according to the mitzvah.

  • Moshe wrote the whole Torah in his own hand before he died, gave a scroll to each tribe, and put a thirteenth in the Ark as testimony: לקח את ספר התורה הזה ושמתם אתו מצד ארון ברית ה׳ (Vayelech).
  • The explanation he did not write. He taught it to the elders, Yehoshua and all Israel: את כל הדבר אשר אנכי מצוה אתכם אתו תשמרו לעשות. Hence: Oral Torah.

(Recall from the Introduction to the Mishnah: the four-times teaching, Eruvin 54b.) Moshe taught it in his beit din to the seventy elders; Elazar, Pinchas and Yehoshua received it directly; to Yehoshua, his student, he handed it on and charged him with it.

Part 4: The Digression on Rabbeinu HaKadosh

Instead of continuing to the Amoraim, the Rambam stops to talk about Rebbi. Why? "He loved Rabbeinu HaKadosh. And notice he calls his own book Mishneh Torah. I think he saw similarities between himself and Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi." (The Rabbi's view: "I've got no source for this.")

מימות משה רבנו ועד רבנו הקדוש לא חברו חבור שמלמדין אותו ברבים בתורה שבעל פה.

From Moshe to Rebbi, no one composed a book of the Oral Torah to teach in public.

But in every generation, the head of the court or the prophet would write for himself a record of the traditions he heard from his teachers, and teach them orally in public.

Writing down the Oral Torah

"Notice: the Rambam seems to say there's no problem writing down the Oral Law. We know Rebbi had to make a decision, despite the prohibition, to write it. But the prohibition is on writing it en masse, to teach from. Private notes to remember and transmit properly were always fine."

Each leader wrote according to his ability: what he'd heard, and the new laws each generation derived by the 13 principles and the Great Court agreed. Then Rebbi gathered it all, every tradition, law and explanation from Moshe on, and composed the Mishnah, taught it publicly, and it spread to all Israel.

Why?

לפי שראה שהתלמידים מתמעטים והולכים, והצרות מתחדשות ובאות, ומלכות הרשעה פושטת בעולם ומתגברת, וישראל מתגלגלין והולכין לקצוות.

He saw students dwindling, new troubles coming, the wicked empire spreading and growing, and Israel wandering to the ends of the earth.

So he made one book in everyone's hands, to be learned quickly and not forgotten, and spent the rest of his life teaching it with his court.

"I believe the Rambam sees himself (not spiritually, but in identity) as a kind of Rebbi. Rebbi gathered the Oral Torah into the Mishnah around 250; the Rambam does the same a thousand years later, in 1177. That's why he calls it Mishneh Torah."

נמצא מרב אשי עד משה רבנו ארבעים איש.

From Rav Ashi back to Moshe: forty men.

Over about 2,000 years: from Sinai (2448, 3,338 years ago) to Rav Ashi, about 500 CE. The Rambam lists them backwards, ending …Yehoshua, Moshe Rabbeinu, from the mouth of Hashem, God of Israel. "Incredible."

Where did he get it all? "From Tanach, from Avot chapter 1, and from what's scattered through the Mishnah and Gemara about who learned from whom. Remember his ten appendices last week. There are small disagreements, but it's basically accepted."

"And thousands and tens of thousands heard from them." These forty were the heads of their generations (heads of academies, Exilarchs, Sanhedrin), but thousands of others learned with them.

Why list them?

  1. Everything in the Mishneh Torah goes back to Moshe. It all comes from the written Torah or the Oral Torah (Mishnah and Gemara), and anything else he labels (a later authority said, a custom). "The problem with books today is they put everything together and you don't know the origin. No kitniyot on Pesach: there are many examples."
  2. The answer to his critics. Why no sources? "Because it all comes back to Moshe. I don't need to cite them."

Part 6: What the Talmuds Contain

Rav Ashi composed the Bavli in Shinar (Babylonia), about a century after Rabbi Yochanan's Yerushalmi (today dated about 495 CE). Both explain the Mishnah, and with the Tosefta, Sifra and Sifrei they clarify forbidden and permitted, pure and impure, liable and exempt, valid and invalid, plus:

גזרות, fences

ושמרתם את משמרתי: עשו משמרת למשמרתי (e.g. poultry and milk)

תקנות, enactments

Good practice, binding but not a fence (e.g. prozbul). "The Rambam is very particular about the difference between a gezerah and a takkanah"

מנהגות, customs

Each court's safeguards: binding too, לא תסור מן הדבר אשר יגידו לך ימין ושמאל

Derived laws

By the 13 principles, ruled by the Great Court of the generation

Part 7: Why the Talmud Binds Us, and Nothing After It Does

נמצא רבינא ורב אשי וחבריהם סוף גדולי חכמי ישראל מעתיקי תורה שבעל פה: the last great transmitters of the Oral Torah, whose gezerot, takkanot and customs spread through all Israel, wherever they lived.

After Rav Ashi, Israel scattered further, to distant islands ("places like England"); roads became unsafe; Torah study dwindled; no more yeshivot of thousands, just "a few survivors whom Hashem calls," in each city and land.

Any beit din after the Talmud, in any land, that decreed, enacted or set a custom: its acts did not spread to all Israel, because of distance, bad roads, and because the Great Sanhedrin of 71 had ceased years before the Talmud was compiled.

The Talmud

After the Talmud

Authority

"All the Sages of Israel, or most of them," who received person from person, back to Moshe

Local courts

A gezerah or custom

Binding on every Jew, everywhere; we compel every city

Binds only its city. One city can't be forced to adopt another's practice; a court needn't adopt another's decree

A later scholar with better proofs

Irrelevant: "you can have the best proof; the Talmud is the Sanhedrin, you must accept it"

Follow whoever's reasoning is stronger, earlier or later: the 700 scholar can override the 600 one

A modern example: "A beit din in New Square declared it forbidden to touch an iPhone. That's a gezerah. Does it apply to me? No. לא פשטו מעשיו בכל ישראל."

The Sanhedrin in exile: after the destruction it moved through ten places in the Land, until about 425 CE. But since everything in the Talmud was accepted by all Israel, its authority holds.

Why we still mourn on Tisha B'Av

"We have a sovereign state. Why mourn?"

"We don't have the Temple. But greater than that, we don't have a Sanhedrin. We don't have a supreme court. Our Torah, since 500 CE, has been fossilised. The beauty of the Sanhedrin was that it could interpret the Torah as each generation needed, with authority. Our hands are tied."

Second-day Yom Tov. "People ask why we keep it: we know the calendar exactly. You're right. It doesn't make sense, and I'd be the first to say we don't need it. The problem is the Gemara says we must, and the Gemara is the Sanhedrin. Until you give me a new Sanhedrin, we keep it. If Mashiach comes before Tisha B'Av and we have a Sanhedrin, it may decide we don't need it."

Part 8: The Geonim, and the Problem

Everyone after the Talmud = the Geonim: in Eretz Yisrael, Shinar (Babylonia), Spain and France. They explained the Talmud because:

- its way is very deep, and

- it's in Aramaic mixed with other languages: clear to Babylonians when composed, but understood by no one elsewhere, nor in Babylonia by the Geonim's time.

What they produced:

- Responsa, collected into books

- Commentaries on single laws, difficult chapters, whole tractates

- Halachot pesukot: decided law, for those who can't reach the Talmud's depths

"Holy work, from the Talmud's close until now": 1,108 years after the destruction, i.e. 1177.

No chain for this period. "Notice: he doesn't trace the Sages from 500 to his own time. He could have. It makes no difference: there's no longer a binding transmission. Everyone interprets on his own."

"In our time, troubles have multiplied"

ובזמן הזה תכפו צרות יתרות… ואבדה חכמת חכמינו ובינת נבונינו נסתתרה.

Even the Geonim's explanations, which they thought plain, have become hard, and few understand them properly. "Needless to say the Talmud itself": Bavli, Yerushalmi, Sifra, Sifrei, Tosefta, which require a broad mind, a wise soul and a long time.

Part 9: The Mishneh Torah

ומפני זה נערתי חצני אני משה בן מימון הספרדי, ונשענתי על הצור ברוך הוא, ובינותי בכל אלו הספרים.

So I, Moshe son of Maimon the Sefaradi, girded my loins, relied on the Rock, and studied all these books, and decided to compose from them, on forbidden and permitted, impure and pure, and all the Torah's laws:

כולן בלשון ברורה ודרך קצרה

Clear language, concise

בלי קושיא ובלי פירוק

No questions and answers

ולא זה אומר בכה וזה אומר בכה

No "this one says, that one says"

אלא דברים ברורים קרובים נכונים

Only clear, accessible, correct rulings, as they emerge from everything from Rebbi until now

"What an incredible statement to make."

"No other book"

כללו של דבר, כדי שלא יהא אדם צריך לחבור אחר בעולם בדין מדיני ישראל, אלא יהא חבור זה מקבץ לתורה שבעל פה כולה, עם התקנות והמנהגות והגזרות שנעשו מימות משה רבנו ועד חבור התלמוד, וכמו שפירשו לנו הגאונים.

In short: so that no one needs any other book in the world for any law of Israel. This one gathers the whole Oral Torah: every takkanah, custom and decree from Moshe to the Talmud, as the Geonim explained them.

לפיכך קראתי שם חבור זה "משנה תורה", לפי שאדם קורא בתורה שבכתב תחלה, ואחר כך קורא בזה, ויודע ממנו תורה שבעל פה כולה, ואינו צריך לקרות ספר אחר ביניהם.

Therefore I called it Mishneh Torah, the "second Torah": read the written Torah first, then this, and you know the whole Oral Torah, with no need for any other book.

"He was criticised for this line."

What he did not mean

As he wrote in the letter to Rav Pinchas HaDayan: he never said not to study Mishnah and Gemara. It's a summary. To go in depth, study Mishnah, Gemara, Geonim, Tosefta, baraitot.

"And it's true. If you know Tanach and the Mishneh Torah inside out, you can't really go wrong: you know the basics of everything. And it doesn't take as long as the whole of Shas. That's why he called it the second Torah. Remarkable."

The structure

Divided by topic (halachot), each into chapters, each into numbered halachot, "so they can be ordered in one's memory." Some sections cover one mitzvah with many details; others, many mitzvot on one subject. Named by topic, not by number of mitzvot.

613 mitzvot: 248 positive, like the limbs of the body, and 365 negative, like the days of the solar year.

Summary

  1. Opening verse, אז לא אבוש: a confident nod to critics (Rabbeinu Yonah, Rav Pinchas, the Ra'avad, who glossed it anyway).
  2. Written Torah = התורה; Oral = המצוה. Moshe wrote 13 scrolls; the explanation he taught.
  3. Forty generations from Moshe to Rav Ashi, all named: prophets, the Great Assembly (120, last Shimon HaTzaddik), the Zugot, the first titles (Rabban), Rabbi Akiva (עקיבה with a heh), Rebbi, the Amoraim. It shows everything in the Mishneh Torah goes back to Sinai: hence no need for sources.
  4. Rebbi wrote the Mishnah because students dwindled and exile spread. Private notes were always allowed. The Rambam saw himself as a Rebbi a thousand years on.
  5. The Talmud binds all Israel: accepted by all, the last link of the chain, the final Sanhedrin. Nothing after it does, beyond its locality (New Square's iPhone ban); later scholars are judged on their reasoning.
  6. Without a Sanhedrin our hands are tied: why Tisha B'Av still matters, and why we keep second-day Yom Tov.
  7. The Geonim explained, but by 1177 even they were hard. So: Mishneh Torah, clear, concise, no disputes, "no other book needed," as a summary, not a replacement for study. 613 = 248 + 365.
