Topic: The Monday and Thursday supplications, hagbahah, and the three aliyot of a weekday reading Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 134 and Siman 135:1, with the Rema and the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 16 June 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Laws you need on the spot

This class opened a new topic: the laws of the Sefer Torah and the Torah reading, from Siman 134 onwards. The Rabbi recalled being told during his semikhah studies that these are probably the laws a rabbi most needs to know instantly. With any other question you can say, "I don't know, I'll check." But when the Levi is called up by mistake, or an Israel instead of the Kohen, the whole congregation is waiting. "You don't have time to start looking up in the books."

He compared it to the Yerushalmi's teaching about saving life on Shabbat: if people have to ask the rabbi, he has not taught them properly beforehand.

Extra supplications on Monday and Thursday

Siman 134 begins with the extra supplications said on Mondays and Thursdays, beginning Vehu Rahum. They are first recorded among the later Geonim, around the tenth century. They are said standing, and the Shulhan Arukh uses strong language for anyone who sits. The Rabbi explained that whenever the Rishonim use such language, it means people were in fact doing otherwise: here, sitting through them.

Why these days? The Mishnah Berurah explains that they are days of favour. Moshe went up the mountain for the second Tablets on a Thursday and came down forty days later on a Monday, with forgiveness. That is why Monday and Thursday became days of extra supplication, days for fasting, and the days of the Torah reading.

The Ari later added passages borrowed from Yom Kippur, such as Anshei Emunah Avadu. The Rabbi shared a personal view: borrowing so much from the Yamim Noraim, as happened earlier with Aleinu, which came from Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, takes something away from those days when they come.

Some practical points:

  • The point is extra supplication. Someone who cannot read all of it should stand with everyone and say one paragraph, in Hebrew or in English.
  • When the description says "aloud", it means not whispered, as opposed to parts of Tahanun said quietly. The Sefaradi way is the correct one: the hazan says it and everyone follows quietly or listens.
  • In the winter weeks when sunrise was very late, the Rabbi moved the supplications earlier so that the Amidah could be said at sunrise. Praying the Amidah at sunrise is more important.

Hagbahah: showing the Torah to everyone

The Shulhan Arukh writes that the Sefer Torah is shown to the people standing to the right and to the left, in front and behind: all four directions. It is a misvah for all men and women to see the writing and to bow.

When? The Shulhan Arukh puts this before the reading. How do we know? The next siman deals with the reading itself, and the Shulhan Arukh is written in the order of the day. (The Rabbi gave a parallel: its chapters on Seudah Shelishit come before the chapter on Minhah of Shabbat, which shows that eating Seudah Shelishit before Minhah was normal.)

The Rema records that the Ashkenazi custom is to lift the Torah after the reading. He gives it in the Darkhei Moshe as a custom, without a source. Why would you show the Torah after reading it? The Rabbi quoted the Knesset HaGedolah: people would see the Torah lifted and think they had done their part, then leave for work. So the lifting was moved to the end, to keep people for the reading, which is the main thing. Leaving while the Sefer Torah is open is treated very severely by the Gemara.

He mentioned a change he had proposed for Yom Kippur in his synagogue for the same reason. Neilah would start a little earlier, and the final shofar would be blown after Arvit, so that people pray Arvit before they leave. Someone remarked that in a few hundred years people will give other reasons for such a custom, and the Rabbi agreed: that is how many customs began.

Who may look? Both men and women. Some women are worried about this, so the Rabbi was clear: a woman who is niddah is permitted to look at the Sefer Torah. Some with a Kabbalistic bent say it is better not to, but in halacha there is nothing wrong with it.

Bowing does not mean a special action. The correct custom is to bow the head when seeing the Torah, while it is being shown.

How much? The Mishnah Berurah says the one lifting should open it to show three columns if he is able, and one column is also enough. For a Sefaradi Torah in its case the question does not arise. In the Spanish and Portuguese community, the Rabbi described, you must pass a test before you may do hagbahah: three practice sessions, including lifting at Bereshit and at Vezot HaBerakhah, when all the weight is on one side, and a test before three people. "When you see it being done properly... it's majestic."

The Mishnah Berurah adds that it is good to see the letters clearly enough to read them, quoting the Kabbalists that a great light comes from this. The Rabbi noted that the Mishnah Berurah never quotes the Kabbalists on its own; it does so when the Magen Avraham has, because the Magen Avraham was the first Ashkenazi codifier to bring the Ari into halacha. (The Shulhan Arukh sometimes quotes the Zohar, but never the Ari.) The Magen Avraham is so terse that whole commentaries were written just to explain him.

Three aliyot on a weekday

The class began Siman 135. On Monday, Thursday and Shabbat afternoon three people are called up, no fewer and no more, and there is no haftarah. Why no more? "It's a day of work. People need to go to work. This is an important halacha for people to know. Don't draw out the services."

The Rema, quoting the Mordekhai, records one exception. If there are two bridegrooms, each with a claim to an aliyah, four may be called on a weekday, because for a bridegroom it is like Yom Tov, and on Yom Tov we add. The Rema extends this to two people each with a berit milah that day. What happens with more than two, or with Kohanim, would come later.

Morning or afternoon? The Shulhan Arukh does not say the weekday reading must be in the morning, though the Rambam mentions Shahrit. The Noda BiYehudah was asked about a community snowed in on a Monday morning who could only gather a minyan in the afternoon, and he allowed them to read then. The Hatam Sofer held the same and would read in the afternoon when travelling. The Hida, however, writes that this is only for a one-off: where there is normally a morning minyan and it was missed. A place that only ever has an afternoon minyan should not make it the regular practice. That, said the Rabbi, is the halacha we follow. The synagogue had done it once a few months earlier when the morning minyan fell short, but it should not become regular.

Practical summary

  • Stand for the Monday and Thursday supplications. If you cannot say all of them, say one paragraph with the congregation.
  • Sefaradim lift the Torah before the reading, as the Shulhan Arukh rules; Ashkenazim lift it after, a custom meant to stop people leaving early.
  • Men and women should see the writing and bow their heads. A woman who is niddah may look at the Sefer Torah.
  • Three are called on a weekday, no more, so as not to delay people going to work; two bridegrooms or two baalei berit allow a fourth.
  • A missed weekday reading may be made up in the afternoon as a one-off, not as a fixed practice.
