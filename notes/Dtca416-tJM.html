These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

What the sixth principle really says

The Rabbi reminded the class that the 13 principles fall into three groups. Principles 1 to 5 are about God Himself. Principles 6 to 9 are about revelation: prophecy, the uniqueness of Moshe's prophecy, the divinity of the Torah and the unchanging Torah. The last group covers reward and punishment, Mashiah and the resurrection of the dead. This class opened the second group.

He began by correcting a common misunderstanding. The sixth principle is not that every word recorded in the books of the prophets is true. That is true, he stressed, but it is not what the Rambam is saying here, and it belongs more to principle 8. The sixth principle is that there is such a thing as prophecy: God reveals Himself to perfected human beings.

For this reason he said the familiar Ani Maamin wording, "I believe with perfect faith that all the words of the prophets are true", misses the point. It was written a few hundred years ago as a short summary to say after prayers. The Yigdal we sing on Friday night is more precise: "The rich gift of His prophecy He gave to the men of His choice."

Why prophecy matters so much

Why give two whole principles to prophecy, when we no longer have prophets? Because some philosophies, and sadly some Jews, believe that God created the world and then left it to run on its own. The Ramban stresses this in the Ten Plagues: Moshe tells Pharaoh that there is a God "in the midst of the land", still involved in the world.

Prophecy, in one sentence, is God revealing His will to human beings. The greatest revelation in history was Har Sinai, before the whole nation, something no other religion claims. But there were prophets for many centuries after that.

The Rabbi gave an analogy. A God who created the world but never said what He wants is harder to believe in than no God at all. It is like bringing home a large flat-pack box from IKEA without the instruction sheet: "If you want to give them to me, tell me what I need to do with it." The Torah and the prophets are the instruction book.

Three levels: dreams, Ruah HaKodesh and prophecy

He described three ways a person can know things beyond the natural, from lowest to highest.

Dreams. At night, says the Zohar, the soul leaves the body. This is why the Talmud calls sleep one sixtieth of death, why we wash our hands in the morning to remove the impurity that rests on that vacuum, and why we thank God for returning our soul. A dream is a fuzzy transmission of what the soul picked up from spiritual sources while it was free. Since it can reach true sources or false and negative ones, we do not take dreams too seriously, though some do come true.

Someone asked how this fits with the modern idea of the subconscious. The Rabbi answered that the Talmud itself says most dreams come from what a person thought about during the day. The soul goes at night to the places the person has been inclined to by day, so someone who lives in a holy world will be drawn to holy places at night.

Another asked what a soul is. Briefly, he said, it is our ability to make an informed choice rather than be driven by instinct, and above all our intellect, which is what remains of us after death. This led to a lively discussion about whether animals have free will, and why the serpent in Eden was punished. He answered that the serpent, like Adam and Havah, was not like anything we know today.

Ruah HaKodesh, divine inspiration, is a kind of intuition while awake. His example was the 72 sages whom King Talmai locked in separate rooms to translate the Torah into Greek. Each one made the same changes in the same places (Talmud, Megillah).

Prophecy is the highest level, marked by two things: clarity and cleaving to God. The Rambam writes that the prophet's soul joins the level of the angels and he becomes a different man. The Ramhal adds that he knows without any doubt that he is cleaving to his Creator. His analogy: philosophy is like groping in a dark room with your eyes closed, while prophecy is opening your eyes on a party full of colour and faces. "If you've experienced prophecy, you know you've experienced prophecy."

How does a person become a prophet?

Everything so far is agreed by all, he said. Here the debate begins. In the Moreh Nevuchim (II:32) the Rambam brings three views: 1. God chooses whom He wishes, wise or ignorant, young or old, and sends him. The Rambam calls this the view of the ignorant masses. Yet the Rabbi pointed out that it is the view of many medieval thinkers, including Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi in the Kuzari and Rabbi Yosef Albo, and probably the majority. 2. Aristotle's view: prophecy is the natural perfection of man. Once a person has perfected himself morally and intellectually, he must become a prophet. 3. The Rambam's view: like Aristotle, only a perfected person can prophesy, but even then God may withhold it. "According to the Rambam, the miracle of prophecy is that you've perfected yourself... and still you don't become a prophet."

In short: in the first view God chooses man; in the second, man chooses God.

The views lead to different answers on other questions. According to the Kuzari, God chooses from His people, so a non-Jew cannot become a prophet, and even a convert cannot, only his children. Prophecy also only happens in the Land of Israel. According to the Rambam, anyone who has perfected himself can become a prophet, Jew or not. Bilam, all agree, was an exception. Hazal say he was given prophecy so that the nations could not claim they would have been righteous had they had a prophet too.

The three requirements

The Rambam's Mishneh Torah says prophecy rests only on "a very wise sage of strong character, who is never overcome by his natural inclinations", with "a very broad and accurate mental capacity". The Rabbi then read the Moreh Nevuchim (II:36), which says that no one can reach prophecy without perfection "in the theoretical sciences", refined moral qualities and the most perfect imaginative faculty.

Putting the two together gives three requirements: - Intellectual perfection: knowing the world God created, including the sciences. For the Rambam, Maaseh Bereshit, one of the Torah's great secrets, is the study of the natural sciences. - Moral perfection: never being driven by animal instincts. - A perfect imaginative faculty: the ability to "think big", like the person who first combined a ship and a bird and imagined an aeroplane.

Someone asked whether the science of the Rambam's day has not been proved wrong. The Rabbi answered that what matters is being engaged in understanding God's works, even if later generations know more.

Har Sinai, and what prophecy is not

If prophecy needs such perfection, how did a whole nation of former slaves hear God at Sinai? The Rambam's answer: they heard only the first two commandments, the existence and unity of God. These can be reached through philosophical reasoning. The other eight, like Shabbat, needed Moshe's prophecy.

Someone said that prophecy means foretelling the future. The Rabbi corrected this firmly. Hazal say that Israel had twice as many prophets as the number who left Egypt, yet almost none of their prophecies are recorded. A prophet may be sent on a mission or told the future, but that is incidental. "The essence of prophecy is that relationship with God."

He ended by reading the Rambam's description of the prophetic experience itself: the prophet's limbs tremble, his senses stop, and he is overwhelmed. Moshe's prophecy was different, and that would be principle 7. He announced that principle 8, the divinity of the Torah, would follow on 25 November and would need two parts.

Summary

  • The sixth principle is not that the books of the prophets are true, but that prophecy exists: God reveals Himself to human beings.
  • A God who created the world without revealing His will makes less sense than no God at all; prophecy is the instruction book.
  • Dreams, Ruah HaKodesh and prophecy are three levels; prophecy alone brings full clarity and cleaving to God.
  • For the Rambam, prophecy requires intellectual, moral and imaginative perfection, and even then God may withhold it. Many others hold that God simply chooses the prophet.
  • Prophecy is not fortune-telling. Its essence is the prophet's relationship with God.
