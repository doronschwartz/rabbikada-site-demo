Why customs matter so much

Dayan Kada titled the class "Halacha and Minhag: How Powerful Are Customs, and Can They Be Changed?" He opened with a story of a rabbi who recorded, among his community's customs, the custom to keep Shabbat. When a learned man objected that Shabbat is in the Torah, he answered that most people attach more importance to customs than to halacha.

Customs are a powerful part of the identity of a family, a community and the nation. Some are national, like the second day of Yom Tov in the diaspora, which has almost the status of law; some are centuries old, like the Ashkenazi custom not to eat kitniyot on Pesah. The Jewish people have three bodies of binding authority: the Torah, rabbinic law and customary law.

The difficulty today, he said, is that Jews from Syria and from Lithuania now live side by side and marry one another. The old idea of a defined community with its own customs no longer fits, and we have not yet come to terms with that.

He divided the class into personal, family, communal and geographical customs.

Personal customs

The Torah in Parashat Matot teaches that a person can take a vow, a neder or shevuah, and bind himself to something specific. Breaking it violates the Torah's command "he shall not break his word."

The Gemara adds a rabbinic version. Something permitted that a person has become accustomed to refrain from, or to do, takes on the status of a vow even if it was never said aloud, and he may not stop without hatarat nedarim, annulment of vows. The Shulhan Arukh gives the example of someone who regularly fasts during the Ten Days of Teshuvah. The Mishnah Berurah explains that this applies once a person has done it about three times, or even once if he intended at the time to make it a regular practice. For example, if someone decides in a moment of inspiration to fast every Friday and intends to keep it up, he is bound.

There are two important limits:

  • It must be accepted knowingly. If someone thought something was required and later learned it was not, he needs no annulment. Someone had lit an extra Shabbat candle for each child, thinking it was the law, and later discovered there is no such requirement. He need not annul anything.
  • A one-off break is not abandoning it. Women are exempt from hearing the shofar, since it is a time-bound positive misvah, yet many have taken it on. During COVID some did not want to go to shul. Did they need someone to come and blow at home? No. Skipping it on one occasion, or not feeling up to a voluntary fast one Monday, is a pause, not a cancellation. Only stopping indefinitely requires annulment.

This is why people say beli neder when they undertake something good, and why, in the annulment of vows before Rosh Hashanah, Sefaradim declare that any misvah done three times in the coming year should count as if they had said it.

Family customs

Is a parent's personal custom binding on the children? The Gemara tells of the people of Beishan, whose fathers had the custom not to sail from Tyre to Sidon on Friday. The sons asked Rabbi Yohanan to release them, and he told them their fathers had accepted it, so they must continue.

Most understand Beishan as a place, which makes this a communal custom. A few later authorities, including the Peri Toar, read it as a family custom, but the consensus is that there is no binding family custom separate from the community. Mar Ukva said that his father waited a full day between meat and dairy, while he himself waited only until the next meal.

So if a father takes upon himself to go to the mikveh every Friday, he must continue unless it is annulled, but he cannot bind his children. The Maharam Schick ruled that someone whose family does not eat gebrokts on Pesah, living in a community that does, is not bound once he sets up his own home.

Communal customs

The Mishnah in Pesahim says that where the custom is to work on Erev Pesah, one may work, and where it is not, one may not. Someone visiting another place with no intention of staying keeps the stringencies of both places. Someone who moves permanently follows his new home. In answer to a question, Dayan Kada agreed that this is the starting point for the discussion of a visitor to Israel and the second day of Yom Tov.

Place or people? Does "the custom of the place" mean the geographical town, or the community living there? This has huge consequences for the communities that moved from Yemen and Iraq to Israel in the last century. The general consensus, following the Rivash, is that the custom belongs to the community and moves with it, and it remains binding on later generations on a rabbinic level. The Beit Yosef suggests that even a communal custom can be annulled through hatarat nedarim. This is not the accepted view, but it can be weighed together with other factors.

Customs that follow a minority view. The Rashba was asked whether a community could keep a custom based on one opinion when the Rif and the Rambam agreed against it. He answered yes, citing the Gemara. In the town of Rabbi Yosei HaGelili they ate poultry with milk, according to his view, and in the town of Rabbi Eliezer they prepared the knife for a berit milah on Shabbat.

But the custom needs a basis. The halacha is unanimous that a Torah reading may not stop within three verses of the end of a paragraph. He had read that in some places the Monday and Thursday reading of Parashat Vaera ended one verse early, to finish on the promise of the land rather than on the people's hard labour. With no authority to rely on, he said, such a custom should not be continued. By contrast, the Yemenite custom to eat meat during the Nine Days until the afternoon before Tisha BeAv has a basis in the sources.

When communities move

An individual who moves permanently to a place with an established community follows its customs. Everyone agrees on this. The hard question is what happens when a whole community arrives in a place that already has a custom.

The Beit Yosef. Before settling in Safed, Rabbi Yosef Karo was the rabbi of Nikopol in Bulgaria. Jews from Hungary settled in nearby Pleven. In his responsa, Avkat Rokhel 212, he ruled that those who come from a stricter place to a more lenient one, without intending to return, follow the place they have come to, and that the Ashkenazim of Pleven may eat from the Sefaradi shehitah. They are not even allowed to be stricter, because of the quarrels it would cause, and this holds even if they become the majority. Rav Ovadia Yosef relied on this often, holding that the Land of Israel is atra deMaran, the place of the Shulhan Arukh, where everyone should follow him.

The Maharashdam, his contemporary, disagreed. Since a communal custom belongs to the community, a community that moves brings its custom with it, and two communities can exist in one place.

Where there was no community. If a community moves to a place with no existing custom, its custom becomes the custom of that place. Medieval England had Ashkenazi communities, including Baalei HaTosafot in London, Lincoln and York, until the expulsion of 1290. The Mishnah Berurah rules that where communal life has not continued, those who come later start afresh. So when the Spanish and Portuguese Jews came in 1656 and later founded Bevis Marks, they established the custom of England.

He gave a striking example. The London Beth Din still spells the River Thames in a get the way the Sefaradim first wrote it, with an aleph and not an ayin, because a place name in a get, once established, is not changed. He agreed that this is a special law of gittin; for general customs the Maharashdam would let a later community keep its own.

The challenge today

In Israel today a Yemenite community can live on one corner and a Lithuanian one in the next building. The Beit Yosef's approach would say everyone should follow the Shulhan Arukh, but this is disputed because whole communities came across. Rav Ben-Sion Meir Hai Uziel, the first Sefaradi Chief Rabbi of the State of Israel, hoped the differences would fall away and was willing to compromise for it, for example on yibum; Rav Ovadia Yosef disagreed, holding that a misvah cannot be given up. The vision was not realised.

Where there is a single established community, the answer is still clear: newcomers follow it. But in most of the Jewish world today there is no single community, people marry across traditions, "and there isn't a tidy solution."

Summary

  • Customs are a third body of binding authority, alongside the Torah and rabbinic law, and they shape our identity.
  • A personal practice taken on knowingly and kept regularly has the rabbinic force of a vow, but not if it was taken on by mistake, and a single break does not end it.
  • The consensus is that there is no binding family custom apart from the community.
  • A communal custom belongs to the community and moves with it. It may follow a minority view, but not something against clear halacha.
  • When communities meet, the Beit Yosef says newcomers follow the established custom and the Maharashdam says they keep their own, a question that remains open in Israel and the diaspora today.
