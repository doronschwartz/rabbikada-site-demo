Topic: Does Lo Tasur apply after the Sanhedrin, to whom, and to what kinds of questions Shiur: Part 2 of a four-part series given by Dayan Daniel Kada in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 9 May 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we left off

The Rabbi began with a review. The Beit Din HaGadol began with Moshe and the seventy elders and later took the Greek name Sanhedrin. It was exiled from the Lishkat HaGazit in the Temple but continued to convene until about the fourth century. On whether you must follow its ruling when you know it is wrong, Rashi and the Ramban say yes, even if they tell you right is left; the Rambam says a talmid hakham who knows they erred does not follow them.

Tonight had three parts: what authority rabbis have after the Sanhedrin, from the fourth century until today; the authority of the Talmud; and the scope of rabbinic authority. Who counts as a hakham would wait for next week.

Three things Lo Tasur covers

The Rambam lists three categories in which we must follow the Beit Din HaGadol: - Tradition. What was handed down, such as "peri es hadar" meaning an etrog. - Interpretation. What they derived through the thirteen principles of interpretation, for example that "do not cook a kid in its mother's milk" also forbids eating meat and milk together. - Enactments. Their own decrees and institutions, like the misvot of Purim or waiting between meat and milk.

The clear implication is that the misvah applies only to the Beit Din HaGadol. The Rambam says the same in Sefer HaMisvot: when there is no Sanhedrin, there is no misvah of Lo Tasur.

The other view: the hakhamim of every generation

Not everyone agreed. The Ran, Rabbenu Nissim of Girona, writes that just as the Sanhedrin were the teachers of Torah, the same power belongs to the great Torah sages of Israel in every generation. The Sefer HaHinukh, written in Barcelona around the thirteenth century, says the misvah includes listening to the great hakham of our own time, and whoever ignores the counsel of the great sages violates a positive commandment.

The Minhat Hinukh, about two hundred years ago, asks where they get this from. The verses speak of going up to "the place Hashem chooses", the court in Jerusalem. He leaves the question open, and the Rabbi agreed that the plain reading of the verses favours the Rambam.

Still, those who say it applies today have a well-known principle behind them: "Yiftah in his generation is like Shemuel in his generation." If you live in a generation whose rabbis are far below those of old, you listen to the rabbis you have.

The Rambam and the Gaon of Bavel

This was a live dispute in the Rambam's time. After the Talmud was sealed, the Geonim in Bavel saw themselves as its continuation and expected their rulings to bind communities everywhere. The Gaon Shemuel ben Eli objected to rulings in the Mishneh Torah that differed from his. The Rambam answered that the Gaon had authority over his own town and those who accepted him, not over the Rambam in Egypt. The exchange became very sharp on both sides.

Today, the Rabbi said, no one would claim such reach. Even the Beit Din HaGadol in Jerusalem, the highest court of the Rabbanut, with local courts of three across the country beneath it, does not expect Jews worldwide to follow its every ruling.

Why does the Gemara bind us?

According to the Ran and the Hinukh the answer is simple: the Amoraim were the hakhamim of their generation. According to the Rambam, who limits Lo Tasur to the Sanhedrin, the Gemara binds us only because all of Israel accepted it. If all the hakhamim of the world, or most of them, were to convene and agree on something today, and it was accepted, that too would bind.

The Rabbi then asked a sharp question. If Lo Tasur includes rabbinic enactments, then every derabbanan becomes a deoraita. Eating milk after meat would violate a Torah prohibition. Why then do we say that a doubt in a rabbinic law is treated leniently?

The Ramban answers that Lo Tasur covers only the first two categories, tradition and interpretation. Rabbinic enactments are not part of it. Why then do we follow them? Because, the Ramban says, the Hakhamim had a kind of insight into what Hashem wants when they made them. For the Rambam, an enactment is closer to sound legislation, accepted by the whole people.

Dayanim who err

The Shulhan Arukh (Hoshen Mishpat 25) rules that if a dayan errs in something explicit in the Mishnah, the Gemara or the poskim, the case is reopened. The Rema adds that if a dayan, with strong proofs, disagrees with later poskim, he may rule as he understands. The Vilna Gaon writes that no one may argue with the Gemara, but beyond that a hakham may disagree.

The Rabbi recalled the saying of his Rosh Yeshivah: you may argue with the Rashba, but you would be foolish to do so. The Vilna Gaon himself argued freely with the Rishonim. So, in theory, a great enough hakham may disagree with the rabbis of the last five hundred years. In practice, you would need to be a very great hakham.

Your own rabbi

So why listen to your community rabbi? Because of local acceptance. A generation or two ago, in Syria, Iraq, Morocco, Tunisia or in Europe, a town normally had one rabbi, and everyone accepted his rulings. One rabbi cannot rule for other communities, though a community without a hakham might write to a distant one, as the Jews of Bombay wrote to the Ben Ish Hai.

Today it is much harder. A city like London has many kinds of communities. The Rabbi gave the example of accepting Shabbat early in summer: if a whole town accepts it, all are bound, but today some synagogues accept it at half past eight and others pray at nine. Where a single community does accept one authority, as in a Hasidic town in America whose rabbinic court recently banned the use of AI chat tools, a resident who ignores the ruling may arguably be in breach of Lo Tasur.

What questions are covered?

The Sefer HaHinukh frames the misvah very broadly: whatever the sages see, whether from their own understanding, from the Torah, or from its secrets, you must follow them. This is the source people quote when a rabbi tells his followers how to vote, and they say that not to follow is to violate Lo Tasur.

The class analysed this together. Is it the Sanhedrin? No. Has everyone accepted it? No. Two great rabbis may give opposite instructions, so whom would you be "veering" from? And is it a halachic question at all? It is very hard, the Rabbi said, to argue that this is Lo Tasur. Someone suggested that great rabbis see the bigger picture. That may be the psychology, he answered, but it is not a halachic obligation.

No psak on hashkafa

Finally, the Rambam writes (in his commentary on Mishnah Sotah) that there is no psak on matters of belief. A court can rule that "peri es hadar" is an etrog and not a lemon, and you must follow. But a belief cannot be decided by a vote.

According to the Rambam, even if every rabbi today convened and declared a view in hashkafa forbidden, it would not bind, because there is psak in halacha, not in hashkafa.

Summary

  • The Rambam limits Lo Tasur to the Sanhedrin; the Ran and the Sefer HaHinukh extend it to the great hakhamim of every generation, though the verses favour the Rambam.
  • According to the Rambam, the Gemara binds because all Israel accepted it; after that, a rabbi's authority rests on local acceptance.
  • The Ramban holds that Lo Tasur does not include rabbinic enactments, which is why a doubt in a derabbanan is treated leniently.
  • Political instructions are very hard to classify as Lo Tasur on any view.
  • There is no psak on hashkafa. Next week: who is a hakham, and what is Daat Torah?
