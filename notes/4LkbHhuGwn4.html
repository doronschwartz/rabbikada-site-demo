These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi said this class would finish Siman 11 and cover Siman 12, both relatively straightforward, before the long Siman 13. Seifim 1 and 2 of Siman 11 had the big debate of the Rishonim. Seif 4 is about the procedure for taking testimony that a woman was unfaithful.

Siman 11, Seif 4: testimony about unfaithfulness

The Shulhan Arukh says that some say (yesh omrim) that witnesses who come to say a woman was unfaithful need drishah vehakirah (full cross-examination). The Rema adds that the testimony is accepted only in front of her and in front of her husband.

1. Cross-examination. This is the view of Tosafot, the Smag, the Rosh and many others. The Gemara in Yevamot discusses witnesses who say a man died. There it concludes that cross-examination is not needed, because money is involved (she receives her ketubah from his estate), and in monetary law we do not require it. Where only a prohibition is at stake, as here, it is required. The Rashba is one of those who disagree. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the majority.

The Rabbi mentioned a view in the Pithei Teshuvah, from the Noda BiYehudah, that for other matters in the siman two witnesses are enough without cross-examination, and only for actual unfaithfulness is it needed. The wording here was not clear in the recording.

2. In front of the parties. As with all testimony, it is taken in front of the litigants: her and her husband. If she has already married the man in question (the nitan), he must be present as well, since he is now her husband. If she has not married him, he need not be, just as a zonah becomes forbidden to everyone without everyone being present.

After the fact. The Beit Shemuel rules that testimony taken without these conditions is not valid, and cannot be repaired by the same witnesses repeating it. Rabbi Akiva Eger, quoting the Meiri, suggests it might be accepted bedieved, since it is to the husband's benefit.

Why "some say"? A question from the class was why the Shulhan Arukh uses this wording for what is the majority view. The Rabbi answered that it often does so when the law is not in the Gemara but comes from the Rishonim, and mentioned the Hida's note on this: it can mean he does not disagree, but found the law in only one or two sources.

Siman 12: the one who brings a get, or testifies that the husband died

The Mishnah says a man who brings a get, or a witness who says the husband died, may not marry that woman, because of the suspicion that he lied in order to marry her. This holds even if he says "I killed him". Why?

The Rabbi asked which case is more serious. If a witness lies that the husband died, she is a married woman wrongly freed to the whole world. With a get, even a lying shaliah leaves a get that may well have been written properly (lishmah), so the suspicion is smaller. For "I killed him", the Gemara says we split his words (palginan dibura): we do not believe the confession to punish him, but we accept it to free the woman.

The last part of the Mishnah: if two witnesses say they killed him together, each may marry her. One man might lie to marry her, but two cannot both marry her, so we do not suspect them.

Nowadays the get is given with "before me it was written and before me it was signed" everywhere, so the law is fully relevant.

Seif 1: the shaliah and the one witness

The Shulhan Arukh says one who brings a get, and likewise one witness to the husband's death, may not marry the woman. If he did, he need not divorce her (kenas lo yotsi). If two witnesses testify, each may marry her, but the Rema adds that it is still baal nefesh yirhak mizeh: a person who cares for his conscience should keep away.

The commentators:

  • Witnesses in the town. The Tur (Siman 141) says that even if the witnesses of the get are in the town, the shaliah may not marry her, since the permission came through him. The Beit Shemuel and the Radbaz (in the Pithei Teshuvah) add that even if the witnesses came afterwards and confirmed the get, he still may not, because from the moment she became permitted through him she became forbidden to him.
  • In Eres Yisrael. There the shaliah need not say "before me" at all. The Beit Shemuel asks what happens if he did say it anyway, and concludes he need not keep away, since he did not need to say it. The Rashba says that if he did say it, there is a problem. Since nowadays it is said everywhere, in the Diaspora or in Israel, a shaliah through whom she became permitted may never marry that woman.
  • How many witnesses. The Rema, from Tosafot, says even two should keep away. The Beit Shemuel says even three or four. The Hida, unlike the Beit Shemuel, says three are enough.

The central mahloket: what is the concern? The Noda BiYehudah says the Rema misread Tosafot. Tosafot spoke of a case where both are needed, such as the witnesses who sign the get (edei hatimah) and those who see it delivered (edei mesirah). Without both, the get fails, so the act happens partly through them, and it is not proper for them to marry her. Where one witness would be enough and two simply came, there is no concern. The Beit Shemuel and the Taz take it more broadly: any involvement in making her permitted makes it improper, whether there are three or four, or whether one would have been enough.

In practice:

  • A shaliah of a get, anywhere, who made her permitted may never marry her.
  • The two sets of witnesses to a get should not marry her either, since both are needed. The Rabbi linked this to Siman 130, where witnesses to a get may be paid for the inconvenience of being unable to marry her. Some disagree, since medin they could.

If he married her anyway (kenas). The Gemara says kenas lo yotsi about the hakham of Seif 2, not about this case. The Rashba limits it to the hakham, where even a wrongful act left a valid get. For a witness or shaliah, where she may still be a married woman, he is stricter. The Shulhan Arukh, the Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel hold kenas lo yotsi here too, since the prohibition is rabbinic and the get may still be valid. The Bah follows the Rashba. The passage was not clear in the recording.

The Beit Shemuel adds that in this seif "if he married her" means a full marriage (nisuin), not just kiddushin. For the hakham in the next seif he accepts even kiddushin, since the concern is only a lack of care.

Seif 2: the hakham who could not release a vow

A woman took a vow never to benefit from her husband, which ends married life. She came to a hakham to release the vow (hatarat nedarim), and he said he could not find an opening (petah). The husband then divorced her. May the hakham now marry her? The suspicion is that he did not search hard because he wanted her. He may not, but if he did, he need not divorce her.

If it was three laymen (hedyotot) who sat as a beit din, each may marry her medin, but following Tosafot and the Noda BiYehudah, since all three were needed it is not proper. The Rabbi said this is less relevant today.

Seifim 3 and 4: the exceptions

Seif 3. If she married another man in between and was divorced or widowed, all of them may marry her. The suspicion is gone, because there was a break.

Seif 4. The Mishnah gives three exceptions:

  1. A relative of the witness, shaliah or hakham may marry her, since no one lies for someone else.
  2. She married someone else in between (Seif 3).
  3. He already had a wife when he testified, and she died, or he divorced her because of a quarrel.

The Gemara has a difficulty: the Mishnah says if the wife dies he may marry her, but if he divorced her he may not, while the Braita says even if he divorced her. The Rabbi gave the plain reading: if there was already trouble at home when he testified, he had a motive to free this woman. If the quarrel began later, we do not suspect him. Rashi, he said, reads it the other way round. The Rishonim and the Shulhan Arukh follow the version that asks who started the quarrel: if she caused it, he may marry her at once; if he started it, he may not.

The Rema adds that if his wife was ill, he should not marry her. The Beit Yoseph explains this is not because he expects her to die, but because with a sick wife he may think of taking a second wife. A view in the Pithei Teshuvah says that nowadays, since a man does not take two wives, there is no concern.

Practical summary

  • Witnesses to a woman's unfaithfulness need cross-examination and must testify in front of her and her husband (and the nitan, if she married him).
  • A shaliah of a get, anywhere, who made her permitted may never marry her. The witnesses of the get should not either.
  • One witness to a husband's death may not marry the woman. Two may, but the Rema says it is still proper to keep away.
  • A hakham who could not release a vow should not marry the woman, but if he did, he need not divorce her.
  • The exceptions are a relative, a woman who married someone else in between, and a man who already had a wife who died, or whom he divorced because of her quarrel.
