A question community rabbis are asked

After a series on conversion in the summer, the Rabbi began a set of classes on Even HaEzer questions that come up in communal rabbinic life. One of the most common: a couple who respect Jewish tradition but would like to see change ask whether their wedding can be a little more egalitarian. Traditionally the bride does very little. She stands and receives.

What the bride does

There are two parts to a Jewish wedding: erusin, the betrothal, and nisuin, the formal marriage. In the time of the Gemara they could be a year apart; today both are under the huppah. After erusin the couple may not yet live together, but she cannot marry anyone else without a proper get. Without one, children of a later union would be mamzerim.

Of the ways a woman can be betrothed, every wedding today uses money (kesef). The groom gives something of value before two witnesses and says, "Harei at mekudeshet li betabaat zo kedat Moshe veYisrael." For centuries the custom has been a ring. The bride's role is acceptance. Her consent is essential, and she shows it by holding out her finger.

With feminism on the rise, some brides want an active part: if he puts a ring on me, I will put one on him. Rav Moshe Feinstein was asked about exactly this in the early 1970s, "which is remarkable, that already even then these questions were being asked."

He takes her

The Shulhan Arukh (Even HaEzer 27) rules that if the man says "I am your husband" or "I am betrothed to you", there is no kiddushin at all, even if they had been discussing marriage. His words actively spoil it, because the verse requires that he take her, not give himself to her.

The Rabbi explained this with an idea Rabbi Tatz often develops. The masculine stands for potential, a burst of energy. The feminine condenses creative energy into tangible form, as a child is conceived in a moment and developed by the mother over nine months. "Hear the discipline of your father, and do not forsake the Torah of your mother" (Mishlei 1:8): the father may teach the abstract Torah, but the mother makes it real. So the process of marriage begins with him.

The Pithei Teshuvah adds that if he says "you are my wife, and I am your husband", it works. He has taken her to him first, and the extra words do not undo that.

Rav Moshe's first teshuvah

Rav Moshe wrote four teshuvot on the subject. In the first, addressed to an Orthodox rabbi, the groom gave the ring with the usual words, and then the bride gave him a ring and said he was betrothed to her. Rav Moshe ruled that this is certainly a valid kiddushin and she needs a get. The moment he gave her the ring, she was betrothed. What she did afterwards is nothing. Even if the rabbi and witnesses knew in advance what the couple planned, the couple cannot make the kiddushin depend on both of them giving.

But do not do it, he wrote, for two reasons. First, it is the custom of non-Jews (hukkot hagoyim), the exchange of rings and vows in church. The Rabbi noted that this is a dispute among the Rishonim, and that Rav Moshe holds even a custom unconnected to idolatry can count. He mentions it in one line and does not lean on it. His main reason is that if every wedding has her giving too, people will forget the halacha. They will think a woman can betroth a man, or that both must give, and that, he wrote, is a serious distortion.

Questions on the first teshuvah

Someone asked about the value of the rings. If his ring for her costs £300 and hers for him costs £400, has he given her nothing? Not at all, the Rabbi said. The moment he gave her a ring worth something, the kiddushin was complete. If she then gives him a gift from her own money, that does not cancel it. The problem is different: if he gives her something and she hands the same thing straight back, that can look like a kinyan halifin, and that must not be done.

The Reform teshuvah

In a later teshuvah Rav Moshe considered a woman married by a Reform minister. Does she need a get? No. The witnesses were probably not kosher, though not always, since an observant relative might serve. But even with kosher witnesses, he held, there was no kiddushin: because she gives him a ring as he gives her one, they are showing that this is an exchange of gifts.

He then compares it with civil marriage. Rav Moshe's ruling on civil marriage is that a get should be given as a stringency. The Rabbi said that in the Beit Din, where the husband could not be traced, they have allowed a woman to remarry without one. But if the husband is available and willing, it is good to obtain it. Why, then, not at least require a get as a stringency after a Reform marriage? Because a couple with no religious ceremony may have meant their life together to make them married. A Reform couple think the marriage took place under the huppah, so nothing afterwards is done for the sake of marriage. A Reform marriage is, in this sense, "worse than a civil marriage". He adds that where there are children, one should not require a get.

Context

The same act, she gives him a ring, gives opposite results. In an Orthodox context we assume everyone intends a proper kiddushin, and her ring is nothing. In a Reform context the intention is mistaken from the start and it is all an exchange of gifts. When Rav Moshe was asked about the apparent contradiction, he answered that context gives the whole thing its meaning.

The Rabbi drew a lesson for future community rabbis. Many of the weddings they will do are for people who are not Orthodox in practice or belief, yet who still feel that to do things properly you go to the Orthodox. That is why it matters so much to have Orthodox bodies that marry and divorce such couples properly.

Asked how to define a Reform rabbi, he said it is not where someone was ordained but who they are and what they believe, and the witnesses matter as much as the officiant.

Conservative weddings, and a rabbi under pressure

On Conservative weddings Rav Moshe was much more equivocal: perhaps it is a kiddushin, perhaps an exchange of gifts. The Rabbi doubted he would be as equivocal today.

In the same teshuvah Rav Moshe addresses a situation "you may need to know in your careers". If an Orthodox rabbi's position is at risk because, say, an important family insists on the bride giving a ring under the huppah, he may go ahead, but should announce that the kiddushin is through the groom's ring and hers is only a gift.

A possible way forward

Is there an elegant solution for a bride who will not marry Orthodox otherwise? The Rabbi described an idea he had read a few years earlier, possibly from Rabbi Daniel Sperber. Among Oriental Sefaradim the ketubah is read and its kinyan made under the huppah, between the kiddushin and the sheva berakhot. The kinyan does not need the rabbi's pen or handkerchief; anyone can hand the groom the object. So after the kiddushin is complete, the bride could hand him a ring and say, in front of everyone, that by lifting it he accepts all the obligations of the ketubah. That is clearly about the ketubah, not the kiddushin.

He was emphatic about the limits. "I'm not advocating doing this. Don't get me wrong." It is untested in England as far as he knew, and people who do not know the difference may still think the ketubah is the kiddushin, "which is why I think this is a dangerous solution." It is for cases where the alternative is a couple leaving Orthodoxy altogether.

He also answered practical questions. The groom would have to lift the ring as a kinyan, not merely wear it. In current practice, brides who want to give a ring are told to do it in the yihud room, or at the end of the ceremony while the civil register is signed after the glass is broken, and most are happy with that. And if a bride jumped the gun and gave her ring before the groom, he would pause the ceremony, take a break, and start afresh, since her ring had done nothing.

Practical summary

  • The ideal is the traditional form: he gives her the ring with the words, and nothing more under the huppah.
  • If she also gives him a ring at an Orthodox wedding, the kiddushin is valid and a get would be needed.
  • In Rav Moshe's view a Reform double-ring ceremony is no kiddushin, and needs no get even as a stringency. Conservative is doubtful.
  • A bride who wants to give a ring can do so in the yihud room or at the end of the ceremony.
  • Using her ring for the ketubah kinyan is an untested idea, not a recommendation.
