Siman 448 – Hames After Pesah (דין חמץ שעבר עליו הפסח)

Contains 7 Se'ifim

Note: The Shulhan Arukh does not have a dedicated section on Mekhirat Hames (selling hames). The laws of selling hames appear within this siman, as a natural extension of the discussion on hames left over after Pesah.

Se'if 1 – A Non-Jew's Hames After Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: Hames that belonged to a non-Jew throughout Pesah is permitted after Pesah — even to eat.

  • Why? No prohibition was violated. The non-Jew was not subject to any restriction on owning hames.
  • Note: There was an opinion in the Gemara that even a non-Jew's hames would be prohibited after Pesah, but the halacha rejects this.

Se'if 2 – Receiving a Gift of Hames from a Non-Jew on Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: If a non-Jew brings a Jewish person a gift of hames during Pesah (even on the 8th day, which is de-rabbanan):

  1. Do not accept it — accepting creates ownership, which violates Bal Yera'eh / Bal Yimatzei.
  2. Do not imply willingness to accept — e.g., saying "leave it in my house, I'll have it after Pesah" constitutes implied consent, and your chatzer (property) would acquire it on your behalf.
  3. It is good (though not strictly required) to explicitly state: "I do not want to take ownership of this."

Why the Emphasis on the 8th Day?

The Beit Yoseph explains: this halacha applies throughout Pesah, but the chiddush is that even on the 8th day (which is only de-rabbanan outside of Israel), you still cannot accept hames.

The Chatzer Koneh Principle

  • Normally, your property (chatzer) automatically acquires ownerless items or gifts placed within it — this is called chatzer koneh.
  • However, nothing can be acquired against your will. If you don't want your chatzer to acquire the hames, it doesn't.
  • The Rosh says: You don't even need to explicitly state that you don't want your chatzer to acquire it. The default assumption (stam) is that during Pesah, a person would not want their property to acquire hames. It's implicit.
  • However: If you do something that implies consent (e.g., putting it in a cupboard, saying "I'll have it after Pesah"), then you've given authority to your chatzer to acquire — and that's a problem.

Practical Scenario: Hames Delivered Through Your Letterbox

  • If a company delivers hames through your letterbox on Pesah (e.g., promotional Weetabix), by default your chatzer doesn't acquire it (you wouldn't want hames during Pesah).
  • Don't pick it up and put it in a cupboard — that implies consent.
  • Do: Leave it where it is. Ideally, say explicitly "I don't want my chatzer to acquire this." Cover it with a utensil (as we learned in Siman 446). After Pesah, you can acquire it.

If the Non-Jew's Hames Is in Your House (Not Yours)

If hames is in your house but doesn't belong to you (non-Jew left it, or you explicitly rejected ownership):

  • You still need to build a mechitzah (partition) of at least 10 tefachim (approx. 80cm) or at minimum cover it — so you don't forget and come to eat it (as learned in Siman 440).

Se'if 3 – The Core Din: Hames She'avar Alav Ha-Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: Hames that a Jewish person owned over Pesah — asur be-hana'ah (forbidden to benefit from) after Pesah.

This is even if the person:

Circumstance

Still Forbidden?

Shogeg (forgetful — forgot to get rid of it)

✅ Yes — forbidden

Ones (completely helpless — no way to do anything about it)

✅ Yes — forbidden

Why So Severe?

  • This is a kenas (penalty) instituted by the Hachamim.
  • The Hachamim observed that people were lax about getting rid of hames before Pesah. When Hazal impose very strict penalties, it typically indicates they saw widespread laxity.
  • Parallel: The Hachamim similarly banned bathing in hot water on Shabbat (even if heated before Shabbat) because people were lying about when they heated the water.

Even Ones?

  • Normally, ones carries zero culpability — you had no choice, no way out (e.g., you were in prison, on a voyage, an onein dealing with a funeral).
  • Shogeg (forgetfulness) has some culpability — you didn't check carefully enough.
  • Even so, the Hachamim applied the penalty to both — because people would claim "I was ones" to circumvent the rule.
  • The blanket rule: Any hames owned by a Jew over Pesah is permanently forbidden — no exceptions for intent or circumstances.

What Must You Do?

  • The hames must be destroyed — pour it down the drain, throw it away, etc.
  • It is forbidden for any Jewish person — not just the owner.
  • You cannot sell it, eat it, or benefit from it in any way.

One Leniency: Safek (Doubt)

  • If there is a genuine doubt whether the hames was owned by a Jew over Pesah → one may be lenient.
  • Why? Because this entire prohibition is de-rabbanan, and safek de-rabbanan le-kula (in cases of rabbinic doubt, we are lenient).

Practical Ramifications of Hames She'avar Alav Ha-Pesah

Buying from Jewish shops after Pesah:

  • You must ensure the shop either has a hekhsher (the entire shop is under a kashrut authority that handles mechirat hames) or that the owner reliably did mechirat hames.
  • If the owner did not sell properly or at all — it is forbidden for any Jew to buy from that shop's hames stock.

Eating at a non-religious Jewish friend's home after Pesah:

  • If they serve pasta, bread, or other non-perishable hames immediately after Pesah — there's a concern it was owned over Pesah.
  • If it's clearly something that could have been bought fresh the morning after Pesah — it may be fine.
  • If it's a genuine safek — you can be lenient (safek de-rabbanan le-kula).
  • If it's highly likely or certain that the hames was owned over Pesah — asur. No way around it, however uncomfortable.

Se'if 3 (Continued) – The Solution: Selling or Gifting to a Non-Jew Before Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: If, before Pesah, you sold or gave as a gift your hames to a non-Jew — the din of hames she'avar alav ha-Pesah does not apply. After Pesah, the hames is fully permitted.

Conditions:

  1. Outright gift or complete sale — must be genuine with no conditions attached.
  2. Even if the Jew and non-Jew are friends and the Jew knows the non-Jew will keep it and return it after Pesah — it's still valid.
  3. Even if sold for a small amount — valid (mechira gemura be-davar mu'at).
  4. No tanai (conditions) — cannot say "I'm giving it on condition you return it" (see below on Matana Al Menat Lehachzir).

The Laws of Mekhirat Hames (Selling Hames) — Detailed Discussion

Origins and History

  • The Talmud does not explicitly discuss mechirat hames as we practice it today.
  • The Tosefta (Pesachim) describes someone at sea who cannot do bi'ur hames — he can do a kinyan with a non-Jew to transfer ownership, even remotely. This is the only original source.
  • The Rambam codifies this — notably, in the context of someone who is unable to destroy their hames (on a voyage, etc.).
  • The Sdeh Hemed (approx. 250 years ago) writes that the widespread practice of institutional mechirat hames began with Ashkenazi shopkeepers in Europe who had large quantities of hames stock and couldn't afford to destroy it all.
  • Originally, individuals would sell directly to their non-Jewish neighbour — a simple, personal transaction. The institutional community-wide sale through a rabbi is a later development.
  • Hacham Ovadia wrote at length defending the current practice, saying there is plenty to rely upon.

Ideal Practice

Everyone would agree: the ideal is to finish your hames before Pesah, not to sell it. Plan ahead — use up pastas, flour, bread, etc., in the weeks before Pesah. Mekhirat hames should be a backup for items you genuinely cannot consume or dispose of (e.g., expensive whiskey).

Taking the Hames Out of the House

Shulhan Arukh writes: You sell or give it to a non-Jew outside your house (mi-chutz la-bayit) before Pesah.

Two readings of this requirement:

Reading

Explanation

Practical Consequence

Strict reading (many Aharonim, Pri Hadash)

You must physically remove the hames from your house for it to feel like a genuine sale. If it stays in your house, the concern is: (a) it doesn't feel like a real sale; (b) you might come to eat it.

You'd need the non-Jew to come and take the hames out — which we don't do nowadays.

Lenient reading (other Aharonim)

"Mi-chutz la-bayit" doesn't mean literally take it out — it means do a proper kinyan (of which meshicha / physical transfer is one common method). Other kinyanim work even if hames stays in your house.

The hames can remain in your home as long as a valid kinyan was performed.

The solution that satisfies both readings:

Together with selling the hames, you also rent/lease the area/space where the hames is stored to the non-Jew (makneh the area space). This means:

  • The space no longer belongs to you — it belongs to the non-Jew.
  • The non-Jew has authority and permission to enter and take from that place.
  • Even according to the strict reading, it's no longer considered "your house" (beitcha) — the concern is alleviated.

This is what we do in practice today — the mechirat hames document includes renting/leasing the storage area together with selling the hames.

The pasuk says "ve-kano mi-yad amitecha" — "acquire from your fellow [Jew]." The Gemara derives: whatever kinyan works for a Jew from the Torah does NOT work for a non-Jew from the Torah.

In practice today: The mechirat hames document incorporates all of these kinyanim together — kesef, chalipin, chatzer, agav karka, contract, handshake — to ensure the sale is valid according to every opinion.

It Must Be a Genuine Sale

  • If the non-Jew decided to keep all the hames and not sell it back — that's his right. The Jew cannot sue him in non-Jewish courts.
  • If you did sue — it would make a mockery of the entire institution.
  • Rav Moshe Feinstein was asked about a non-religious shopkeeper who did mechirat hames but told others "I don't really believe in this." Rav Moshe ruled: no problem — the person signed, gave authority to the rabbi, and the kinyan was performed. Internal thoughts don't invalidate a legal transaction. Only if an action demonstrated insincerity would it be a problem.

Pricing

  • The Shulhan Arukh writes you can sell "even for something small" (davar mu'at).
  • Some poskim were concerned that selling a huge amount of hames for very little money implies the sale isn't genuine.
  • Counter-argument: Because of the urgency to avoid transgression, a person is willing to sell cheaply.
  • In practice today: The sale is for the full estimated value. The non-Jew pays a first instalment (e.g., £50–100). The remainder is a debt (zekufah alav be-milveh). After Pesah, the Jew buys it back — cancelling the debt.

Specifying the Hames

The Biur Halacha and Hacham Ovadia emphasise: you should specify where your hames is on the sale form.

  • Good: "In the kitchen, third cabinet above the sink; garage shelf, bottom row."
  • Not good: "All the hames in my house." (Too vague — weakens the sale.)

Matana Al Menat Lehachzir Does NOT Work

Matana al menat lehachzir = a gift given on the condition that it be returned.

  • Normally in halacha, this is a valid gift (shemah matana — it has the legal status of a gift).
  • On Pesah, it does NOT work. Why? Because if the condition isn't fulfilled (the non-Jew doesn't return it), the gift is retroactively void — meaning you owned hames the entire time.
  • Therefore: no conditions (tanai) of any kind may be attached to the sale or gift of hames. It must be an outright, unconditional transfer.

Se'if 5 – Even with Bitul, the Penalty Applies

Shulhan Arukh: Even if you did bitul hames (said Kol Chamira), but hames was found in your house after Pesah — it is still forbidden (hames she'avar alav ha-Pesah).

  • Also: Simply declaring it hefker (ownerless) doesn't help either — you didn't physically remove or sell it.
  • The Hachamim's penalty applies regardless of bitul or hefker — only a genuine sale or gift to a non-Jew exempts you.

Se'if 6 – Feeding Hames to Animals on Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: It is forbidden to feed hames to any animal on Pesah — even other people's animals, even stray animals.

Why can't you feed a stray animal?

  • The Mishnah Berurah explains: there is hana'ah (benefit/satisfaction) in the act of feeding an animal. Every human has an innate instinct to care for animals — feeding a stray cat or dog gives you a sense of satisfaction. That emotional benefit constitutes hana'ah from hames.
  • Also: Even hefker hames that you find on the street — you cannot pick it up to feed animals, because by picking it up, you would acquire it (kinyan).

If a non-Jew asks you to feed his animal on Pesah with hames — you may not.

Se'if 7 – Pet Food on Pesah

Shulhan Arukh / Poskim: You cannot feed your pet hames-based food on Pesah.

  • Pet food containing hames grains belongs to you — you bought it for the dog/cat.
  • It's fitting for animal consumption — so it has the halachic status of hames.
  • You are violating Bal Yera'eh / Bal Yimatzei — owning hames on Pesah. This is potentially an isur de'oraita.
  • It doesn't matter that you're not eating it — the prohibition is on owning it.
