Siman 448 – Chametz After Pesach (דין חמץ שעבר עליו הפסח)

Contains 7 Se'ifim

Note: The Shulchan Aruch does not have a dedicated section on Mechirat Chametz (selling chametz). The laws of selling chametz appear within this siman, as a natural extension of the discussion on chametz left over after Pesach.

Se'if 1 – A Non-Jew's Chametz After Pesach

Shulchan Aruch: Chametz that belonged to a non-Jew throughout Pesach is permitted after Pesach — even to eat.

  • Why? No prohibition was violated. The non-Jew was not subject to any restriction on owning chametz.
  • Note: There was an opinion in the Gemara that even a non-Jew's chametz would be prohibited after Pesach, but the halacha rejects this.

Se'if 2 – Receiving a Gift of Chametz from a Non-Jew on Pesach

Shulchan Aruch: If a non-Jew brings a Jewish person a gift of chametz during Pesach (even on the 8th day, which is de-rabbanan):

  1. Do not accept it — accepting creates ownership, which violates Bal Yera'eh / Bal Yimatzei.
  2. Do not imply willingness to accept — e.g., saying "leave it in my house, I'll have it after Pesach" constitutes implied consent, and your chatzer (property) would acquire it on your behalf.
  3. It is good (though not strictly required) to explicitly state: "I do not want to take ownership of this."

Why the Emphasis on the 8th Day?

The Beit Yosef explains: this halacha applies throughout Pesach, but the chiddush is that even on the 8th day (which is only de-rabbanan outside of Israel), you still cannot accept chametz.

The Chatzer Koneh Principle

  • Normally, your property (chatzer) automatically acquires ownerless items or gifts placed within it — this is called chatzer koneh.
  • However, nothing can be acquired against your will. If you don't want your chatzer to acquire the chametz, it doesn't.
  • The Rosh says: You don't even need to explicitly state that you don't want your chatzer to acquire it. The default assumption (stam) is that during Pesach, a person would not want their property to acquire chametz. It's implicit.
  • However: If you do something that implies consent (e.g., putting it in a cupboard, saying "I'll have it after Pesach"), then you've given authority to your chatzer to acquire — and that's a problem.

Practical Scenario: Chametz Delivered Through Your Letterbox

  • If a company delivers chametz through your letterbox on Pesach (e.g., promotional Weetabix), by default your chatzer doesn't acquire it (you wouldn't want chametz during Pesach).
  • Don't pick it up and put it in a cupboard — that implies consent.
  • Do: Leave it where it is. Ideally, say explicitly "I don't want my chatzer to acquire this." Cover it with a utensil (as we learned in Siman 446). After Pesach, you can acquire it.

If the Non-Jew's Chametz Is in Your House (Not Yours)

If chametz is in your house but doesn't belong to you (non-Jew left it, or you explicitly rejected ownership):

  • You still need to build a mechitzah (partition) of at least 10 tefachim (approx. 80cm) or at minimum cover it — so you don't forget and come to eat it (as learned in Siman 440).

Se'if 3 – The Core Din: Chametz She'avar Alav Ha-Pesach

Shulchan Aruch: Chametz that a Jewish person owned over Pesach — asur be-hana'ah (forbidden to benefit from) after Pesach.

This is even if the person:

Circumstance

Still Forbidden?

Shogeg (forgetful — forgot to get rid of it)

✅ Yes — forbidden

Ones (completely helpless — no way to do anything about it)

✅ Yes — forbidden

Why So Severe?

  • This is a kenas (penalty) instituted by the Chachamim.
  • The Chachamim observed that people were lax about getting rid of chametz before Pesach. When Chazal impose very strict penalties, it typically indicates they saw widespread laxity.
  • Parallel: The Chachamim similarly banned bathing in hot water on Shabbat (even if heated before Shabbat) because people were lying about when they heated the water.

Even Ones?

  • Normally, ones carries zero culpability — you had no choice, no way out (e.g., you were in prison, on a voyage, an onein dealing with a funeral).
  • Shogeg (forgetfulness) has some culpability — you didn't check carefully enough.
  • Even so, the Chachamim applied the penalty to both — because people would claim "I was ones" to circumvent the rule.
  • The blanket rule: Any chametz owned by a Jew over Pesach is permanently forbidden — no exceptions for intent or circumstances.

What Must You Do?

  • The chametz must be destroyed — pour it down the drain, throw it away, etc.
  • It is forbidden for any Jewish person — not just the owner.
  • You cannot sell it, eat it, or benefit from it in any way.

One Leniency: Safek (Doubt)

  • If there is a genuine doubt whether the chametz was owned by a Jew over Pesach → one may be lenient.
  • Why? Because this entire prohibition is de-rabbanan, and safek de-rabbanan le-kula (in cases of rabbinic doubt, we are lenient).

Practical Ramifications of Chametz She'avar Alav Ha-Pesach

Buying from Jewish shops after Pesach:

  • You must ensure the shop either has a hechsher (the entire shop is under a kashrut authority that handles mechirat chametz) or that the owner reliably did mechirat chametz.
  • Not all Jewish shops in areas like Golders Green and Hendon operate under a hechsher. Some operate independently — you are relying on the owner's personal mechirat chametz.
  • If the owner did not sell properly or at all — it is forbidden for any Jew to buy from that shop's chametz stock.

Eating at a non-religious Jewish friend's home after Pesach:

  • If they serve pasta, bread, or other non-perishable chametz immediately after Pesach — there's a concern it was owned over Pesach.
  • If it's clearly something that could have been bought fresh the morning after Pesach — it may be fine.
  • If it's a genuine safek — you can be lenient (safek de-rabbanan le-kula).
  • If it's highly likely or certain that the chametz was owned over Pesach — asur. No way around it, however uncomfortable.

Se'if 3 (Continued) – The Solution: Selling or Gifting to a Non-Jew Before Pesach

Shulchan Aruch: If, before Pesach, you sold or gave as a gift your chametz to a non-Jew — the din of chametz she'avar alav ha-Pesach does not apply. After Pesach, the chametz is fully permitted.

Conditions:

  1. Outright gift or complete sale — must be genuine with no conditions attached.
  2. Even if the Jew and non-Jew are friends and the Jew knows the non-Jew will keep it and return it after Pesach — it's still valid.
  3. Even if sold for a small amount — valid (mechira gemura be-davar mu'at).
  4. No tanai (conditions) — cannot say "I'm giving it on condition you return it" (see below on Matana Al Menat Lehachzir).

The Laws of Mechirat Chametz (Selling Chametz) — Detailed Discussion

Origins and History

  • The Talmud does not explicitly discuss mechirat chametz as we practice it today.
  • The Tosefta (Pesachim) describes someone at sea who cannot do bi'ur chametz — he can do a kinyan with a non-Jew to transfer ownership, even remotely. This is the only original source.
  • The Rambam codifies this — notably, in the context of someone who is unable to destroy their chametz (on a voyage, etc.).
  • The Sdeh Hemed (approx. 250 years ago) writes that the widespread practice of institutional mechirat chametz began with Ashkenazi shopkeepers in Europe who had large quantities of chametz stock and couldn't afford to destroy it all.
  • Originally, individuals would sell directly to their non-Jewish neighbour — a simple, personal transaction. The institutional community-wide sale through a rabbi is a later development.
  • Chacham Ovadia wrote at length defending the current practice, saying there is plenty to rely upon.

Ideal Practice

Everyone would agree: the ideal is to finish your chametz before Pesach, not to sell it. Plan ahead — use up pastas, flour, bread, etc., in the weeks before Pesach. Mechirat chametz should be a backup for items you genuinely cannot consume or dispose of (e.g., expensive whiskey).

Taking the Chametz Out of the House

Shulchan Aruch writes: You sell or give it to a non-Jew outside your house (mi-chutz la-bayit) before Pesach.

Two readings of this requirement:

Reading

Explanation

Practical Consequence

Strict reading (many Acharonim, Pri Chadash)

You must physically remove the chametz from your house for it to feel like a genuine sale. If it stays in your house, the concern is: (a) it doesn't feel like a real sale; (b) you might come to eat it.

You'd need the non-Jew to come and take the chametz out — which we don't do nowadays.

Lenient reading (other Acharonim)

"Mi-chutz la-bayit" doesn't mean literally take it out — it means do a proper kinyan (of which meshicha / physical transfer is one common method). Other kinyanim work even if chametz stays in your house.

The chametz can remain in your home as long as a valid kinyan was performed.

The solution that satisfies both readings:

Together with selling the chametz, you also rent/lease the area/space where the chametz is stored to the non-Jew (makneh the area space). This means:

  • The space no longer belongs to you — it belongs to the non-Jew.
  • The non-Jew has authority and permission to enter and take from that place.
  • Even according to the strict reading, it's no longer considered "your house" (beitcha) — the concern is alleviated.

This is what we do in practice today — the mechirat chametz document includes renting/leasing the storage area together with selling the chametz.

In practice today: The mechirat chametz document incorporates all of these kinyanim together — kesef, chalipin, chatzer, agav karka, contract, handshake — to ensure the sale is valid according to every opinion.

It Must Be a Genuine Sale

  • If the non-Jew decided to keep all the chametz and not sell it back — that's his right. The Jew cannot sue him in non-Jewish courts.
  • If you did sue — it would make a mockery of the entire institution.
  • Rav Moshe Feinstein was asked about a non-religious shopkeeper who did mechirat chametz but told others "I don't really believe in this." Rav Moshe ruled: no problem — the person signed, gave authority to the rabbi, and the kinyan was performed. Internal thoughts don't invalidate a legal transaction. Only if an action demonstrated insincerity would it be a problem.

Pricing

  • The Shulchan Aruch writes you can sell "even for something small" (davar mu'at).
  • Some poskim were concerned that selling a huge amount of chametz for very little money implies the sale isn't genuine.
  • Counter-argument: Because of the urgency to avoid transgression, a person is willing to sell cheaply.
  • In practice today: The sale is for the full estimated value. The non-Jew pays a first instalment (e.g., £50–100). The remainder is a debt (zekufah alav be-milveh). After Pesach, the Jew buys it back — cancelling the debt.

Specifying the Chametz

The Biur Halacha and Chacham Ovadia emphasise: you should specify where your chametz is on the sale form.

  • Good: "In the kitchen, third cabinet above the sink; garage shelf, bottom row."
  • Not good: "All the chametz in my house." (Too vague — weakens the sale.)

Matana Al Menat Lehachzir Does NOT Work

Matana al menat lehachzir = a gift given on the condition that it be returned.

  • Normally in halacha, this is a valid gift (shemah matana — it has the legal status of a gift).
  • On Pesach, it does NOT work. Why? Because if the condition isn't fulfilled (the non-Jew doesn't return it), the gift is retroactively void — meaning you owned chametz the entire time.
  • Therefore: no conditions (tanai) of any kind may be attached to the sale or gift of chametz. It must be an outright, unconditional transfer.

Se'if 5 – Even with Bitul, the Penalty Applies

Shulchan Aruch: Even if you did bitul chametz (said Kol Chamira), but chametz was found in your house after Pesach — it is still forbidden (chametz she'avar alav ha-Pesach).

  • Also: Simply declaring it hefker (ownerless) doesn't help either — you didn't physically remove or sell it.
  • The Chachamim's penalty applies regardless of bitul or hefker — only a genuine sale or gift to a non-Jew exempts you.

Se'if 6 – Feeding Chametz to Animals on Pesach

Shulchan Aruch: It is forbidden to feed chametz to any animal on Pesach — even other people's animals, even stray animals.

Why can't you feed a stray animal?

  • The Mishnah Berurah explains: there is hana'ah (benefit/satisfaction) in the act of feeding an animal. Every human has an innate instinct to care for animals — feeding a stray cat or dog gives you a sense of satisfaction. That emotional benefit constitutes hana'ah from chametz.
  • Also: Even hefker chametz that you find on the street — you cannot pick it up to feed animals, because by picking it up, you would acquire it (kinyan).

If a non-Jew asks you to feed his animal on Pesach with chametz — you may not.

Se'if 7 – Pet Food on Pesach

Shulchan Aruch / Poskim: You cannot feed your pet chametz-based food on Pesach.

  • Pet food containing chametz grains belongs to you — you bought it for the dog/cat.
  • It's fitting for animal consumption — so it has the halachic status of chametz.
  • You are violating Bal Yera'eh / Bal Yimatzei — owning chametz on Pesach. This is potentially an isur de'oraita.
  • It doesn't matter that you're not eating it — the prohibition is on owning it.
