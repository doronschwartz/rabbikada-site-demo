Topic: A review of the 13 principles, and how we relate today to someone who does not accept them Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah 3 and Hilchot Mamrim 3; the Hazon Ish; Rav Kook's letters Shiur: The final class of Dayan Daniel Kada's monthly Sunday-night series in Wembley on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Sunday 15 December 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two years in five minutes

The Rabbi opened the last class of a series that had run for two years (with etrog jam for everyone to keep for Tu BiShvat). He began with a summary of all 13 principles: 1. A Creator who is perfect, the cause of all that exists, and involved in the world at every moment. Believing in the wrong God, such as an old man on a throne, is not believing in God at all. 2. Unity: not only one God, but no separate attributes; His kindness and truth are one with His essence. 3. No body: the one principle with no clear source in the Torah or Talmud. A simple reader might think the opposite, which is why the Rambam fought so hard for it. 4. Creation from nothing. 5. Worship God alone. 6. Prophecy: God communicates with human beings. 7. Moshe's unique prophecy, very likely stressed in answer to Christian and Muslim claims. 8. The Torah is from heaven. 9. The Torah does not change. 10. God knows our deeds, and how this fits with free will. 11. Reward and punishment as the natural consequences of our actions. "The reward of a misvah is the misvah." 12. Mashiah: when he comes, we will see what happens. 13. Resurrection of the dead.

The Rambam's hard statement

After the principles, the Rambam writes that whoever believes in all of them belongs to Israel, and we must love him and have compassion on him, even if he sins because of his desires. But whoever denies one of them is called a heretic, an apikoros, and "it is incumbent upon us to hate and destroy him". In the Mishneh Torah such a person is not only excluded from the World to Come. He is also treated as outside the community: he cannot make up a minyan, his shehitah is not kosher, and so on, though by birth he remains a Jew.

Someone asked whether the Rambam really meant it. "He meant his words," the Rabbi answered. "We have to be willing to face the difficult texts." The Shulhan Arukh also records the law in its harshest form. The Rabbi then gave reasons why, although true in principle and grounded in the Talmud, this halacha is not applied today.

1. Who is an apikoros?

Before anything else, you have to define the apikoros. The Talmud (Sanhedrin 99b) says it is someone who disgraces a Torah scholar. The Rambam defines it differently, and the commentators struggle to reconcile them.

On resurrection, the Raavad wrote that the Rambam's words "appear close to one who says there is no resurrection of the body". In effect he thought the Rambam had misunderstood a principle. Yet he still respected him and spent years studying and commenting on his work. Today, said the Rabbi, a rabbi who said publicly he did not believe in bodily resurrection would be dismissed. Before last month's class, he admitted, he might have thought so too. "Before using the apikoros card", make sure there is no opinion the person can rely on.

2. The Hazon Ish: "chains of love"

But some principles, like belief in God, are beyond debate. Here the Rabbi quoted the Hazon Ish, Rabbi Avraham Yeshayahu Karelitz, a giant of the Haredi world and no liberal. He writes that this law applied only when God's providence was revealed and miracles were common. In our time, when God's face is hidden, harshness repairs nothing. "On the contrary, it is incumbent upon us to bring them back with chains of love (avotot ahavah) and to bring them into the light as far as possible."

This led to a lively discussion. The Rabbi admired the Hazon Ish's courage. Someone worried this was the thin end of the wedge. The Rabbi answered that it is not; this approach is only for something as extreme as this law. Otherwise we would end up deleting every halacha we dislike, "and that's what Reform have done".

3. The captured child

The Rambam himself (Hilchot Mamrim 3) writes that this law applies only to one who denies the Torah by his own reasoning. The children and grandchildren of the Karaites, raised in their ways, are like a child captured and raised among non-Jews, a tinok shenishba. They are not to blame, "even if later he hears that he is Jewish and sees Jews and their faith". Instead, "it is appropriate to draw them back to repentance with words of peace".

The Rabbi applied this to anyone raised in a home without observance. They are a product of their upbringing, and the fact that Torah is now on television and online does not change that. So the old law could apply at most to someone who left an observant home himself.

4. An intellectual "anus"

Here the Rabbi offered what he called his own suggestion. The Rambam's Hebrew describes the heretic as one who follows "the lightness of his mind and the capriciousness of his heart". His example was a teenager who announces after a bus ride home that he has decided there is no God. That is the person the Rambam means: someone who has not thought it through.

But what of someone who has studied deeply, honestly, and still concludes otherwise? Objectively he is wrong, said the Rabbi, but he may be under duress of the intellect, an anus. He added a warning: be honest about hidden motives. Aldous Huxley later admitted that he and his friends wanted to be atheists for the freedom it gave them.

5. Rav Kook: the pressure of the age

Rav Kook wrote to a man whose son had left religious life. It is not God's will to reject such children. Just as some say a person who gave in to temptation in a single lapse may still be a valid witness, so "the spirit of our times entices many of our young people". They act against their will, and their transgression should not be judged like a deliberate one. The Rabbi said he had felt this at university himself, where believing in God was looked down on. A young person who sees professors he admires dismiss God is under a kind of emotional duress.

The sixth reason he mentioned only briefly: a person is only treated as a heretic after proper rebuke, and in our times it is very hard to give rebuke properly.

Conclusion: rational discourse

"In 2019 we have a duty, I believe, to bring rational discourse." Someone who only mocks and will not listen is another matter. But people who question because they truly want to understand must never be pushed away. He spoke of his own good fortune: "I had one great teacher who spent hours upon hours with me" discussing such questions.

The principles remain essential, he stressed. We must believe them, think about them and discuss them, as the group had done for two years. But our response to those who struggle with them should not be a knee-jerk rejection: "embrace them and try to understand". He thanked the regulars, especially one who had attended every class, and asked for ideas for a new series in 2020.

Summary

  • The 13 principles cover God, revelation, and the consequences of our deeds; the summary reviewed each in a sentence.
  • The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh treat a true heretic very severely, and the Rabbi did not soften the texts.
  • But it is hard to define a heretic, the Hazon Ish says to bring people back "with chains of love", and those raised without Torah are not to blame.
  • An honest thinker, or a young person overwhelmed by the culture around him, may be under intellectual or emotional duress.
  • Today our duty is patient, rational conversation with those who question.
