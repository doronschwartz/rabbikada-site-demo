Tamar and Shelah

The "black widow" of the title is not the spider. It is the isha katlanit, a woman whose husbands have died, and the question of whether she may remarry.

The topic comes from Parashat Vayeshev. Tamar married Er, who died for his sins, and then Onan, who also died. Yehudah then held back his third son, Shelah, from marrying her. Rashi, quoting the Midrash, explains that she now had a hazakah that her husbands die.

The halacha of the katlanit

Normally a hazakah, an established pattern, needs three occurrences. The Gemara in Yevamot makes exceptions where there is danger. If two brothers died from their brit milah, a third brother is not circumcised. Likewise, a woman whose two husbands have died is a katlanit, and the Shulhan Arukh (Even HaEzer 9) rules that she may not marry a third husband.

Why? The Gemara brings two views:

Reason

One view

Rav Ashi

Still a katlanit

The halacha follows the mazal view, so even two freak accidents make her a katlanit. The Rosh explains the mazal differently: Hazal never speak of a mazal to kill, only a mazal to be poor or rich. Her mazal is to be poor, and in an age when a woman depended on her husband, the husband dies so that this mazal is fulfilled.

Some side questions

  • A man whose two wives died. The rule does not apply to a man. Had Hazal meant it, they would have said so.
  • Yibbum. If the two husbands were brothers and she falls to a third for yibbum, most poskim permit it, since one who does a misvah comes to no harm.
  • Divorce. The Beit Yoseph considered applying it to a woman divorced twice, but the Shulhan Arukh rules that it applies only to death, not divorce.
  • A wealthy widow. The Noda BiYehudah was asked about a successful businesswoman who was financially independent. According to the Rosh's explanation, her mazal to be poor does not apply. He found it hard to rely on alone, but later poskim used it as a consideration to be lenient. Today, with social welfare, a widow does not become destitute, which strengthens the point.

The Rambam's great hiddush

What if a katlanit went ahead and married a third time? The Rosh holds that the beit din tells the couple to divorce, as with a kohen who married a divorcee. The Rambam disagrees, and the Shulhan Arukh rules like him: they need not divorce. The Rambam adds that even if they only made kiddushin, they may go on to complete the marriage.

The reason is in a teshuvah of the Rambam. Asked about a katlanit who had fallen to yibbum, he expressed surprise at the question. One cannot compare a real medical danger, like brit milah in a family where babies died, to what he calls a matter of superstition. In his view the Gemara is giving advice: it is unwise, and psychologically difficult, to marry a woman whose two husbands died. It is not a prohibition that requires divorce.

He even writes that if such a woman wants to marry, she should quietly arrange kiddushin before two witnesses, and then the beit din will help with the rest of the wedding.

The Rabbi noted that the Rambam takes a passage the other Rishonim read as a prohibition and reads it as advice. For this reason, and with today's medical knowledge of why people die, together with the Noda BiYehudah and other considerations, batei din today very rarely prevent a twice-widowed woman from remarrying. Each case still needs to be brought to a beit din.

Back to the parashah

The Ramban asks three questions on Rashi. If Yehudah feared Tamar, why not tell her plainly? Why was he upset when she became pregnant? And surely he knew Er and Onan died for their own sins, not because of her?

According to the Rambam, Rashi's reading cannot stand. The Ramban explains instead that Er and Onan sinned because they married too young and were not mature enough. Yehudah said, "Let my son Shelah grow up." He wanted his third son to mature before marrying. The problem lay with the sons, not with Tamar.

Practical summary

  • A woman widowed twice is called a katlanit, and the Shulhan Arukh rules that she should not marry a third time.
  • It applies even if the husbands died in accidents, but not to a man, and not to a woman divorced twice.
  • If she did remarry, or even only made kiddushin, they are not told to divorce, following the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh.
  • The Rambam treats the rule as advice rather than a strict prohibition, and today batei din very rarely stop such a woman from remarrying; a beit din should be consulted.
