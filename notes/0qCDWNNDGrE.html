Topic: The twelfth principle: the coming of Mashiah Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek, and Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melakhim, chapters 11-12 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's monthly Sunday-night series in Wembley on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, July 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The principle and its sources

The Rabbi introduced the penultimate principle "if Mashiah doesn't come in the next hour". The Rambam writes that we must believe in Mashiah's coming and "not find him slow in coming: though he tarry, wait for him". No date may be fixed and verses may not be used to calculate it. We must honour and love him and pray for him. Mashiah will come only from the house of David, through Shelomoh.

Where is Mashiah in the Torah? In the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Melakhim 11) the Rambam points to two places: - Parashat Nisavim: "God will bring back your captivity... even if your dispersed are at the ends of the heavens." - Bilam's prophecy: "I see him, but not now" refers to King David, and "I behold him, but not soon" to the final king from his descendants.

The Rabbi recalled that Hakham Menasheh ben Israel took the verse in Nisavim so literally that he spent years petitioning Oliver Cromwell to readmit the Jews to England. He believed the Jews had to be scattered to every corner of the earth before the redemption.

In the prophets the theme is everywhere: Yeshayahu, Yehezkel, Amos, Mikhah. The Ani Maamin says "though he tarries, I will wait for him every day", and the Yigdal "He will send our Mashiah at the end of days".

Believing, and waiting

Mashiah gives hope. As Rabbi Sacks points out, other religions believe their saviour has already come; we are still waiting for our golden age. That hope explains why so many false messiahs found followers, above all Shabbetai Sevi, among Jews suffering persecution.

Mashiah means "anointed". In Tanakh the word is used for any king or priest anointed with oil. The Mashiah is the one designated for a special mission.

The Rambam requires more than belief. One must also "await his coming". That is why three blessings in the daily Amidah speak of the redemption. The Rabbi told the story of the Hafes Hayim, a Kohen, who kept a packed suitcase and a special suit ready to greet Mashiah, and who wrote halachic works on the Temple service so as to be ready.

He stressed that we are told to wait for Mashiah, not to bring him, but we must make the world a place he can come to. That is why Hazal are so harsh on those who calculate the date. Whoever waits for a date forgets the process, the work on ourselves, our communities and the world.

He touched on the debate a century ago, when many rabbis opposed founding a state before Mashiah. In his view, experience has settled that debate. Seeing how much the State of Israel contributes to Jewish life worldwide, he said we can see it as the beginning of the redemption. We cannot rebuild the Temple or restore the Sanhedrin ourselves, but we can begin the process.

A personal Mashiah, or a Messianic age?

For the Rambam, Mashiah is clearly a person. Joseph Klausner distinguishes between the Messianic hope, an age of freedom and moral perfection, and the belief in a personal redeemer who brings it.

The Talmud (Sanhedrin) records a startling statement: Rabbi Hillel said "there is no Mashiah for Israel, for they already enjoyed him in the days of Hizkiyahu". Rav Yosef responded, "May God forgive him." The commentators explain it in different ways: - Rashi: Rabbi Hillel did not deny the redemption, only a personal redeemer; God Himself will redeem us. - Another Rishon: he denied a Messianic era, holding that we go straight to the World to Come. - Rabbi Yosef Albo reads him literally, and concludes that belief in Mashiah, though true, is not a fundamental principle. For Albo there are three roots: God, revelation, and reward and punishment.

So can someone today say, "I am in good company with Rabbi Hillel"? The Hatam Sofer says no. Since the sages as a whole rejected the view, it can no longer be held. The Rabbi explained this as a halachic decision applied to belief. Before the matter was decided, Rabbi Hillel was entitled to his view, as Rabbi Eliezer was in the dispute over the oven. The problem only comes when someone persists after the decision.

He also noted that the Rambam's insistence on the line of Shelomoh appears only here. It seems aimed at the Christian claim of descent through David's son Natan. Elsewhere the Rambam, like other Rishonim, speaks simply of the house of David.

How would we know who is Mashiah?

The Rambam (Hilchot Melakhim 11:3-4) says Mashiah does not need to perform miracles or revive the dead. Rabbi Akiva and the sages believed Bar Kozba was the Messianic king, and asked him for no signs, until he was killed.

The Rambam gives two stages: 1. Presumed Mashiah: a king from the house of David who studies Torah, keeps the misvot, brings Israel back to the Torah and fights the wars of God. 2. Certain Mashiah: if he succeeds, builds the Temple and gathers the exiles.

The Rabbi compared this to witnesses in a Beit Din: we accept them on presumption until shown otherwise. If such a person dies before completing the task, he was not Mashiah. "You can't be dead and be Mashiah." The idea of a Mashiah returning from the dead is a Christian one.

The Rambam adds that Christianity and Islam, though they caused great suffering to Jews, have unknowingly spread the ideas of Torah, misvot and Mashiah through the world, preparing the way for the true redemption. "The thoughts of the Creator are beyond human understanding."

What the Messianic age will be

The Rambam is emphatic (Hilchot Melakhim 12). The Messianic age is not the World to Come. "There is no difference between this world and the days of Mashiah except our subjugation to the nations." The world will follow its natural order. "The wolf shall dwell with the lamb" is a metaphor for Israel living securely among nations once likened to wolves and leopards. The prophets longed for that age so that Israel would be free to study Torah and keep the misvot, and so earn the World to Come.

The Rabbi drew out a key point. In the Messianic age there will still be free will. In the World to Come there is none; it is the consequence of what we did here.

On the order of events, Eliyahu, the war of Gog and Magog and the rest, the Rambam says that no one knows until it happens. These details are not fundamentals of faith, and a person should not spend his time on them. The full sequence, including resurrection, was left for the next class.

In the questions, someone asked what we can do to bring the redemption closer. The Rabbi answered that we can certainly help. We can spread the knowledge of God's unity in the world, through the Jewish people keeping the misvot and the nations keeping the Noahide laws. And Mashiah, he added with a smile, will have to be accepted by everyone: Sefaradim, Ashkenazim, Hasidim and all the rest.

Summary

  • The twelfth principle is to believe in Mashiah and to await him, not to calculate when he will come.
  • Hazal opposed calculating the end because waiting for a date makes people forget the work of preparing the world.
  • The Rambam insists on a personal Mashiah; some sources suggest a Messianic age, but the Hatam Sofer rules that Rabbi Hillel's view may no longer be held.
  • Mashiah needs no miracles: a Torah-true king from David's line who succeeds in rebuilding the Temple and gathering the exiles.
  • The Messianic age is still this natural world, with free will, freedom to serve God, and sovereignty restored to Israel.
