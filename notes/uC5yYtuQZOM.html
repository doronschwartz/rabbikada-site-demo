Topic: The names, laws and structure of the Amidah, blessing by blessing, as said on Shabbat Text: The Shabbat Amidah in the Siddur Shiur: The third class of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on prayer at Holland Park, Wednesday 26 June 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The centrepiece

After the history of prayer and the Shema with its blessings of creation, revelation and redemption, the series reached the Amidah. "If you could only dedicate five minutes, this is what you need to pray." It is the heart of the whole service, and Dayan Kada explained it through its names.

Name one: Amidah, standing

The Amidah is the only prayer for which one must stand by halacha. (The other time one must stand is when the Sefer Torah is in motion.) Elsewhere standing is custom. We stand because here we are, as it were, face to face with the Shekhinah.

That is why the Amidah has laws found nowhere else:

  • No interruption at all. During the Shema one may not start a conversation but may return a greeting, such as a late arrival's "Shabbat shalom". In the Amidah, Hazal say, one does not stop even for a king, or for a snake that is not dangerous (danger to life of course overrides everything). Not even a nod or a gesture.
  • Kavanah. The Amidah is the one place where concentration is strictly required. By strict halacha, one who said it with his mind elsewhere should repeat it; in practice the authorities say we do not, since the second time would be no better. The point is how much it matters.

That is why translations matter so much. The Spanish and Portuguese, he noted, printed translations in their siddurim from the start, which was unusual. Someone recalled her father's frustration at praying without understanding. "We're lucky that we live in a generation where we have access to so much," he said, "and we must use it."

What if standing and concentration conflict, for example on a plane or train? The main halacha is to sit and concentrate. He did not recommend the minyanim at the back of planes to Israel: with stewards passing back and forth, praying alone with focus is better.

Name two: Shemoneh Esreh, eighteen

On weekdays the Amidah has eighteen blessings, instituted by the Men of the Great Assembly over two thousand years ago: three of acknowledgement, twelve of request and three of thanksgiving. (He tested the class by saying fifteen, and was duly corrected.) Reasons given for eighteen include the eighteen vertebrae of the spine, since we bow in the Amidah, and the eighteen mentions of Hashem's name in the Shema. Every Amidah of the year, weekday, Shabbat or festival, begins with acknowledgement and ends with thanksgiving.

Name three: Tefillat Lahash, the silent prayer

Who taught us to pray silently? Hannah. Barren and heartbroken, she prayed in the Mishkan with moving lips and no voice, and Eli the Kohen Gadol thought she was drunk. "I am a woman of broken spirit," she answered, "pouring out my soul before Hashem." From her we learn the laws of the silent Amidah: the central prayer of the service is learned from a woman.

Why silently? Because when you are close to someone you do not shout. That is why there must be absolute silence during the Amidah. Talking then is the worst time of all: you lose your own moment with Hashem and you disturb everyone else's.

Before we begin

  • Three steps forward: we approach the King's palace. The three steps back beforehand only developed so that people would not drift forward all service; they have no meaning of their own, and if there is no room, it does not matter.
  • Feet together, like the angels described by the prophet as having "one straight foot". On a plane, none of this is essential.
  • "Hashem, open my lips": an introduction, not part of the Amidah, which is why the hazan does not repeat it.

Why seven on Shabbat?

On Shabbat the Amidah has seven blessings: three, one, three. To ask for something is to want something; Shabbat is about being, not wanting. On Shabbat we do not worry about next week's income; we live in the present. So the twelve requests are replaced by one blessing about the holiness of the day. The festivals, and even Yom Kippur, follow the same pattern.

The seven blessings

  1. The patriarchs. We come before Hashem in the merit of those who came before us. Although it mentions Avraham, Yishak and Yaakov, it ends "Shield of Avraham". We bow at the opening and closing "Barukh Atah": Sefaradim bow quickly at Barukh, lower the head at Atah, and rise slowly, the Gemara says like a snake, at Hashem's name. Ashkenazim bend the knee as well; it is a difference of custom.
  2. The source of life. Hashem sustains the living, heals the sick, frees the captive, and revives the dead. It ends with the resurrection because that is the time when the world will be rectified, which is what we pray for.
  3. Holiness. Kadosh is better translated distinct, set apart. Under the huppah a groom says "mekudeshet li": you are set apart for me. The same root describes a woman set apart for the wrong purposes. Asked whether a Sefaradi man may take a second wife, he explained that by strict Torah law it is not forbidden, but it is against the law in Britain, Israel and America, and many Sefaradi ketubot include an undertaking not to; parents should make sure of it.
  4. The holiness of Shabbat. "Let Moshe rejoice": the tablets bearing the command of Shabbat, a sign between Hashem and Israel, given in love to Israel alone. Its closing request to be made holy by the misvot is a holy request, so it is allowed on Shabbat.
  5. Accept our prayer. We are concluding: accept our prayers, restore the Temple service and return the Shekhinah to Siyon. That is why the kohanim go up for the priestly blessing here, since they served in the Mikdash. Asked about praying for offerings, he said the key is Hashem's presence, of which the offerings are one expression.
  6. Thanksgiving. "We thank You... for Your miracles that are with us every day". That is why Al HaNissim for Hanukkah and Purim is inserted here. We bow at the beginning and end of this blessing too, four bows in all. Women bow in exactly the same way as men, he told a class that was surprised to hear it.
  7. Peace. "There is nothing more important than peace," so the Amidah ends with it.

After the Amidah

"My God, guard my tongue" is a later addition, not part of the Amidah, which is why the hazan does not say it. The Gemara records many personal prayers rabbis added at this point, and this one was chosen; one may add personal requests here, though not material ones on Shabbat. "May the words of my mouth" is said because even if one did not concentrate throughout, one asks that the words be accepted. Then three steps back, bowing first to the left, since we are leaving the King and His right is opposite our left.

Why is the right so important? It is the stronger side and stands for kindness, the left for strict judgment; in kabbalistic thought the right is the giver and the left the receiver. "You can't have right without left," he said: balance is the goal.

Summary

  • The Amidah is the heart of prayer: we stand, may not interrupt, and must concentrate on the words.
  • Its three names teach its laws: standing, eighteen blessings, and silent prayer learned from Hannah.
  • Every Amidah begins with acknowledgement and ends with thanksgiving; on Shabbat the middle is one blessing on the holiness of the day, because Shabbat is about being, not wanting.
  • The seven Shabbat blessings: patriarchs, life, holiness, Shabbat, acceptance, thanks and peace.
