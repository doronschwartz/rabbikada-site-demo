Source: The Rambam's Introduction to Perek Helek: Principle 4 (Kadmut) and Principle 5 (worship of Hashem alone)

Shiur: Part 11 of the series on the Rambam's Introduction to Perek Helek, given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 16 December 2025 (third night of Chanukah; the last shiur of the term)

These notes are based on the transcript of the recording.

Recap

Principles 1–3: a First Cause; one, in the refined sense; no body, no physical attributes or emotions, the only principle with no explicit source in Torah or Chazal. Tonight, 4 and 5, completing the five about Hashem. (Remember: the Rambam wrote this in his twenties; the Moreh came decades later.)

Part 1: Principle 4: Two Texts

The common text

היסוד הרביעי: הקדמות. והוא שנאמין כי זה האחד האמור הוא קדמון בהחלט, וכל נמצא זולתו הוא בלתי קדמון בערכו אליו. והראיות על זה בכתבי הקודש רבות. והיסוד הרביעי הזה מורה עליו מה שנאמר: מעונה אלהי קדם.

He alone is absolutely prior; everything else, however ancient, is not, relative to Him. מענה אלהי קדם: from the Torah's last parashah, "which we say on Simchat Torah."

On this text, Principle 4 is only: Hashem precedes all else.

Rav Shilat's text

The same, then an addition:

ודע כי היסוד היותר גדול שבתורת משה רבנו הוא היות העולם מחודש, המציאו הקב״ה ובראו אחר ההעדר הגמור. וזה שתראני סובב סביב ענין קדמות העולם על דעת הפילוסופים, הוא כדי שיהיה המופת על מציאותו יתעלה מוחלט, כמו שבארתי במורה.

The greatest foundation of Moshe's Torah is that the world is newly made: Hashem brought it into being after complete nothingness. When I seem to entertain the philosophers' eternity of the world, it's only to prove His existence absolutely, as I explained in the Moreh.

"As I explained in the Moreh": written decades later. Rav Shilat says the Rambam added it when revising; the Oxford manuscripts show his marginal additions to the Commentary.

The difference: "Hashem precedes everything" vs "Hashem created from nothing." "You may think it's the same. It's not so straightforward."

Sympathetic (below)

Aristotle "was very much the scientific view until the Big Bang theory. We now know he was completely wrong, and the Rambam called him out in the 12th century." (The Moreh lists eight proofs of Aristotle's; "we don't need to go through them.")

Rishonim who held like Plato

Ibn Ezra on בראשית ברא אלהים: most commentators say ברא = something from nothing (e.g. Korach: ואם בריאה יברא ה׳). But ויברא… את האדם, from earth (adamah)! So ברא means לגזור ולשום גבול נגזר, והמשכיל יבין: to shape, to set boundaries. Hashem formed the world from pre-existing matter.

The Ralbag held the same.

The Kuzari: himself ex nihilo, but ואם יצטרך בעל תורה להאמין ולהודות בהיולי קדמון ועולמות רבים קודם העולם הזה, לא היה בזה פגם באמונתו, כי העולם הזה חדש מזמן ידוע מתחלת האדם. If a believer feels he must accept eternal matter, even prior worlds, his faith is unblemished: what matters is that this world began.

The Ramban (opening of Bereishit): yesh me'ayin is a principle of faith, possible for an omnipotent God; deny it and you're a heretic. The mainstream of the Rishonim.

What did the Rambam hold?

Moreh 2:25:

- Aristotle: if his proofs were compelling, I'd reinterpret the Torah. They aren't. And his view uproots miracles, and so the whole Torah.

- Plato: דעת זו אינה הורסת יסודי התורה: this view doesn't destroy the Torah's foundations. It allows miracles; the verses can be read according to it; some even support it better. But nothing compels us, so we read the verses simply: creation from nothing.

"The Rambam held ex nihilo, like the Ramban and most Rishonim, no question. But I read him like the Kuzari: Plato's view is compatible with the Torah. Beseder, you may."

So which text?

Common text: Principle 4 = Hashem precedes everything, which all agree is fundamental. Ibn Ezra is within the faith.

Rav Shilat's text: ex nihilo is part of the principle, so Ibn Ezra would be a kofer.

"I find Rav Shilat's text difficult": the greatest foundation of Moshe's Torah is creation from nothing, when in the Moreh he's sympathetic to Plato?

Hilchot Teshuvah 3 ("Rav Shilat doesn't reference this"): the five kinds of min: no God; two gods; a body; והאומר שאינו לבדו ראשון וצור לכל: "not alone First and Rock of all": just first, matching the common text. "Interesting that he doesn't emphasise ex nihilo here."

What matters most

There is a beginning: now accepted by science; the world isn't eternal as Aristotle thought. Hashem precedes everything. Possibly also ex nihilo: certainly the correct and majority view, and the Rambam's own.

Why the Torah begins with Bereishit. Talmai (Ptolemy) had 72 Sages translate the Torah separately, and miraculously they all made the same changes in several places, lest it be misread. One: they reordered בראשית ברא אלהים to אלהים ברא בראשית*, lest readers think "Bereishit" is a god who created Elohim. (The Gemara: darkness descended that day; "so many misconceptions come from not reading the original*.")

"So why does the Torah open with Bereishit? Because there is a beginning, and so things can change. Believe like Aristotle and nothing changes: no miracles, no control. A beginning means Hashem can change the world, and so can we: the world, and ourselves."

Ani Ma'amin: שהבורא… הוא ראשון והוא אחרון. "Last isn't part of this principle" (eternity belongs to Principle 1). Yigdal: קדמון לכל דבר אשר נברא, ראשון ואין ראשית לראשיתו: nothing precedes His being first. Neither addresses pre-existent matter.

Robert: if there's a beginning, must there be an end? Or does matter continue forever?

"I need to get back to you on that. My understanding is it's possible the world is eternal, but I need to check the Moreh. I don't think the two are necessarily dependent."

Part 2: Principle 5

היסוד החמישי: שהוא יתברך הוא הראוי לעבדו ולגדלו ולהודיע גדולתו ולעשות מצותיו, ושלא יעשו כזה למי שהוא תחתיו במציאות, מן המלאכים והכוכבים והגלגלים והיסודות ומה שהורכב מהם, לפי שכולם מוטבעים בפעולותיהם, אין להם משפט ולא בחירה אלא לו לבדו יתברך. וכן אין ראוי לעבדם כדי שיהיו אמצעים לקרבם אליו, אלא אליו לבדו יכוונו המחשבות ויניחו כל מה שזולתו. וזה היסוד החמישי הוא שמזהיר מעבודה זרה, ורוב התורה מזהרת עליו.

#

1

Serve Hashem: worship, magnify, make His greatness known, keep His mitzvot

2

Serve nothing below Him: angels, stars, spheres (which the Rambam, and Rav Saadia, held have awareness), the elements, or what's made from them (idols). They act by nature, without judgement or choice

3

"Most of the Torah warns against it": from the Golden Calf through the prophets and Baal.

Intermediaries (perhaps implied)

Yigdal: הנו אדון עולם לכל נוצר, יורה גדלתו ומלכותו

He's Master of all creation

"The vaguest stanza of Yigdal. Where does it say don't serve anyone else? You have to add: He's Master of all, therefore serve Him only"

Element 1: why we serve

"Hashem doesn't need our service. We don't pray to change His mind. We serve because it's best for us: a perfect God needs no worship."

Element 3: the Golden Calf

The Ramban: after Sinai they believed: they'd seen and heard Hashem. They wanted something tangible.

Why Moshe broke the Tablets ("I read this somewhere; I can't remember where"): not anger. He saw they needed something physical: give them the Tablets and they'll worship the Tablets.

"This replays in every generation: always something new to connect to Hashem through. Because He's so hard to relate to, we want something tangible: strings, blue things on our bodies, everyone has their own. All distractions from focusing on God directly, and distractions are always alluring."

Graves

"It's incredible how widespread this has become in the frum world. Every few years, a new grave where you'll see a miracle. The newest, in the last ten years: Reb Shayele of Kerestir. Hardly anyone had heard of him; now ordinary people fly to Romania: go to him and you'll have **parnassah. He wrote no sefarim; we have none of his. He was a great rebbe who fed the poor, so if you go, you'll have food."

Is visiting graves permitted?

Yes, there's a source. Calev went to pray at the Avot in Me'arat HaMachpelah on the spies' mission. A tzaddik's resting place has significance, and prayer may be more accepted there.

But nothing is intrinsic. It inspires us to connect to Hashem: praying by the Avot, he thought of Avraham, Yitzchak and Yaakov, and was moved to act like them. "Perhaps in that person's merit your prayer is more accepted. Perhaps."

"But Meron and such places, if you've been you know: people pushing each other out of the way to get one metre closer, to touch the tziyun, as if prayer works better nearer. For me, that's avodah zarah. A violation of this principle."

Enosh's mistake

Hilchot Avodah Zarah 1: in Enosh's day people reasoned: Hashem made the stars and spheres to run the world and honoured them, so He wants us to honour them: as you'd show respect to the guards at Buckingham Palace out of honour for the king. They built shrines, offered sacrifices: avodat kochavim.

Ch. 2: עיקר הצווי בעבודה זרה שלא לעבוד אחד מכל הברואים, לא מלאך ולא גלגל ולא כוכב… ואף על פי שהעובד יודע שה׳ הוא האלהים והוא עובד הנברא הזה על דרך שעבד אנוש.

Even knowing Hashem is God, serving a creature as Enosh did, as an intermediary, is forbidden.

Summary

  1. Principle 4: Hashem is absolutely prior to everything. Rav Shilat's text (a later addition referencing the Moreh) makes ex nihilo "the greatest foundation."
  2. Three views: ex nihilo (Torat Moshe); Aristotle's eternal universe (rejected: no miracles; disproved by the Big Bang); Plato's eternal matter (Ibn Ezra, Ralbag; the Kuzari tolerates it; the Ramban calls its holders heretics).
  3. The Rambam held ex nihilo but found Plato compatible with the Torah. On the common text (and Hilchot Teshuvah's "First"), Ibn Ezra stays within the faith.
  4. Bereishit: there's a beginning, so change is possible, for Hashem and for us (and Talmai's 72 Sages).
  5. Principle 5: serve Hashem alone, for our sake; nothing beneath Him, not even as an intermediary (Enosh, the Golden Calf, the broken Tablets).
  6. Graves inspire; nothing is intrinsic. Pushing to touch the tziyun is avodah zarah.
