Where we are

The Rabbi finished Siman 2 with Seifim 4 and 5. Seifim 6-10 were done a few weeks earlier. He said there are three sugyot in these two seifim:

  1. When can we determine that someone is a pasul: the case of one who is called an insulting name and stays silent (Seif 4).
  2. Almanat Isa (Seif 5).
  3. Mishpahah shenitmaah, the family that has already been absorbed (the Rema in Seif 5).

Seif 4: you called him a mamzer and he stayed silent

The Gemara in Ketubot. A man calls another a mamzer, or a netin, or a halal, or a slave, and the other stays silent. Is he now presumed to be a pasul? There are three views:

View

Rule

Tanna Kama

Mamzer, netin and eved are pasul if he keeps quiet. A halal is not, since it is a lesser issue and he would not bother to object.

Rabbi Meir

Appears to repeat Tanna Kama

A third view, in the name of Rabbi Meir

The opposite: silence to "mamzer" proves nothing, since everyone knows who the mamzerim are. Silence to "halal" does make him pasul.

The Gemara answers that any time you call someone a pasul and he stays silent, the disqualification "sticks". All the Rishonim rule like the Tanna Kama, as the Shulhan Arukh does. The Rabbi called this "pretty harsh", so the Rishonim try to limit it, in four ways.

Four limitations in the Rishonim

  1. The Rambam: a bedikah helps. The silence creates a hashash (a concern), not certainty. If we check and find nothing, he is fine. The Shulhan Arukh quotes this.
  2. The Raavad: only in those generations. In earlier generations a person who was insulted could go to Beit Din for malkut or damages, so it made sense to object. Nowadays shouting back only starts a fight, so keeping quiet is a sign of restraint. The exception is someone who comes to you with no quarrel and says "I heard you are a mamzer". Even the Raavad would expect a reaction then. The Shulhan Arukh brings this as "some say".
  3. The Ramban, first approach: only a family already under concern. This applies only where there is already a concern about the family (almanat isa, a mixture). If a random person is called a mamzer and stays silent, it means nothing. The Rema brings this.
  4. The Ramban, second approach: only against a background of fighting back. The silence counts only when it contrasts with another time the person was insulted and did fight back. A one-off silence means nothing.

The Rabbi said these four limits do not seem to be in dispute with one another.

The result today. Nowadays there is nothing to gain by arguing back, there may be no other time the person fought back, and there may be no concern about his family. So when people throw around labels like mamzer, it is not to be taken literally. If a friend says "I think you are a mamzer, based on my research" and the other just walks away, the Rambam requires a bedikah, the Raavad might see a problem, and the Ramban might not. The halakhah is "extremely limited" today.

A question on the Ramban's first approach. If there is already a concern about the family, a bedikah is needed anyway (Seif 3). So why does the insult add anything? The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel both ask this.

  • The Helkat Mehokek, first answer. Seif 3 is about a family where there is an irur, meaning two witnesses. Seif 4 is about a family where there is only a kol (a rumour), not witnesses. A rumour alone is not enough, but the rumour together with the silence needs a further bedikah to ensure that he is kosher.
  • The Helkat Mehokek, second answer. A lenient one: when I insult one member of such a family and he stays silent, he is the problem, and the rest of the family becomes kosher. The Beit Shemuel rejects this, since the Shulhan Arukh's language implies a concern for the family too. He and the Bah accept the first answer.

One more point in the Rema. All of this applies only when the insult is directed at the person himself. If someone says "your son is a mamzer" and the father stays silent, none of the above applies. Likewise if someone insults him in any way other than lineage, such as "you are a horrible person", silence does not show agreement.

Questions from the class

  • Does it apply to someone called a Mitsri (an Egyptian convert's descendant)? It is not discussed in Seif 4, but the Rabbi saw no reason to distinguish. He added an argument for leniency: someone called a Mitsri who stays silent may simply be saying "so what, I am a completely kosher Jew now", so the insult is not inherent. One would need to see the Gemara and Rishonim on why the Gemara chose its examples.

Seif 5, part one: Almanat Isa

The name means "the widow of Isa". It refers to a woman who is a widow of a man from a family where there is a doubt of halalut.

The Gemara.

  • Ketubot. A woman was seen having relations with a man who has since left the country. She says he was fully kosher. The mishnah says she is not believed, so we assume he was a mamzer until she proves otherwise. This matters because a woman who had a relationship forbidden to her by the Torah becomes a zonah for a Kohen. (Zonah here does not mean a prostitute, but a woman who had a forbidden relationship. It comes up in Siman 6.) Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel disagrees and believes her, since she knows he was kosher.
  • Eduyot. The same Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel does not believe a woman who says she was married to a kosher man in the case of almanat isa. The Gemara answers that in Ketubot she is certain, while in the almanat isa case there is a doubt about the family. Rabbi Yehoshua holds the opposite way: he believes her in almanat isa, since there are two doubts (safek safeika), but not in Ketubot, where there is only one.

What is the double doubt? Rashi explains it in a way the Rabbi called really one big doubt, and Tosafot and the Rambam reject it. We rule like Tosafot and the Rambam: in a family, somewhere, someone is a safek halal (his father married a woman who may or may not have been divorced). A woman married someone from that family, and now she is his widow. That is a safek safeika: it is not certain she is the widow of the halal, and even if she is, he is only a safek halal.

The Shulhan Arukh's ruling. We follow Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. In the first instance she should not marry a Kohen, but if she did, she need not divorce, because of the double doubt. The Rabbi noted that the Rambam says this ruling applies only lekhathilah. If it is a definite halal (vaday halal) in the family, then any woman widowed from that family may not marry a Kohen without a bedikah, and if she did marry, she must leave.

The Shulhan Arukh's own addition. He says that the same applies to a safek mamzer mixed into a family. The Beit Shemuel asks where this comes from, since the whole din was learned only about Kehunah, and a safek mamzer is a classic double doubt. He rejects it, and that seems to be the consensus of the Aharonim. Mamzerut is the subject of Siman 4.

The Beit Yoseph's hidush, quoted by the Rema. The ruling that she may remain married because of the double doubt applies to the almanah herself, but not to her daughter. The daughter was born into the double doubt, and never had a presumption of being kosher. The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel both disagree. The Beit Yoseph's proof from Tosafot only fits Rabbi Yehoshua's opinion, so it does not hold, and the Aharonim do not rule like him.

Seif 5, part two: Mishpahah shenitmaah

The Gemara in Kiddushin: "It is in our hands to reveal the pesulim (invalid families), but what can I do, since the great ones of the generation are mixed in?" Once a family has mixed into the community, it has mixed in. The Gemara adds that Eliyahu will in the future remove those brought in by force or money, and bring back those who were pushed away. It also says the rabbis who knew about it would tell their children and students once every seven years, and some say twice.

What does "it is in our hands" mean? It could mean that we could do a bedikah and find out. But "to reveal" can also mean to publicize: I know this person is a pasul, everyone thinks he is kosher, and I could publicize it but will not. This is how the Ran understands it. In that case, where I know personally that a person is a mamzer and nobody else knows, I keep quiet. People marry him, and no one thinks they are doing an isa. But I may tell my children and students to stay away, once every seven years, so that it is remembered.

The Rema. All of this applies only where the matter is known to the person (lemi sheyodea badavar), and the family has mixed in with the pesul. In that case one tells one's own children to stay away, so that they keep a kosher yihus. The Beit Shemuel rules like this. The Rabbi explained the Rema's point about numbers: if more than two people know, it is considered public knowledge and this halakhah no longer applies. But if it is just me, or me and my colleague on the Beit Din, I should not reveal it, though I may tell my children and my successor.

The Rabbi called this "a huge hidush" for rabbis today. As a hypothetical example, a rabbi doing archive research finds that a woman's great-grandmother received her get only after remarrying, so her great-grandchild now in the community is a mamzer, and is about to marry in a few weeks. According to what we learned, it is absolutely fine to keep quiet. Do not speak about it. If your own child comes to marry that girl, tell your child not to marry, and you may tell him why. But you do not need to tell anyone else. He and his family are completely part of the community.

Practical summary

  • Silence in response to being called a mamzer, netin or halal can make a person a pasul, according to the Gemara and all the Rishonim, but there are four limits and the halakhah is extremely narrow today.
  • A bedikah removes the concern, according to the Rambam.
  • Nowadays, silence in an argument is understood as restraint and not as admission, according to the Raavad.
  • A woman widowed from a family with a safek halal should not marry a Kohen in the first instance, but need not divorce if she does. If the halal is definite, she must leave.
  • Where a family has mixed into the community and the problem is not widely known, the halakhah is to keep quiet and not reveal it, and to tell only one's own children.
