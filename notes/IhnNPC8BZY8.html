Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 301, seifim 43-51 (end of the siman); 302, seif 1 begun Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 11 July 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seifim 43-44: A Sefer Torah in the rain, and a fire

Seif 43: if you find a Sefer Torah in a field on Shabbat in a time of persecution, you leave it. Otherwise you stay with it until Shabbat ends. If it is raining and the letters will fade, you may wrap yourself in it like a garment, put your clothes on top, and walk home. The Rabbi admitted he cannot picture the practicalities, but the point is that protecting it is its honour. He added that if a non-Jew is available, it would be better to ask him to carry it, since in streets that are a karmelit that is a rabbinic prohibition, and one may ask a non-Jew to do that for a misvah.

Seif 44: if there is a fire and you cannot carry your clothes out, you may wear as many as you can, go to a friend's house, take them off, and come back for more. Since it is all wearing, it is permitted.

Seifim 45-47: Wet clothes

Earlier in the siman we learned not to walk through water, since wet clothes may lead to wringing them out. But if your clothes got wet, from rain or a fall, you may keep walking in them. The Mishnah Berurah: if these are your only Shabbat clothes and they got wet earlier, you may even put them back on to go out again; if you have other clothes, wear those.

Spreading out to dry. At home you may not spread wet clothes out to dry, because people will suspect you washed them on Shabbat. The Gemara says this applies even in the most private room: when the rabbis forbade something because of onlookers, they forbade it everywhere. (Some Rishonim did not record this, and Tosafot and others limit it to cases where the suspected act is a Torah prohibition, as washing is.) Clothes hung out before Shabbat may stay.

  • Dry clothes: the Ben Ish Hai writes that even dry clothes should not be spread out. Rav Ovadia Yosef strongly disagrees: the concern is only with something wet.
  • Hanging is not spreading. Someone asked whether he could hang his wet coat on a hanger in a warm laundry room. Yes: spreading out means laying it out fully, as on a washing line or a banister. Simply hanging it up is fine.

Seif 46: near the heat. One may not dry wet clothes near a fire. There are two issues: heating the water in them (cooking, here rabbinic), and laundering. Mishnah Berurah 169-170: heat brightens the garment, which is a form of laundering, and "unfortunately people are not careful about this" in winter. This applies only if the water in the garment can reach yad soledet bo, which we take as 45 degrees Celsius.

A lively discussion followed about whether radiators and heated towel rails get that hot; someone looked up that radiator water is typically 60 to 82 degrees. The Rabbi's guidance: - A warm radiator you can comfortably touch: fine to dry things on it. - A very hot radiator, towel rail or boiler that singes your hand after a couple of seconds: putting wet gloves or a wet coat on it may be a Torah prohibition, possibly two. This includes hanging a soaked coat on a hot radiator in shul.

The Rema (Rabbenu Yeruham): do not go somewhere you are likely to fall in water in your clothes. Swimming trunks are made to get wet, so they are not a problem. Swimming in the sea is forbidden; swimming in an indoor pool is strictly permitted when needed, though there are several details to be careful of.

Seif 47: wet clothes are Mukseh. Clothes you would not want wet become Mukseh mehamat issur, Mukseh because of a possible prohibition (like the flame of a candle). While wearing them, or while holding them after taking them off, you may carry them until you put them down. Shaar HaSiyun: once you put them down, they are Mukseh until they dry. Since this Mukseh arose during Shabbat, once they are dry they may be used again. A towel is meant to get damp and is not Mukseh, but a towel that becomes soaked, like the hand towel at a busy kiddush, does become Mukseh.

Seifim 48-51: Towels, hands from the sea, wigs

Seif 48: you may dry yourself with a towel and carry it, and do not worry that you will wring it out. The rabbis would not let you hand your wet clothes to the bathhouse keepers, whom they did not trust. The Rabbi explained that the bathhouse keepers would heat water on Shabbat and claim it was from before, which is why bathing the whole body in hot water on Shabbat was forbidden even if heated beforehand.

Seif 49: you may wash your hands in the sea, but before walking four amot you must let the water drip off, or you are carrying it. Taking your hands out is no problem, since the shore is almost never a Reshut HaRabbim. Water absorbed in a beard or hair after the mikveh is batel; only if it is actually dripping is it like the hands.

Seif 50: one may go out in a wig, provided it was dyed, worn before, or tied on, showing it is a wig. Today's wigs, for men and women, are a takhshit. Seif 51: a scarf around the neck, or a bandage on the hand, is permitted.

Review

The Rabbi tested the class: - Jewellery without an eruv? An adornment is permitted from the Torah; the rabbinic worry that she will take it off to show it is set aside because our streets are a karmelit, not a Reshut HaRabbim. He added that one concern of those who do not use the local eruv is that a road like the North Circular may be a true Reshut HaRabbim. To be consistent, if they still allow jewellery there, they must be strict about the Torah prohibition of carrying while relying on the lenient view of Reshut HaRabbim for the rabbinic decree. - A blind person's stick? Not permitted, since he can physically walk without it. - Gloves? Clothing, and permitted; better tied on without an eruv.

Siman 302:1: Shaking out a garment

Laundering (melaben) has three parts, soaking, rubbing and wringing, and each is a Torah prohibition. The Gemara says one who shakes out a garment on Shabbat is liable. Rashi: shaking off dust, which lodges in the fabric. The Rif and the Rambam: only a new black garment shaken free of dew, which soaks in and which the owner is particular about.

The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rif and the Rambam, so shaking dust off your clothes is permitted. The Rema extends the dew case to rain on a garment one is particular about, and quotes Rashi's view on dust, saying it is good to be strict; Mishnah Berurah 6 says one must be, and advises keeping clothes where they will not get dusty. Following the Hida's principle, where the Rishonim disagree, an Ashkenazi may ask a non-Jew to shake it. Everyone agrees surface dirt may be removed: feathers, droplets on a plastic coat, or the top layer of hummus scraped off a jacket with a knife.

Can a Sefaradi shake an Ashkenazi's garment? The Sis Eliezer discusses the similar case with reheating soup in a yeshivah; the Rabbi said he would post the teshuvah.

Practical summary

  • You may keep walking in clothes that got wet; at home hang them up rather than spreading them out.
  • Do not put wet clothes or gloves on a very hot radiator or near a fire; a mildly warm radiator is fine.
  • Once you put wet clothes down, do not move them until they are dry.
  • Let water drip off your hands before walking after washing them outdoors.
  • Sefaradim may shake dust off a garment; Ashkenazim should not, but may ask a non-Jew.
