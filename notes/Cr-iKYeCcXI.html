Why the Greeks are called darkness

The Rabbi opened with last week's parashah, Miketz. The Zera Shimshon says that Miketz should always be linked to Hanukkah, and the Rabbi tied it to the coming fast of the Tenth of Tevet. He invited questions throughout.

The Midrash reads the "darkness" at the start of Bereshit as a hint to Yavan, Greece. The Greeks gave the world democracy, the gym, the theatre, concerts and architecture, so why call them darkness? The Rabbi built the answer in stages, going back through the Torah.

Beauty in Egypt and in Yefet

First, Yoseph and the wife of Potiphar. Egypt is called a beautiful calf, and Yoseph is described as extremely handsome. When she grabs him, Yoseph leaves his garment in her hand and runs. The Rabbi read this as Yoseph saying: you want my outer garment, you can have it, but I am far more than my appearance.

Second, Noah's sons. When Noah was exposed, Shem and Yefet took a garment to cover him. The verse says "he took" in the singular, and Rashi explains that Shem put more effort into the misvah. Yefet is the ancestor of the Greeks, and the word yefet means beauty. Shem's reward was tsitsit for his descendants, while Yefet's was that his descendants would have burial.

Two kinds of light

Third, the light of the first day. The sun was only made on the fourth day, so what was the first light? The Rabbi preferred the word insight to "spiritual light": the sun and electric light let us see the surface of things, but the first light lets you see inside things and beyond what meets the eye. Hazal call it the hidden light, and it was hidden away after Adam sinned.

His example was Adam naming the animals. In most languages a word is only a convention: "table" or "cup" just labels an object. In Lashon HaKodesh a word is the essence of the thing, which is why a thing is called a davar. The Rabbi said, "Don't ask me how," but Adam needed the first light to see each animal's essence and name it truly.

Real and false beauty

Is physical beauty good in the Torah? Sarah, Rahel and Yoseph are all described as beautiful, yet on Friday night we sing "sheker hahen vehevel hayofi," that charm is false and beauty is vain. The Rabbi's answer: beauty is true when the outer face (panim) reflects the inner face, a person with fear of Heaven. Beauty alone is a lie, because it promises something that is not there. He quoted the verse about a golden ring in a pig's snout: not only does the pig look absurd, the expensive ring loses its beauty too.

Now Shem and Yefet make sense. Yefet saw a beautiful body that needed respect and covered it. Shem saw a body as a vehicle for the neshamah, and understood that this is why it must be buried. Yefet received burial, a reward about the body. Shem received tsitsit, whose blue reminds us of the heavens: we see something physical and connect it to something beyond. Egypt's beauty and the Greek beauty are the same: all outside. Hidur misvah, beautifying a misvah, is learned from "zeh Keli ve'anvehu" at the Sea, where beauty is used to express the spiritual.

Hanukkah and the Tenth of Tevet

The basic obligation is one light per household each night. Lighting more is the hidur described in the Gemara. The Rabbi noticed that people who are not mehadrin in everything else become mehadrin at Hanukkah, because the essence of Hanukkah is looking at the flame and seeing beyond it. Adding one light a night gives 36 lights in all, matching the 36 hours (a Friday of 12 hours plus a full Shabbat of 24) in which Adam had the hidden light. The flame is the light of the fourth day, and Hanukkah asks us to look past it.

The Tenth of Tevet is linked to this. The Gemara says that on this date the Torah was first translated into Greek. The problem with translation is that it gives only the surface and loses the depth of the Hebrew. Translations are a blessing today, but they are not the real thing. The Rabbi added that the siege of Yerushalayim began on this date, and Yerushalayim is Tsion, whose letters are those of Yavan with a saddik on top: beauty with something deeper inside. The lesson of the whole section: do not go through life taking things at face value.

Miketz: how to treat dreams

The Zera Shimshon asks why the Torah says Par'oh woke between the dreams, and why only after the second does it say "behold, a dream." He quotes the Gemara in Berakhot with three signs of a dream with real content: it recurs, it comes in the morning, and it concerns someone else. Par'oh's dreams had the first two, and only Yoseph's interpretation, about the whole Egyptian economy, fulfilled the third.

The Rabbi explained that in sleep the neshamah receives information from sources that are reliable and sources that are not, and morning is more reliable than night. His practical advice: generally do not take dreams too seriously. If a bad dream bothers you, interpret it positively yourself. If you have a recurring dream, it deserves more attention.

Questions from the class: - Do rabbis today interpret dreams? Many people claim to. The best interpreter of your dream is yourself, and you should always interpret it positively. - Was Yoseph always a master of dreams, or was he prepared in prison? It seems he was from a young age, when he told his dreams to his brothers at seventeen. Dreams in Bereshit are taken very seriously, and a dream can be predictive only if it comes from the right source, which no one can know for certain.

Vayigash: why Yaakov said the Shema

When Yaakov and Yoseph meet, Yoseph falls on his neck and weeps, while Yaakov seems passive. Hazal say Yaakov was reciting the Shema, when one may not even gesture. The Zera Shimshon asks: if it was the time of Shema, Yoseph should not have been hugging him, and if it was not, why was Yaakov reciting it? His answer: the ayin of Shema is written large, standing for seventy, and Yaakov came down to Egypt with sixty-nine descendants. Only on seeing Yoseph did the number reach seventy, so he declared "Shema Yisrael."

The Rabbi then drew a lesson. We say the Shema, with the mezuzah and tefillin, to remember the oneness of Hashem. This is not for protection: the Rambam says we should not use misvot for personal benefit. If Hashem is one, both the good and the difficult come from Him. There are three levels: saying it, believing it, and living it. Yoseph lived it, because he came to see that everything he had suffered led to his becoming ruler of Egypt. Yaakov believed it but had not yet experienced it, until he saw Yoseph. That is why we cover our eyes when saying the first verse: we proclaim the unity of God while it is hidden from us.

His challenge: there is no halakhah to kiss the mezuzah. When leaving the house, look at it and think about what it contains, the unity of God.

Summary

  • Greece is called darkness because its great achievements stop at the surface; the first light of Creation sees inside things.
  • Beauty is true when the outside reflects the inside; otherwise it is a lie.
  • Hanukkah's added lights, 36 in all, point to the hidden light, and the Tenth of Tevet marks the loss of depth in translation.
  • Dreams deserve attention when recurring, in the morning or about others; otherwise interpret them positively and move on.
  • Saying "Hashem Echad" is not enough: aim to believe it and to live it, including in hard times.
