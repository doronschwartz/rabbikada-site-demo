These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The shortest principle

"We began a year and a half ago with the first principle, the belief in God. And now we are coming to the end." The Rabbi called the resurrection of the dead perhaps the most controversial of the 13 principles, "simply because it seems the Rambam had perhaps different views to what he actually wrote".

After pages on the other principles, the Rambam's text on the thirteenth says only: "The resurrection of the dead: we have already explained it." Earlier he had written just a few lines: it is one of the foundations of the Torah of Moshe, there is no religion for one who denies it, and it is for the righteous. The Ani Maamin says the dead will live "when it is the Creator's will", and the Yigdal: "God will revive the dead in His great kindness."

The Torah itself only hints at it: "I put to death and I bring to life" (Haazinu), in that order. The prophets are clearer. "Your dead shall live... awake and sing, you who dwell in the dust" (Yeshayahu), which we say at a stone setting. Daniel is the most explicit: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake."

Why was the Rambam so brief?

Even in his lifetime, people noticed how little the Rambam says about resurrection: - The Moreh Nevuchim never discusses it. - The Mishneh Torah only lists those who deny it among those who lose the World to Come. - Yet in the same work he describes the World to Come at length: "there is no body or physical form, only the souls of the righteous... like the ministering angels". The Talmud's "the righteous sit with their crowns on their heads" he explains as the knowledge they acquired.

The Raavad attacked him: "the words of this man appear to me close to one who says that resurrection is for the soul alone." The Talmud speaks of the righteous rising in their clothes (Ketubot 111b) and of the blemished being healed. That only makes sense with a body. Others concluded that the Rambam treated resurrection as a metaphor for the immortality of the soul.

The Essay on Resurrection

In 1189 the Rambam heard that a scholar in Syria was publicly denying resurrection on the basis of his writings. He was not too worried. Then a letter came from Yemen: ordinary people there were saying the same. So he wrote the Maamar Tehiyat HaMetim. Even a clear text can be misread, he says. The Christians read "Hashem Elokeinu Hashem Ehad" as the Trinity.

There he states plainly that he believes in a physical resurrection, souls returning to bodies. Then he adds something astonishing: the resurrected "will eat, drink, marry, have children, and then die". Only afterwards comes the eternal, purely spiritual World to Come. This answers the Raavad: the Talmud's clothes and healed bodies belong to that temporary stage.

Why so brief elsewhere? His style is brief, he says, and resurrection is a belief that cannot be proved philosophically. Why bring people back to life only to die again? "We cannot know." The Rabbi admitted that this, for him, makes little sense: why take someone out of spiritual bliss to put them back in a body?

Some academics, such as Marc Shapiro, say the Rambam's essay on resurrection was written for the masses. The Rabbi called that harsh to say of the Rambam, "a seeker of truth", but showed two proofs that the Rambam did equate resurrection with the immortality of the soul.

Two proofs

The list in Hilchot Teshuvah. The Rambam lists 24 kinds of people who lose their share in the World to Come. Twelve of them match the 13 principles. Only reward and punishment is missing. If resurrection means the eternal life of the soul, the gap is explained: denying resurrection is denying reward and punishment.

The blessing at the cemetery. Rabbi Shem Tov Gaguine, a past Av Beit Din of the community, notes in Keter Shem Tov that the Talmud's blessing on seeing Jewish graves ends "and He will raise you up in judgment". The Rambam's version adds three words: "to the life of the World to Come". Rabbi Gaguine adds only "the wise will understand". The Rabbi suggested that for the Rambam, resurrection and the World to Come are the same thing.

Hardly anyone followed this reading in the Middle Ages, the Akedat Yishak being one. In the 20th century Chief Rabbi Joseph Hertz wrote in his commentary on the siddur that the Rambam "makes the doctrine of the resurrection of the dead identical with that of the immortality of the soul".

His conclusion on the Rambam: one thing is certain, physical resurrection was not a major part of his philosophy, and a permanent bodily life is ruled out. Either he meant a short physical revival followed by death, or resurrection means the immortality of the soul.

The Ramban and the Ramhal

The Ramban disagrees. The Talmud must be taken literally: we will rise in our bodies, but refined bodies, without the need to eat, drink or the other appetites. The Ramhal, Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto, adds that this is only fair. Body and soul served God together for 120 years, so "it is not right that the body should labour but not reap the fruits".

The Rabbi laid out the stages. We live; we die and the soul waits; Mashiah comes; the dead rise. For the Ramban and the Ramhal, that refined bodily life lasts forever. For the Rambam there is a fifth stage: death again, and then eternity for the soul alone. Rabbi Yoseph Albo brings both views and says we will see who is right when it happens.

How does it happen? Rabbi Saadiah Gaon says God will reassemble the actual remains. Rabbi Hasdai Crescas says God will form a body anew. The Rabbi noted this answers the argument that cremation denies resurrection, though he stressed: "of course cremation is strictly forbidden". And those who perished in the Holocaust will certainly rise, if there is a physical resurrection.

Questions

Someone asked about reincarnation, gilgul. The Rabbi noted that the Rambam never mentions it. Those who believe in it speak of a soul returning during this world. Resurrection comes only after Mashiah, in a renewed world. Asked about non-Jews, he said they have a share in the World to Come; if that is resurrection, the question is answered.

He announced the final class, "Conclusions: who really is a heretic", for Sunday 15 December.

Summary

  • The Rambam says very little about resurrection, though he calls it a foundation of the Torah.
  • In his Essay on Resurrection he affirms a physical resurrection, followed by death and an eternal life of the soul.
  • His list in Hilchot Teshuvah and his wording of the cemetery blessing suggest that he identified resurrection with the immortality of the soul.
  • The Ramban and the Ramhal hold that we will rise in refined bodies forever, since body and soul served God together.
  • Whatever the details, the core is that our existence continues after death, and that is where reward and punishment lie.
