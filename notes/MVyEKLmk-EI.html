The halacha

  • There are six fasts: Yom Kippur from the Torah; Tisha BeAv, a 24-hour fast from the Neviim; the 17th of Tammuz, 10th of Tevet and 3rd of Tishri, also from the Neviim but daytime only; and Taanit Esther.
  • Taanit Esther is not from the Neviim and is even post-Gemara. It is first recorded explicitly in Midrash Tanhuma, from the time of the Geonim. Some even argued one may not fast the day before the happy day of Purim, but as the Rambam writes, all Israel have accepted it, so every person is obligated.
  • As the Rama writes, it is not obligatory in the same way as the other fasts, so there are leniencies. Pregnant and nursing women do not fast (Sepharadim exempt them from all fasts except Yom Kippur and Tisha BeAv; on Taanit Esther even Ashkenazim agree).
  • Also exempt: the very old even if not weak; a hatan in his seven days of Sheva Berakhot, even when the fast is on its proper day; the sandak, the mohel and the father of the boy; and one in considerable pain, such as from the eyes, for which he would take medication, even if not bedridden.
  • We fast not because Esther fasted but because on the 13th (of Adar) they went out to war, and our ancestors fasted when going to war as a merit for victory. Fasting three days after Purim to commemorate Esther's fast is a separate minhag.
  • For Sepharadim the fast begins 72 minutes before sunrise, per Hakham Ovadiah, about 5 a.m. in London. One may eat before then only if one stipulated before sleeping; otherwise, per the Shulhan Arukh, going to sleep accepts the fast.
  • It is not a sad fast: we mourn nothing, but remember our ancestors going out to war and succeeding.

Sources quoted

  • Midrash Tanhuma: the earliest explicit record of the fast.
  • The Rambam: "Kevar nahagu kol Yisrael", all Israel have accepted it.
  • The Rama: the fast is less obligatory than the others, and one in great pain may break it.

In one line

Taanit Esther is binding by universal custom but carries more leniencies than the other fasts, and it commemorates going out to war, not mourning.
