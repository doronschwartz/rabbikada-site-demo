Where we are

This is the second part of that Monday evening. The first part reviewed the whole Introduction of the book, and its summary can be found in the previous recording. Now the class begins the book proper: Essay 1, which is about creation ex nihilo, the world being created from nothing.

The essay has a short introduction and then four chapters. Rav Saadiah's position is clear: Hashem created the world from nothingness. He will bring biblical proofs and four intellectual proofs, and then he will discuss twelve other opinions and refute them. That is the plan for the next few weeks.

Not as obvious as it sounds

You might think this is obvious: of course every Jew believes that Hashem created the world from nothing. But it is not so simple. Other philosophers did not believe it, and even among our Hakhamim there were those who held differently. The Rambam at least contemplated the possibility that the world was formed from something already there, and other Rishonim, such as the ibn Ezra, held that although the world was created by Kadosh Barukh Hu, it was created from pre-existent matter. This matter is called hylé in Greek, and the class will come back to it. The Rabbi said this may sound surprising, but we will see the other views, and Rav Saadiah's own view is very clear.

The problem: which source of knowledge can we use?

In the Introduction we learned that all knowledge comes from one of three sources: sense perception, reason and inference. Rav Saadiah says that when it comes to how the world was created, he has a problem.

  • Sense perception is out. Nobody was around when the world began.
  • Self-evident reason does not settle it either; nothing here is self-evident.
  • Inference needs something to infer from, either a sense perception or a piece of reason, and there is nothing to start with.

So he will have to use reason in an extended form, not just self-evident reason but broader philosophical argument. Such arguments do not have the same force as a direct observation, and that is the nature of the subject. He asks the reader not to criticise him for that, for two reasons. First, it is impossible to proceed otherwise. Second, everyone who holds a conflicting view had to rely on exactly the same kind of reasoning.

The introduction itself

Rav Saadiah begins by warning the reader. What we are about to discuss is extremely fine and subtle, perhaps the most difficult subject in the book, because none of our senses can perceive it and the best we can do is use our intellect. So the reader has to be willing, before setting out, to accept whatever conclusion we reach, even though we cannot sense it. Do not try to change the conclusion at the end just because it is something you never saw.

He adds that this is not only our problem. The Islamic philosophers, the Mutakallimun, did not see it either. (The Rabbi explained that the name comes from the Arabic for "word", and that the Hebrew translation medabrim, "speakers", is imprecise but the best available. Both Rav Saadiah and the Rambam, whom the class studies on Tuesday nights, wrote in Arabic and often quote these thinkers.) If we conclude that the world was created from nothing, we should be happy with that conclusion, since we have reached our goal.

He also explains why this is the right way. If everything could be proven by what we see, there would be no need for philosophy. There would be nothing to analyse, and everyone would agree. We only need reason and inference for what we cannot sense.

Everyone accepts something unseen

Now he shows that every other view asks us to accept something nobody has ever seen.

  • An eternal world. This is the Aristotelian view, that the world has no beginning and no end. The Rabbi noted that the Rambam famously said he does not find Aristotle's view convincing, but that if he had been convinced, he would have been willing to reinterpret the Torah accordingly. But can anyone name one thing they have touched, seen or heard that has no beginning and no end? Even the sun will come to an end. Everything we know has a beginning. Yet they accept something eternal, using their intellect.
  • Two beginnings. The dualists say there were two gods, or two opposites, light and dark, good and bad, and that the world came from the conflict between them. Have we ever seen two opposing forces together produce something as remarkable as a world?
  • Pre-existent matter. This is the view of Plato and others: the world was created, but from an everlasting matter with no heat or cold, no moisture or dryness, which at some point, through some force, started to produce things that are warm, cold, moist and dry. But nothing in our world lacks all four of these qualities. Nobody has ever seen such pure matter, and still they accept it.

So whichever road you take, you accept something you cannot see. Therefore, if our conclusion is that the world was created from nothing, do not be worried by the fact that we cannot see it.

Three things to remember

Rav Saadiah ends the introduction with three things to keep in mind throughout every essay.

  1. Our proofs will be stronger than the proofs of the other views.
  2. We will have a refutation for every counter-argument.
  3. We have an advantage that the others do not: the Torah and the miracles of the prophets, which verify our beliefs.

The Rabbi noted that there is some psychology here. Twelve different opinions are about to be presented, and the reader is told not to worry. Our proofs are stronger, we can answer every one of them, and we have the Torah on our side.

Summary

  • Essay 1 is about creation from nothing, with an introduction, four chapters, and twelve rival views to refute.
  • Some Hakhamim and Rishonim held or considered that the world was formed from pre-existent matter; Rav Saadiah's view is that Hashem created it from nothing.
