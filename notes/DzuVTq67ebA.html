Recap of Bishul

  • Bishul — transforming food with the power of fire, as in the Mishkan. Cooking directly in the sun is permitted; cooking in something heated by the sun is rabbinically forbidden (an onlooker assumes it came off the fire).
  • A kli rishon — even off the fire — still cooks while above 45°C. In a kli sheni there is strictly no Bishul, apart from a few tender foods (eggs, perhaps tea).
  • No problem of bishul after afiyah or vice versa — croutons may go into soup (for Ashkenazim too if a ladle was used).
  • Reheating: solids may be reheated (ein bishul achar bishul be'davar yavesh) — their main purpose, being edible, remains. Liquids, once cooled below yad soledet bo (45°C), may not be reheated.

Part A — Completing Bishul

A solid dish with some liquid

Salmon cooked before Shabbat in a sauce, taken from the fridge: solid or liquid? Chacham Ovadia Yosef: follow how the dish is called. We call it "salmon" (with some sauce for taste), not "sauce with salmon" — so it is a solid and may be reheated. But a very watery cholent or a stew that is more liquid than solid counts as a liquid and may not be reheated.

Asking a non-Jew to reheat a liquid

Normally what is forbidden to us may not be asked of a non-Jew. But many great authorities — including the Rambam — hold there is no Bishul in reheating an already-cooked liquid. Although we don't follow them, on the strength of their view one may directly ask a non-Jew (e.g. an au pair) to reheat soup that was fully cooked (boiled) before Shabbat and has since cooled — no need even to hint.

Eating in a home where Bishul isn't kept

For many melachot there is a penalty (knas): one may not benefit on Shabbat from something done in a forbidden way — even if done in error, and even by a guest who did nothing — so as not to encourage, even implicitly, the breaking of Shabbat. Food cooked (or finished cooking) on Shabbat may not be eaten on Shabbat; after Shabbat it may be eaten (if done unintentionally).

  • If you know or strongly suspect your host does not keep the laws of Bishul — that is a serious problem; if already invited, consult a rabbinic authority.
  • This applies to Bishul, which changes the food's essence. Laws like Borer (selecting) don't change the food itself — so eating where the host may not know the details of Borer is fine.

Part B — She'hiyah: Leaving Food on the Fire into Shabbat

The principle — and the rabbinic exception

Anything set going before Shabbat may continue into Shabbat: a garden sprinkler switched on at 3:45 may water all Shabbat; timers may switch lights on and off. By the same logic, a raw piece of meat put on at 3:30 to cook through Shabbat should be fine from the Torah — and it is.

But Chazal knew human nature — especially our love of Shabbat food. You come home from shul at 5, guests arrive at 5:30, and the meat needs two more hours… You may be tempted to turn up the flame — two Torah prohibitions at once:

  1. Bishul — speeding up cooking (megis).
  2. Mav'ir — kindling: raising a flame is like lighting one.

In Talmudic times this meant stoking the coals. So Chazal forbade leaving food on the fire into Shabbat unless it has reached a certain stage.

How cooked must it be?

A major debate in the Gemara and poskim:

  • One view: fully cooked.
  • The other: ma'achal ben Derusai — edible in a pinch, roughly half-cooked. (Ben Derusai was a highwayman always on the move, who ate his food half-cooked.) Once half-cooked, there is no fear you will tamper to finish it.

The halacha follows the second view: once food is half-cooked before Shabbat, it may be left on even an uncovered flame to finish cooking overnight or until morning.

Winter warning: in summer, food put on at 7 is ready long before a 9 pm Shabbat. In midwinter, with Shabbat at 3:35–4:00, food put on at 3 for a 5:30 meal may not be half-cooked when Shabbat begins — then it may not be left as is.

The blech

Chazal permitted less-than-half-cooked food if the coals were covered — a clear sign that you've taken your mind off the fire. That is the origin of the blech: a sheet of metal covering the flame as a reminder not to touch the fire. If food is already half-cooked (and you won't return food during Shabbat), you don't need a blech.

  • Covering the dials: today one raises the heat with the knobs, not the coals, so some cover the dials too (some blechs bend over them). Strictly, not necessary — the sign belongs on the place of the fire.
  • Hotplates: modern hotplates in England have no dial — a fixed temperature you cannot change. So food — even less than half-cooked — may be left to cook on a hotplate from before Shabbat. (Old hotplates with dials would be a problem.)
  • Gas or electric makes no difference.

The urn

The same rule applies to liquids. Fill the urn early enough — at least half an hour before Shabbat (more for a big urn) — so the water reaches at least 45°C ("half-cooked") by Shabbat. Better still, fully boiled: if you take water out while it is still heating (say at 75°C), the smaller remaining volume will heat faster — you've indirectly sped up the boiling. If only at 45°C at Shabbat, don't draw from it until it has fully boiled.

Stuck with no blech or hotplate

  • Make a makeshift blech from several folded layers of aluminium foil (one sheet is not substantial enough).
  • Question from the floor: no foil — can you put an empty pot on the flame and the food pot inside or on top of it? — Yes, excellent: it serves as the sign, as long as there is a gap.
  • If nothing is available and Shabbat is minutes away — seek rabbinic guidance. Some argue the decree doesn't apply to a steady modern flame: Chazal feared stoking coals that die down; a gas flame doesn't fade. We don't follow this, but it is a significant opinion for someone stranded.

Part C — Hazara: Returning Food to the Fire on Shabbat

From the Torah, putting a fully cooked schnitzel into a pot on a lit flame on Shabbat is no problem — solids aren't re-cooked and no flame is being adjusted. Chazal forbade it for two reasons:

  1. Mechzei ke'mevashel — it looks just like weekday cooking (fridge → pot → fire).
  2. You may be tempted to turn up the flame to reheat it faster.

This applies even to food that was on the fire when Shabbat began but was taken off and has cooled.

Returning food to a blech — two conditions

  1. The flame is covered (blech, foil) — a sign against tampering, and not the normal way of cooking.
  2. Continuity — the food was taken off intending to return it and not put down somewhere that breaks the connection (e.g. the fridge or the floor). Off the blech, onto the table, serve, back on — fine. Into the fridge and back — a new act, forbidden even with a blech.

Hotplates — a different category

Neither concern applies to a dial-less hotplate: you can't raise the temperature, and it doesn't look like cooking — it's even called a "plata shel Shabbat". So with a hotplate there is no Hazara problem at all:

  • A solid (or a mostly-solid dish like salmon in sauce) may be taken straight from the fridge and put on the hotplate — directly on the plate too.
  • Challah may be warmed on the hotplate (but not placed fresh on a blech).
  • Liquids: the issue isn't Hazara but Bishul — a cooled liquid may not be reheated. Soup taken off to serve and returned while still above 45°C is fine.

Reheating cold soup yourself — the timer method

  • Ask a non-Jew to put the cold (pre-cooked) soup on the hotplate — or even a blech (not a naked flame).
  • Or: if the hotplate is on a timer and currently off, you may place the pre-cooked, cooled soup on it; when the timer switches on, it heats by itself (Chacham Ovadia — explicit). E.g. put it on at 8:30 before shul for an 11 o'clock lunch. Only for food already cooked — never raw food.
  • Q: If the hotplate went off at 11 and comes back on at 12, may you put the soup on while it's still warm? — Only if the soup is below 45°C; above that, it would be cooking.

Part D — Hatmana: Insulating Food

Wrapping to retain heat

Without a hotplate, people used to boil the food just before Shabbat and wrap the pot in towels — big pots stay hot for four or five hours (a common practice in Israel). Chazal forbade fully wrapping food on Shabbat itself, even with something that only retains heat (davar she'eino mosif hevel) — because one naturally heats food up before wrapping it, and so may come to cook or kindle.

  • Wrapping before Shabbat with something that only retains heat — permitted.
  • Only full wrapping is forbidden. Leaving one side open, or a tea towel just on top, is fine.
  • Wrapping in something that adds heat (davar ha'mosif hevel) — e.g. burying a pot in coals — is forbidden even before Shabbat.

Crockpots

A crockpot's element surrounds the pot — seemingly hatmana in something that adds heat, forbidden even from before Shabbat; some do not allow crockpots for this reason. But the halacha is that we may use crockpots. Among the reasons: a pot's own lid is not considered wrapping (otherwise one could never put a lid on), and the crockpot has only that single lid — but one should not put anything further on top of it. To satisfy all views, place two balls of aluminium foil between the pot and the heating element, creating a gap.

Thermos flasks and hot-water bottles

Both are permitted — including filling them from the urn on Shabbat. Hatmana applies only to a kli rishon; once water is poured into a flask or bottle it is a kli sheni, with far less concern that one will heat it first. Likewise, hot water poured into a pan may be wrapped.

Summary

  • A mostly-solid dish (salmon in sauce) may be reheated; a watery stew may not. A non-Jew may be asked to reheat cooled soup.
  • Food cooked on Shabbat may not be eaten on Shabbat — beware hosts who don't keep Bishul (not an issue for Borer).
  • She'hiyah: food left on from before Shabbat must be at least half-cooked — or on a blech or dial-less hotplate. Fill the urn early; ideally fully boiled.
  • Hazara: returning food to a blech needs a covered flame and continuity (not via the fridge). On a hotplate any cooked solid may go on, even from the fridge; cooled liquids only via a non-Jew or a hotplate on a timer that is off.
  • Hatmana: don't fully wrap a kli rishon on Shabbat; before Shabbat is fine with heat-retaining wraps. Crockpots permitted (ideally with foil balls); thermos flasks and hot-water bottles permitted.
