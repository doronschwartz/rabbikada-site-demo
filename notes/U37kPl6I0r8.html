Topic: The berakhah on Shabbat and Yom Tov candles, and whether Shehiyanu is said when lighting Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263, seif 5, with the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada in his Thursday-night Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 1 July 2021 (a short recording, about ten minutes)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The previous week the class had learned why women usually light rather than men, giving both the mystical and the rational reasons, and the practical differences that follow. They had also discussed not lighting too early or too late, and a man who must leave for synagogue early and has someone light for him at home. This short recording covers seif 5, and the Rabbi said it would show, in three places, how important minhag is.

The berakhah: "lehadlik ner shel Shabbat"

Whoever lights, man or woman, says the same berakhah, "asher kiddeshanu bemisvotav vesivanu lehadlik ner shel Shabbat", because men are equally obligated. The berakhah says ner, one flame, because, as the class learned two weeks earlier, the misvah is one candle.

On Yom Tov one also makes a berakhah: "lehadlik ner shel Yom Tov".

Shehiyanu at the candles?

A man living alone who lights on Yom Tov certainly does not say Shehiyanu then; he will say it in Kiddush. The question is a woman who lights.

The Mishnah Berurah (seif katan 23) writes that she should not say Shehiyanu at the lighting, since it will be said later at Kiddush, and that only where women have the custom to say it should they not be stopped. The Rabbi said this is the halacha for both Ashkenazim and Sefaradim.

Why Shehiyanu belongs at Kiddush. Shehiyanu on Yom Tov is said because a new festival time has arrived. Strictly, it does not need any misvah: "You can do it in the shuk, in the middle of the street." It became customary to say it at night with Kiddush, at the start of the festival.

Why not at the candles. Some women began saying it because they felt that by lighting they were accepting the festival. The Rabbi gave two reasons this is wrong:

  1. The idea that lighting the candles means accepting Shabbat or Yom Tov is strongly disputed, and Sefaradim do not hold it. The class would learn this in detail in a later seif. If you have not accepted the festival, you cannot say Shehiyanu, because the festival has not begun.
  2. You will hear Shehiyanu shortly at Kiddush, so there is no reason to say it now.

So the standard practice is that a woman does not say Shehiyanu when lighting, and waits for Kiddush.

Minhag. However, the Ben Ish Hai records clearly that the women of Baghdad did say it, and that is the Iraqi custom. "I don't want to object." Where that is the custom, it is kept. This was the first example of the importance of minhag.

The price of saying it

The Rabbi pointed out a practical consequence. During the year, a woman who lights early has not accepted Shabbat unless she says so explicitly. On a summer Friday she may light at eight o'clock, remember something she forgot, and still do it before Shabbat actually begins.

But on Yom Tov, if she says Shehiyanu, she has declared that the festival time has arrived, and she cannot go back on that. For example, on Shavuot night, when the festival begins late, a woman who lights early with Shehiyanu can no longer do work.

A question from the class. Someone said that some women would rather not light early at all, so that they can keep cooking until Shabbat. The Rabbi answered that if she wants to keep working until Shabbat begins, she can simply light later, nearer the time. Lighting itself does not stop her, as the class would learn. "For the rest of your life, I made your wife's life easier," he joked.

Answering Amen to her husband's Shehiyanu

Now suppose a woman follows the Iraqi custom and said Shehiyanu when she lit. Her husband comes home and makes Kiddush, and she fulfils Kiddush through him. He then says Shehiyanu. Should she answer Amen?

The class suggested it would be a hefsek, an interruption. The Rabbi agreed and illustrated: if you are in the middle of Kiddush, about to drink, and hear someone else finish a berakhah, you do not answer Amen, because it is an interruption between your berakhah and the drinking. Here too, she has already said her own Shehiyanu, and answering Amen to his would seemingly be a hefsek within the Kiddush she is hearing. The recording ends as he begins to explain this further.

Practical summary

  • Men and women say the same berakhah, "lehadlik ner shel Shabbat", and on Yom Tov "lehadlik ner shel Yom Tov".
  • A woman generally does not say Shehiyanu when lighting Yom Tov candles; she hears it at Kiddush. This is the halacha for Ashkenazim and Sefaradim.
  • Where women have the custom to say it, as in Baghdad according to the Ben Ish Hai, the custom is kept and should not be objected to.
  • Saying Shehiyanu at the candles is a declaration that the festival has begun, so after it she may no longer do work.
  • Without Shehiyanu, lighting does not by itself mean accepting Shabbat for Sefaradim, so a woman who lit early may still do something she forgot.
  • If a woman said Shehiyanu at lighting, answering Amen to her husband's Shehiyanu at Kiddush is seemingly a hefsek.
