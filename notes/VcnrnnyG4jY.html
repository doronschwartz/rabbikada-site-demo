Sefer: Pirkei Avot, Chapter 3, with the commentaries of the Rambam and the Meiri Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 20 April 2021, the first class of a four-part series preparing for Shavuot

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where you came from, where you are going

The Rabbi chose to read the third chapter of Avot with only two commentators, the Rambam and the Meiri of Perpignan, who share a style of explanation.

Akavya ben Mahalalel says: gaze at three things and you will not come to sin. Know where you came from, where you are going, and before whom you will give judgement and accounting. The Rambam explains these as past, future and present: - The past brings humility: look at your origins. - The future frees you from the physical world: one day we will all share the same bed. - The accounting keeps you observing the misvot.

The word is histakel, "gaze," not just look. People confuse the two, he noted.

Akavya himself was a remarkable man. The Mishnah in Eduyot tells that he disagreed with his colleagues on four points, and they offered to make him Av Beit Din if he retracted. He answered: "Better to be called a fool all my days than to be wicked for one moment before HaKadosh Barukh Hu." Yet before he died he told his son to retract, because the son had heard these views only from him, against the majority. And when his son asked him to put in a good word for him with his colleagues, he refused: "Your deeds will bring you close, and your deeds will distance you."

Why do we say din veheshbon, judgement and accounting, and not the other way round? The Hida explains that in Heaven a person is first asked to give his own judgement on such a case. That is why, as the Gemara in Shabbat teaches, one who judges others favourably is judged favourably. Every person is complex; if you find the good in others, HaShem will find the good in you.

Law, Torah and company

Rabbi Hanina, the deputy Kohen Gadol, taught: pray for the welfare of the government, for without fear of it people would swallow each other alive. He lived at the end of the Second Temple, when the high priesthood was bought with bribes, and chose to stay as deputy rather than join the corruption. The Rabbi brought the Gemara in Yoma about the two kohanim racing up the ramp, where one stabbed the other to get there first. Even people serving HaShem need order.

When two sit together without words of Torah, it is a gathering of scoffers; when they share Torah, the Shekhinah rests between them. A moshav lesim is not people who fail to talk Torah every minute. It is sitting down to talk about nothing. Three who eat together without words of Torah are as if they ate from sacrifices to idols; with words of Torah, it is "the table that is before HaShem."

The Meiri's down-to-earth reading

Rabbi Hanina ben Hakhinai warns against staying awake at night, travelling alone, and turning the heart to idleness. Many commentators read this mystically. The Meiri says simply: - Staying awake at night is bad for your health. - Travelling alone is dangerous, even by day. - Sitting idle without work leads to a kind of mental death. "Get yourself a job."

On "whoever takes on the yoke of Torah is freed from the yoke of government," the Meiri adds with humour that soldiers who see you always with your books will decide you cannot fight and leave you alone. The Rabbi added, without boasting, that in the periods of his life when he was fully immersed in learning, things somehow took care of themselves.

"Give Him what is His, for you and yours are His." The Meiri: when you spend money on a mezuzah, say Barukh HaShem you have a house; on a parapet, that you have a roof; on sisit, that you have clothes.

Stopping to admire a tree

The most beautiful Mishnah of the evening: one who is walking while learning, stops and says "how beautiful is this tree," is considered to have forfeited his life. What is wrong with admiring nature? Does the Rambam not teach that understanding the world is part of Torah? The Rabbi read it this way: the fault lies in considering it an interruption. If you see stopping to appreciate HaShem's world as a break from Torah, your hashkafah is wrong. That appreciation is itself Torah.

He also covered the Mishnah on revision: forgetting is a fault only when you never set aside time to review. And the Rambam, citing Aristotle, on fear of sin coming before wisdom. When a person comes to study with good traits and fear of sin already in place, what he learns confirms who he is, so he holds on to it. When he comes without them, the learning goes against his nature, and he will not retain it.

A barometer: if people are pleased with you, HaShem is pleased with you. Not popularity, he stressed, but whether people feel at ease with you and have a good word to say. And the Meiri on sleeping late, drinking wine in the middle of the day and idle chatter: luxuries are fine on holiday, but as a lifestyle they trap you in materialism.

No share in the World to Come

Rabbi Elazar HaModai lists those who have no share in the World to Come, even with Torah and good deeds: one who profanes sacred things, despises the festivals, shames others in public, annuls the covenant of Avraham, and reveals meanings in the Torah not according to halacha.

The Meiri equates this with karet. The Rabbi explained that, against the view that karet means early death, the Rambam and the Meiri understand it as the soul being cut off and ceasing to exist. Gehinam is in a sense good news: it means there is something to fix. He gave the parable of the apple: a bruised apple you keep and cut away the bruises; a rotten one you throw away.

The common thread, says the Meiri, is either heresy or moral bankruptcy. Despising the festivals denies HaShem's involvement in the world. And the Meiri divides the Torah into three kinds of verse: - Only deeper meaning: anthropomorphic verses, such as "the hand of HaShem." - Only plain meaning: do not murder, do not steal. - Both: misvot such as peot, Shabbat and tefillin, which have a reason but must still be done.

The danger in his time was people who said: the point of Shabbat is to remember the Creator, so I can drive and think about creation; the point of tefillin is God's unity, so I will meditate instead. That, says the Meiri, destroys the Torah, because "you don't know all the reasons of the Torah."

Everything is foreseen

Rabbi Akiva: everything is foreseen, yet freedom is given; the world is judged with goodness, and all is according to the majority of deeds. The Rambam explains that for HaShem there is no past or future, so His knowledge does not remove our choice. And "the majority of deeds" means it is better to give 10 pounds to 100 people than 1,000 pounds to one. The reward may be equal, but each act of giving shapes your character. The same is true of bad habits: a repeated small wrong does more damage than a single one.

The class ended at nightfall, with counting the Omer together.

Summary

  • Akavya's three things, past, future and accounting, bring humility, detachment and observance.
  • The Meiri reads Avot practically: sleep, safety, work and gratitude for what you have.
  • Admiring HaShem's world is not an interruption of Torah; thinking it is, is the error.
  • The Meiri warns against keeping a misvah's reason while dropping the misvah.
  • Many small good deeds shape a person more than one large one.
