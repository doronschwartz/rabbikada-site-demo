Sefer: Pirkei Avot with the Rambam's commentary Chapter: Chapter 3 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series on the Rambam's commentary to Pirkei Avot, Tuesday 25 June 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Three things to keep in mind

The Rabbi said the Rambam's commentary on Chapter 3 has only two big new points, so he planned to move quickly. In the end it took the whole evening.

Mishnah 1. Akavya ben Mahalalel: consider three things and you will not come to sin. Where you came from, a putrid drop; where you are going, a place of worms; and before whom you will give account, the King of kings. The Rambam sees these as a complete combination. Remembering where you came from is humility. The Rabbi noted that the people who stay successful are the ones who remember their roots. Remembering where you are going keeps you from sinking into the pleasures of this world. Remembering the reckoning reminds you that time is short.

Pray for the welfare of the government, for without fear of it people would swallow each other alive. The Rabbi linked this to Yirmiyahu's call to seek the peace of the city of exile, and to the prayer for the royal family on Shabbat morning: law and order is something we pray for.

Sitting together: scoffers and the Shekhinah

Mishnah 2. Two who sit together with no words of Torah between them are a moshav lesim, a session of scoffers; two who share words of Torah have the Shekhinah among them. The Rambam reminds us that for him "Torah" includes hokhmah, wisdom and understanding of the world. The point is people who meet and talk only about the news, with no content at all.

The Gemara brings verses for ten, three, two and even one person studying. Why so many?

  • One person studying alone still has the Shekhinah with him.
  • Two: Hazal say their words are "written in a book of remembrance". The Rabbi cited the Maharsha: what is learned together is written in the mind and lasts longer. That is why we learn in havruta.
  • Three are a beit din, and their sitting in judgement is itself Torah study. The Rabbi found this reassuring as a dayan. Sometimes in beit din "you feel you're wasting your time" on spreadsheets and the details of people's arguments, yet the Shekhinah rests there.
  • Ten: the Shekhinah comes first and waits for them. "I presume all of this applies on Zoom as well."

So no one should think: I have no shiur or havruta tonight, so it does not matter. There are times for light conversation, he added, but not as the norm; we should choose friends we can have meaningful conversations with.

Mishnah 3. Three who eat at one table without words of Torah are as if they ate from offerings to the dead; with words of Torah, they eat from HaShem's table. A long meal should carry some content. That means the commanded or desirable speech from the five categories of Chapter 1, not talk about other people or the news.

Thoughts in the dark

Mishnah 4 warns about one who is awake at night or travels alone and turns his heart to idleness. The Rabbi brought a commentary of the Rambam not printed in his edition. These are the times, in the dark, alone and with time on your hands, when heretical thoughts come. He told of a teenager who told his rabbi he no longer believed in God. Had he studied the Moreh Nevuchim, Aristotle and the other works? No. He had been on a coach home after a long day, in the rain and traffic, looked out of the window, and decided there could not be a God. That, said the Rabbi, is exactly the Mishnah's point: use those hours for constructive thoughts.

The yoke of Torah (Mishnah 5). Whoever accepts the yoke of Torah, which the Rambam understands as regular, fixed study, is freed from the yoke of government and of worldly concerns. "I'm telling you from experience," he said: when a person truly commits to something, other demands somehow get done by others or fall away. Hazal's reading of the engraving on the tablets, harut, as herut, freedom, means for the Rambam freedom from life's upheavals. The Rabbi added that this must be done responsibly, not by ignoring everything else.

The tree and the field

Mishnah 7. Someone travelling who stops his learning to say "how beautiful is this tree, how beautiful is this field" is as if liable for his life. What could be wrong with admiring HaShem's world? The Rabbi shared an explanation he had seen some years ago, not his own. The mistake is compartmentalising: Torah in one box, nature in another. Someone who feels he must stop learning to admire a tree has the wrong picture. A living Torah colours how we see the world. One of his roshei yeshivah said that someone who has really learned Eruvin sees the walls in the street differently.

Forgetting (Mishnah 8). We cannot forget on purpose. Removing Torah "from your heart" means neglecting it and filling the mind with other things.

Wisdom, fear of sin and habits

Mishnah 9. Whoever's fear of sin comes before his wisdom, his wisdom will endure; whoever's wisdom comes first, his wisdom will not endure. The same for deeds and wisdom. This is the first place in the chapter where the Rambam refers back to the eight chapters. It is not a metaphysical punishment, the Rabbi explained. Someone with good habits who then studies ethics finds it describes who he already is, and wants more. Someone with bad habits finds the same study tells him he is doing wrong, so he will not keep at it.

What drives a person from the world. Late morning sleep, wine at midday, chatter with children and sitting with the ignorant. For the Rambam these block the development of character because they waste time. "My most productive hours are always early morning."

Mishnah 11. One who shames another in public, or "reveals faces in the Torah", has no share in the world to come. Rashi reads this as false interpretation; the Rambam as public, brazen transgression, the root of heresy. Teshuvah always helps; the Mishnah means these are sins that even death does not atone for. He explained Olam HaBa not as a store of reward but as the identity a person builds. He admitted he could not explain why shaming someone, though terrible, should break that identity: "I don't have a good explanation."

Mishnah 12. Be submissive to a superior, by which the Rambam means someone greater in virtue, and serve him. Tishhoret, from black hair, means the young: an older person keeps his dignity with them. Yet receive every person with joy. This goes further than Shammai's "with a pleasant face". Status does not stop us from laughing or sharing a hug with someone younger.

All is foreseen, and the majority of deeds

Mishnah 15. Rabbi Akiva: all is foreseen, and freedom is given; the world is judged with goodness, and all is according to the majority of deeds. The Rambam says you cannot understand this without the eight chapters. As we saw in Chapter 8, HaShem and His knowledge are one, and as we cannot grasp His essence we cannot grasp His knowledge. Somehow foreknowledge and free will are compatible.

Then the second big point. Virtue is not built by one great act but by many small ones. In Chapter 4 of the eight chapters we learned to bend toward the opposite extreme to break a bad trait. Here the Rambam adds that many small actions do this better than a few large ones. A stingy person who once gives a thousand pounds to someone in need has done something wonderful and broken his stinginess once. Someone who gives one pound to a thousand people has repeated the act a thousand times, and it becomes part of him. "It's according to the majority of one's actions and not the significance of one's actions."

The Rabbi applied it widely. Headlines go to the one heroic act, but greatness is consistency. He quoted an explanation of the midwives Shifrah and Puah: one made the babies beautiful and one cooed to them. Why name them for that rather than for saving lives? Because true greatness shows in the small acts of care. The same is true of hatmadah: five hours of learning two days running is better than ten hours one day and none the next. A participant added the Sefer HaHinukh: a person is shaped by his actions.

Mishnah 17 pairs things that complete each other. Binah is understanding; daat, as in the Torah's "knowing" someone, is connecting to it. Without daat, knowledge stays abstract, like a Torah that never reaches how we see the world.

Summary

  • Remembering origin, destiny and reckoning keeps a person from sin; pray for the government that keeps order.
  • People should meet with content between them; the Shekhinah is with one, two, three and ten who learn, and a beit din's work is Torah.
  • A living Torah shapes how we see the world, including a beautiful tree.
  • Good habits first, then study of ethics, or the wisdom will not last.
  • "All is according to the majority of deeds": many small acts shape character more than one big one.
