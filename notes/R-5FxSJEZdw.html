These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two principles before we start

This was the first of three Tuesday-night classes before Pesah: preparing the home, then the Seder, then the rare year (2021) when Erev Pesah falls on Shabbat. The Rabbi set out the evening's plan: hekhsherim, Ashkenazim and Sephardim eating together, medicines, cosmetics, and cleaning the house.

To understand what needs a hekhsher, he began with the one unique stringency of Pesah. All year, non-kosher food mixed into kosher food is nullified in sixty. On Pesah, hames bemashehu: even a drop of hames forbids the whole dish. The reason is that hames will become permitted again after the festival, and the Hakhamim do not allow nullification for something that will soon be permitted anyway. This is a rabbinic enactment.

The question is whether this one stringency spreads to the other leniencies of kashrut. Two examples from Siman 447:

  • A pot unused for 24 hours. All year, taste absorbed in a pot more than 24 hours old is a spoiled taste (noten taam lifgam), and food cooked in it by mistake is permitted. The Shulhan Arukh rules that this applies on Pesah too. The Rema writes that some are strict. So if, by mistake only, Pesah food was cooked in a hames pot not used for 24 hours, that food may be eaten on Pesah. Never do it deliberately; if done on purpose the food is forbidden.
  • Nullified before Pesah. If hames fell into food before Pesah, it is nullified in sixty and does not "reawaken" when Pesah arrives, since a nullified prohibition does not return. That is the Shulhan Arukh. The Rema says the custom is to be strict.

A second principle runs through all of kashrut: we do not presume that things went wrong. Everything has a presumption of being kosher unless we know otherwise. The Shulhan Arukh applies this on Pesah too, for example to meat, cheese and fish salted before Pesah without special care; the Rema is stricter.

What needs a hekhsher?

Take sugar, salt and oil, with labels saying 100% sugar, salt or oil. What could go wrong?

  • Hidden ingredients? In countries like England and America every ingredient must be listed by law, even those under 2%. He cited Rav Moshe Feinstein, who relied on government regulation for unsupervised milk.
  • A worker's sandwich? Two answers. We do not presume it happened. And even if a crumb fell in, it fell in before Pesah, so it is nullified in sixty and does not reawaken.

So for Sephardim, anything with purely natural ingredients and no additives does not need a Pesah hekhsher, provided it is bought before Pesah, not on Hol HaMoed. Ashkenazim, who hold that hames reawakens, may be stricter. This includes sugar, salt, oil, pure milk and yogurt, sparkling water, whole spices and pure spices, kettle chips made of potatoes, sunflower oil and salt, and even Coke (which Ashkenazim avoid because of kitniyot).

To show how far the principle goes, he brought rulings of Rav Ovadia Yoseph. If a child threw bread into the barrel of wine meant for the four cups, strain it and use it: the crumbs fell in before Pesah and are nullified. Even wheat grains that became hames, mixed before Pesah into sixty times as much good wheat, may be used to bake massot. Rav Ovadia also records the custom of buying dried fruit in the market without any supervision.

He added a word on cost. "Pesah for many people is a difficult time financially." Anyone who wishes to be strict like the Rema may do so, but we must know what is required and what is an extra stringency.

One caution from the questions: a label saying "may be processed on the same machinery as wheat" is different. There you are told of a possible problem, so the "do not presume" logic falls away, and he would not allow it without knowing more.

Ashkenazim and Sephardim at one table

Many Sephardim eat kitniyot such as rice. An Ashkenazi guest or son-in-law should not eat the kitniyot themselves. But the utensils are completely fine for them, even if rice was cooked in the pot within the last 24 hours. Kitniyot are not hames, so what the pot absorbed is no problem for an Ashkenazi.

Medicines

The Rambam writes that forbidden food taken in a way that gives no pleasure to the palate, such as bitter medicine, is permitted even where there is no danger to life. Some object: if a person chooses to eat something inedible, he has given it importance (akhshevei). Almost all authorities answer that taking a tablet does not give importance to the hames mixed into it; you want the tablet.

  • Rav Moshe Feinstein (Igrot Moshe, Orah Hayim 2:92) permits any tasteless medicine, even for a slight headache.
  • Rav Ovadia Yoseph is stricter: rely on this only when ill, though not necessarily diagnosed. He also notes that most medicines today contain no hames.

The Rabbi's conclusion:

  • If you do not know of hames in a tablet, you may take it even for a slight headache.
  • If you know it contains hames, take it only when ill, or when stopping would make you ill, such as a course of antibiotics.
  • Coated tablets without taste are fine.
  • Flavoured syrups and medicines, including children's medicine, are forbidden unless checked to be free of hames, unless it is a matter of life and death.

Cosmetics, toothpaste and lipstick

Creams and perfumes may be kept and used on Pesah even if they contain hames, as the consensus of the poskim permits them; wash your hands afterwards. Flavoured toothpaste and mouthwash are different, because they have a taste and go into the mouth. Some are lenient, but the Rabbi's own view is to be strict and buy them with a hekhsher. He was unsure about flavoured lipstick and leaned towards using unflavoured lipstick. Ordinary lipstick is fine, since it is inedible even for a dog. Flavoured dental floss is better avoided.

Cleaning without the stress

"People come into Pesah stressed, anxious, upset, for doing things which no one needs to do." The Hakhamim of old checked their homes on one night, the night of the fourteenth. Starting from Purim or earlier gives people bad feelings towards Pesah.

Biblically, nullification would be enough. The Hakhamim required removal for two reasons: you might not nullify with a full heart, or you might come to eat it. The Sephardi ruling follows the first reason, so we search for a kazayit of hames. He put this at around 26-27 grams, or for a rabbinic matter about 18 grams, roughly a third of a slice of bread. Some are stricter about smaller pieces that might tempt you, such as croutons in the kitchen cupboard. But crumbs under the sofa are neither a kazayit nor tempting.

  • No need to dismantle beds, move freezers, or search bedroom cupboards where food never goes. A quick look is enough.
  • Where young children run around, check wherever their hands can reach, for pieces, not crumbs.
  • Where you will eat or cook on Pesah, the kitchen and dining room, you must remove every crumb, because on Pesah even a drop of hames forbids food.
  • The car: check every compartment for pieces. The fridge: clean properly or cover the shelves.

"Literally, you can take the whole checking for hames outside the kitchen... a couple of hours at most." Spring clean if you like, "but don't blame Pesah" for it.

From the questions

  • Glassware does not absorb: Sephardim may use year-round glasses, even whiskey glasses, once washed. Ashkenazim do not. Still, it is praiseworthy (meshubah) to keep separate Pesah utensils for those who have the space and the means.
  • Oat milk is hames: oats are one of the five grains.
  • Rice should be checked once properly (Rav Ovadia allows once), and the Rabbi suggests two quicker checks after.
  • Dog food with hames may not be owned on Pesah; check the ingredients.
  • Sold hames must be in sealed cupboards, which need not be a single cupboard. A half-open cupboard is not enough. Crumbs are nullified, not sold.

Practical summary

  • For Sephardim, products of purely natural ingredients bought before Pesah do not need a Pesah hekhsher; Ashkenazim may be stricter.
  • Tasteless or bitter medicines are permitted; if known to contain hames, take them only when ill. Flavoured medicines need checking.
  • Creams and perfumes are fine; flavoured toothpaste and mouthwash are better bought with a hekhsher.
  • Outside eating areas, search only for pieces of about a kazayit, not crumbs; in the kitchen and dining room remove every crumb.
  • Ashkenazim may eat non-kitniyot food cooked in a Sephardi pot used for rice.
