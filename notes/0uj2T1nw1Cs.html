Topic: Divination, astrology and mazal: what is forbidden, and what to do with information you did not seek Sources: Vayikra 19:26; Devarim 18:10-13; Shabbat 156a; the Rambam, Hilchot Avodah Zarah, Chapter 11, and his letter to the sages of Marseille; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 179 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 12 July 2022, the third shiur in a series on the weekly parashah (Parashat Balak)

What the Torah forbids

This was the third shiur of the summer series, after classes on tekhelet and on abortion, timed for Parashat Balak, where Bilam declares that there is no divination in Israel. The Rabbi noted that although our scientific understanding has grown enormously, many people are still drawn to the supernatural, so we need to know the Torah's position. He named the shiur after Rabbi Yaakov Hillel's book Faith and Folly, in Hebrew Tamim Tihyeh.

Two passages set out the prohibitions. In Parashat Kedoshim: "do not practise divination or soothsaying". Rashi explains:

  • Nihush is taking signs from events: a black cat crosses the road, so you turn back; bread falls from your mouth, so it is a bad omen.
  • Onen comes from the word for time: "I won't start this job today, it isn't auspicious", or "I won't sign the contract at thirteen o'clock".

Parashat Shoftim adds more: the kosem who decides by his stick, the mekhashef who practises witchcraft, the hover haver who gathers snakes or scorpions, the one who consults an ov or a yiddoni, and the doresh el hametim, who seeks out the dead. The Rabbi stressed a point many people miss: going to a cemetery and asking a departed loved one to do something for you, or to send you a sign, is forbidden. We go to pray to God in the merit of those buried there, not to pray to them.

The passage ends: "You shall be wholehearted (tamim) with the Lord your God." Rashi explains: walk with Him wholeheartedly and do not try to probe the future; accept whatever comes, and then you will be with Him. All of this is straightforward and agreed.

Astrology, and what mazal means

The Torah's list does not mention astrology, which the Rabbi carefully distinguished from astronomy. Astrology is the belief that the time or sign of your birth decides your character and fate. The Gemara (Pesahim 113b) learns from tamim tihyeh that we may not consult astrologers, and that is unanimous: it is in the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh. It covers fortune tellers and anyone else who claims to read a person's future.

But the Gemara also says that life, children and livelihood depend not on merit but on mazal. What is mazal? Not "luck". It comes from nozel, to flow: as the Ramhal and others explain, God's influence reaches the world through a chain, a flow. Mazal tov means "may the flow be good".

Then came the famous Gemara in Shabbat (156a). A person born on Sunday, Monday, Tuesday and so on will have a matching character, depending on what was created that day. Rabbi Hanina says it depends on the hour and the planet: someone born under Mars will shed blood, as a robber, or, the Gemara adds, as a mohel or a shohet. Rabbi Hanina holds there is mazal for Israel; Rabbi Yohanan holds "ein mazal leYisrael", there is no mazal for Israel. And the story of Rabbi Akiva's daughter: astrologers foretold a snake would bite her on her wedding day; she unknowingly pinned the snake to the wall with her hairpin, because that evening she had looked after a poor man everyone else had ignored. "Charity saves from death."

Even Rabbi Hanina, the Rabbi pointed out, limits mazal. In another Gemara (Niddah 16b) he teaches that the angel over conception asks whether the child will be strong or weak, wise or foolish, rich or poor, but never whether he will be righteous or wicked: "everything is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven."

The Rambam: it is all false

The Rambam goes furthest. In Hilchot Avodah Zarah he rules that even if you did not consult anyone, but someone told you, it is forbidden to act on it. A person who arranges his work by an astrologer's timing receives lashes. If someone says, "I can see from your eyes you will have an accident next week, don't drive," and you leave the car at home because of it, according to the Rambam you have violated a prohibition. Anyone who thinks there is truth in these things but that the Torah merely forbids them lacks understanding; people of knowledge know it is all nonsense.

When the sages of Marseille asked him about astrology, he answered in a famous letter. A person should only accept three things: what is proven by reason, as in mathematics and astronomy; what is perceived by the senses; and what is received from the prophets and the righteous. He wrote that he had read every work of astrology translated into Arabic, and found it all without foundation. Astronomy, by contrast, is "an exceedingly glorious science", the kind of wisdom the Hakhamim said the nations possess. The Rabbi pointed to the Rambam's laws of sanctifying the new moon: "you have to be a scientist to understand what he's saying there."

What about the Sages in the Gemara who did believe in mazal? The Rambam answers at the end of the letter: do not abandon what reason has proven for the words of an individual sage, from whom the matter may have been hidden, or who may have spoken in a metaphor or to the needs of the moment. "A man should never cast his reason behind him, for the eyes are set in front, not in back." The Rabbi called this "a sentence to remember for life".

The Rambam reads other passages the same way. The Gemara says a warning given by a shed, a demon, is valid; the Rambam codifies it as a warning from someone you heard but did not see. The Mishnah exempts one who puts out a light on Shabbat because of a ruah raah, an evil spirit; the Rambam explains this as melancholy, a depression in which a person can only find relief alone in the dark. Amulets that may be carried on Shabbat, he writes, help psychologically, not magically.

The Ramban and the other side

The Ramban greatly respected the Rambam but disagreed sharply here. Astrology, he holds, is not one of the forbidden forms of divination, though we still may not go and ask. But if information reaches you, you must take it seriously: pray about it, avoid the bad day, and not rely on a miracle. And he notes that the Gemara is full of Sages who did believe in mazal. Rabbenu Nissim wrote that the Rambam was drawn after the natural scientists. Most sharply of all, the Vilna Gaon wrote that all who came after the Rambam disagreed with him, and that philosophy led him to read these passages as metaphors; they should be taken as written, with an inner meaning, he says, from Kabbalah rather than philosophy.

So, the Rabbi said, you are caught. Told to stay home for seven days: by the Rambam, acting on it is forbidden; by the Ramban, ignoring it is wrong.

The halacha, and his conclusion

The Shulhan Arukh (Yoreh Deah 179) rules that it is forbidden to consult astrologers or lots, and all the more so all other forms of divination. It does not rule between the Rambam and the Ramban. Yet it records a custom not to begin things on Monday or Wednesday, and to marry while the moon is waxing. The Rema, for Ashkenazim, follows the Ramban: once you know something is against the mazal, do not rely on a miracle.

His conclusion: stay away from all of it. Do not go to fortune tellers or to people who read the future from your body, and do not use astrology to find out what lies ahead. But if information comes to you unasked, he believed the choice depends on how you see the world. If your outlook is mystical, follow the Ramban. If not, "you have the Rambam to rely on", and you may ignore it. "As always there's no tidy answer."

In questions he explained that the "gifts" Avraham gave the sons of the concubines are read by some Midrashim as occult knowledge, and that Yissakhar was gifted in astronomy, calculating the months, not astrology.

Summary

  • All forms of divination, consulting the dead and seeking the future are forbidden; we are to be tamim, wholehearted with God.
  • Consulting astrologers is forbidden by everyone; astronomy, though, is a true science.
  • The Rambam holds astrology, demons and spirits are false and forbids acting on such information; the Ramban, the Rema and the Vilna Gaon disagree.
  • The Shulhan Arukh does not decide; the Rabbi saw room to follow either view, depending on one's outlook.
