Topic: The history and purpose of prayer, and a walk through the structure of the Shabbat morning service Source: Berakhot; the Rambam, Hilchot Tefillah 1; Rabbi Yosef Albo Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Wednesday 22 February 2023 (Rosh Hodesh Adar), the second class in a short series on prayer

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where prayer comes from

Dayan Kada began by saying it is sad that many of us have prayed all our lives without knowing what we are doing or why. As in the previous class, he asked whether prayer comes from the Torah, the Rabbis, or custom.

The Talmud asks what the "service of the heart" in the Shema is, and answers: prayer. So the basis of prayer is biblical, and its essence is activity of the heart. "Unfortunately today it has become a service of the lips." If we are only going through the motions, we are missing something.

The Rambam draws two conclusions. First, the Torah fixes no number of prayers and no text: each person prays as well as he can, with praise, then requests, then thanks. Second, it fixes no time. That is why women are obligated in prayer, even though they are exempt from positive time-bound misvot such as sukkah and lulav. Hazal later tied the three daily prayers to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.

Why did the text become fixed? After the exile in Babylon, the Rambam says, the people's languages were mixed and no one could express himself well in any one tongue. Ezra and his court set the eighteen blessings so that the inarticulate would pray as completely as the eloquent. So the fixed prayer is not how things began, and not the ideal. Spontaneous prayer is. The word siddur comes from "seder", order. But the highest prayer is the one from the heart, and the best prayer is in one's own language.

Why do we pray?

Every prayer fits one of four categories:

  1. Adoration, such as Kadosh Kadosh Kadosh, Barukh Hashem LeOlam, and Hallel.
  2. Thanksgiving, such as the morning blessings, for the body's basic abilities.
  3. Penitence, asking forgiveness. This is the heart of Yom Kippur and also in the weekday prayers, though never on Shabbat.
  4. Petition, which fills most of the weekday Amidah: wisdom, healing, livelihood.

God does not need our adoration. When we say "Barukh Atah Hashem" we recognise that He is the source of all, from the same root as "berekhah", a flow. Thanking God also trains us to thank people.

Then the hard question that the medieval philosophers asked: if God is all-good and all-powerful, what is the purpose of asking? Are we trying to change His mind? Participants offered answers: we should do our part ourselves, we are praying for strength, there may be a bigger picture. He answered that prayer is not about changing God. "Prayer is about changing yourself."

He gave an analogy from Rabbi Yosef Albo, the fifteenth-century Spanish philosopher. A patient is told both legs must be amputated next month. He changes his diet and exercises, and returns: now only one leg. He tries harder: no operation, only medication. Then he is sent home. The doctor seems to keep changing his mind, but it is the patient who changed. In the same way, God does not change, but a person who prays and examines himself becomes a different person, and for that person things are different. Each petition becomes a question: "You give wisdom": am I doing all I can to become wise? "Heal us": am I doing all I can for my own health?

The very word "tefillah" comes from the root pey-lamed-lamed, which is also found in "pelilim", judges. At its root, prayer means self-scrutiny. A participant asked how this fits with God knowing in advance who we will become. He said that is a different question, about God's knowledge and free will, for another day.

Why Shabbat has no petition. The Shabbat service is longer than the weekday one, yet has almost no requests. The Hakhamim took them out because asking for something makes you feel what you lack, and on Shabbat "you live in the present". They made the Zemirot and the psalms longer instead, and the Amidah shorter. Asked about the priestly blessing, he explained that the kohanim bless us; we are not asking. Where there is no kohen, the verses are read only as a reminder of Birkat Kohanim.

The structure of the service

(The page numbers below are those of the siddur used in class.)

The morning blessings (page 108 to the second paragraph of 114). He said, perhaps to the surprise of many, that the service really starts here. These are what every man and woman should say every day: thanks for being able to be excused, to open our eyes, to walk, to have clothes, to be Jewish, to learn Torah. They take three or four minutes and can be a woman's daily prayer.

The sacrifices (from page 114). A reminder of the sacrifices of the past, and not the most important part. "The first thing people do when they come late to service is sacrifice the sacrifices," as one rabbi joked.

Zemirot (from page 136). Songs, mostly from the Book of Psalms, about fifteen of the 150. He compared them to a warm-up before a football match.

Nishmat (page 172). Said only on Shabbat and Yom Tov. Rubrics note that Shaharit begins here, so the men should aim to be present by Nishmat. Its author is unknown. One tradition, seen in the enlarged letters in many Ashkenazi siddurim, finds an acrostic of Yitzhak and Rivka in its central lines. Historians do not know who wrote it.

Barekhu. Until here the prayers could be said alone. Now the leader calls the community to join him in blessing God. This is "prayer proper", so a Kaddish comes before it.

The Shema and its blessings. The Shema has two blessings before it and one after, each with its theme: - Creation ("Yotser Hame'orot"): the sun, moon and stars, with the Kedushah, "Kadosh Kadosh Kadosh", in the middle as we are overwhelmed. It answers the dualism of the Zoroastrians of that age, with a god of light and a god of darkness: one God forms both. - Revelation ("Ahavat Olam", page 182): thanks for the Torah and a prayer to understand, teach and keep it. - Redemption (top of page 186, "Ga'al Yisrael"): the salvation from Egypt, and a request to redeem us once more.

A participant asked why we cover our eyes for the Shema. The simple reason is focus. The deeper reason is that "Hashem Ehad" means there is only one ultimate force, but the world does not look like that. He connected this to Purim, which was a few weeks away: God's name is not mentioned in the Megillah. You can read it as a string of coincidences, or see the hand of God behind them. We believe it but cannot see it, so we cover our eyes, and we say "Barukh shem kevod malkhuto" quietly. On Yom Kippur we say it aloud.

The Amidah

It has three names. Amidah, "standing", because it is the one part where one must stand. Shemoneh Esreh, the eighteen blessings, though on Shabbat there are only seven. And Tefillah Lahash, the silent prayer, learned from Hannah, whose lips moved while her voice was not heard. "The whole concept of silent prayer is learned from a woman." Sometimes the most powerful prayer is when you are silent. That is why it is such a shame when people talk during the Amidah: this is five minutes of silence, the heart of the service, "If you only got five minutes, this is what you say."

The seven blessings of Shabbat: 1. Avot, our ancestors and the shield of Abraham. 2. Gevurot, life, ending with the resurrection of the dead. 3. Kedushat HaShem, "You are holy". 4. The sanctity of Shabbat ("Yismah Moshe"), with no requests. 5. Retsei, "accept our prayer as You accepted the sacrifices of the past", ending with the return of the Shekhinah to Zion. We are not asking outright for the sacrifices to return, but hinting. 6. Hoda'ah, thanks for everything, ending with "Hatov Shimkha ulekha na'eh lehodot". 7. Shalom, peace.

The Amidah proper ends with "Hamevarekh et amo Yisrael bashalom"; the paragraph after it is an addition.

Questions: Amen after your own blessing

Someone asked why we say Amen at the end of the Amidah's last blessing, when one does not answer Amen to one's own blessing. Amen has two functions. It can affirm what someone else said. It can also mean "the set is finished", which is how we use it at the end of a set of blessings. Other examples are the end of the main part of Birkat HaMazon, "boneh Yerushalayim", and "Shomer Amo Yisrael La'ad" in Arvit.

Another participant asked why we do not say Amen after the fifteen morning blessings. His answer: they were never meant to be a set. Until about four hundred years ago each was said when the action happened, on hearing the rooster or getting dressed, and the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say they should be said only when you experience them. Later they were said in one go.

Summary

  • Prayer is a "service of the heart". The fixed text was added later to help us, and one's own words are still welcome.
  • Every prayer is adoration, thanksgiving, penitence or petition. Petition is meant to change the person praying, not God.
  • The service starts with morning blessings, the sacrifices, Zemirot and Nishmat, and begins in earnest at Barekhu with the Shema and its blessings on creation, revelation and redemption.
  • The Amidah is the climax. On Shabbat it has seven blessings and no petitions, because Shabbat is lived in the present.
  • Amen can affirm someone else's blessing or mark the end of a set.
