These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

Last time we saw that a father is believed to say "this is not my son" (Seif 29), with limits. Today the Rabbi took Seifim 26, 27 and 28 as one big sugya. Seif 26 is about a single woman (penuyah) who is pregnant. Seifim 27 and 28 are about a betrothed woman (arusah) who is pregnant: in Seif 27 we do not know that she had relations with her arus, in Seif 28 we do.

He said the order of the Shulhan Arukh is not the best way to understand it, so he taught Seif 28, then 27, then 26.

Three background rules

  1. A married woman who is seen with another man: the child is still presumed to follow the husband (rov biot ahar habaal). We do not have this rule for an arusah or a penuyah.
  2. A father is believed to say his child is a Mamzer (Seif 29), as we saw last time.
  3. A shetuki is a child whose mother we know but whose father we do not. Shmuel called a shetuki a beduki, "one who can be checked": we can ask the mother and rely on what she says. So a mother has a measure of neemanut, and today we see how far it goes. The Rabbi noted that today every single woman who becomes pregnant brings us into the realm of shetuki.

The Gemara: Rav and Shmuel

A man had relations with his arusah in her father's house. Rav says the child is a safek Mamzer. Shmuel says he is a shetuki, not a Mamzer. The Rabbi explained that Rav is speaking where there is a strong rumour (kol) that she goes with other men. Then the rule "follow the husband" does not apply, because they are not married. Shmuel agrees that the child is a safek Mamzer, but adds that he is a shetuki and so can be checked: if the mother says "I had relations with my arus", the child is kosher.

A student asked why we do not simply say that the child follows the arus, since we know they had relations. The Rabbi said that in matters of lineage (yuhasin) we need clearer evidence than the usual rules of probability.

Two versions of the Gemara. The Rabbi stressed that a lishna (version) shapes everything:

First version (lishna kama)

Second version (lishna batra)

To be kosher you need

Two good things: (a) it is known she had relations with her arus, and (b) there is no rumour about other men

Only one: it is known they had relations, even if there is a rumour about others

To be a safek Mamzer you need

Only one bad thing

Two bad things: no known relations with the arus, and a rumour of other men

Followed by

The Rambam, the Tur and the Shulhan Arukh (stricter)

The Rosh

The Rabbi noted that the Tur here follows the Rambam, not his father the Rosh, and the Shulhan Arukh follows the Tur. Everyone agrees that the halachah follows Shmuel, because other Gemaras (Ketubot and Kiddushin) support him. The Rishonim differ only on which version we accept.

A Gemara in Ketubot adds that where both agree the child is his, the child is kosher.

Seif 28: an arusah known to have been with her arus

Here the relations are known. The Shulhan Arukh says that if she is rumoured to be with other men too, the child is a safek Mamzer. She has one good thing, but not two. Unless she says "learus nivalti" ("I had relations with my arus"): then the child is kosher, as in "shetuki beduki". The Rabbi added that if there is no rumour about other men, the child is kosher even if she says nothing, because there are two good things.

If she says "from my arus", the arus cannot deny it here, since we know they were together, so his words change nothing.

Summary of Seif 28. The child is kosher in two cases: (a) there is no rumour about other men, or (b) even with a rumour, the mother says it was from the arus. If she is silent, because she cannot speak or does not know, and there is a rumour, the child is a safek Mamzer.

Seif 27: an arusah who just became pregnant

Here we do not know that they had relations, and she lives at home.

  • If the arus agrees, the child is kosher.
  • If the arus says nothing, is absent or does not remember, the child is still kosher, because "kediavad domeh" ("it is like a post-facto case"), says the Gemara. This is the Gemara's conclusion, drawing on Rabbi Yoseph.
  • If the arus contradicts her, saying "we never had relations", the child is a definite Mamzer. This is based on Seif 29: a person is believed to say "it is not my son". The Rabbi stressed this is not like the Bahag, and is a consequence of the father's neemanut.
  • If she says nothing, or says she does not know, and he is not before us to ask, the child is a safek Mamzer. This is the first version: we do not know they were together, so we do not have two good things. We go back to Rav.

What does kediavad mean? The Rabbi said the expression is strange, since a child who has not married is in a lehatehilah position: we could tell him who to marry. The Ran gives two explanations:

  1. The expression refers to the woman and to the child together. If we were to disqualify the child, we would cast doubt on the arusah, and then the arus could not live with her. They are only engaged, but the Ran sees this as a diavad situation. The Rabbi noted that in other contexts an engaged couple is still lehatehilah.
  2. The word is about the child only. The consequences for the child are much wider than for the woman, who would only be forbidden to a small group, so it is like a diavad.

Another view holds that it applies to the woman only, not to the child. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Ran, and the child is kosher according to both of his explanations.

The woman herself. If the arus denies, the child is a Mamzer, but he has no power to make her a zonah. She is not a hezkat zonah, and may marry a Kohen. But if the arus is himself a Kohen, he cannot take her back, since by his own words she is a zonah. The Rabbi stressed that he is believed for himself, but not for others. For everyone else, it is fine.

Seif 26: a single woman who is pregnant

This is the classical shetuki. The woman is single, she is pregnant, and she is not before us, or cannot speak.

  • If she says "it is from X" and X is kosher, the child is kosher. We can "check" the mother, as in shetuki beduki. This holds whether X agrees, keeps quiet, cannot be found, or cannot remember.
  • If she says "it is from X" and X is a Mamzer, she is not believed to make the child a definite Mamzer. The child stays a safek Mamzer. She keeps quiet: the same.

Why does the Gemara believe her for kosher but not for Mamzer? The Rabbi gave the explanation of the Ran, the Rabbenu Magid and others: a safek Mamzer is permitted to marry a kosher Jew by Torah law; only Hazal forbade it. So Hazal gave her neemanut to say "kosher", because it is a rabbinic matter. But if she made him a definite Mamzer, this has a Torah consequence, and Hazal did not give her neemanut for that. The Rabbi said the Beit Shemuel asks a strong question: it works both ways. A definite Mamzer by Torah law may marry a definite Mamzeret, and a safek Mamzer by Torah law may marry kosher. So why should she not be believed? He said the Perishah's answer may be that Hazal did not want to increase the number of Mamzerim in Israel (harbatat mamzerim), though the Beit Shemuel does not like this answer, and he left it open.

Other consequences. For inheritance she is not believed, even with one witness, since two are needed. But for marriage the child is treated as a relative of X, because we cannot accept her words and then let the child marry X's relatives.

The Rabbi's summary

  • The default status of a child whose father is unknown is a safek Mamzer, with no check.
  • We follow Shmuel: the mother may be checked. A single woman who says "from a kosher man" is believed, but she is not believed to say "from a Mamzer".
  • An arusah: it matters whether we know she was with her arus. If we know, the child is kosher, even if the mother is silent, unless there is a rumour of other men, and even then, if she says it was her arus, kosher.
  • If we do not know, and she says "my arus", she is believed. If she is silent, the child is kosher only if there is no rumour, and generally we need her to say something.

Practical summary

  • The first thing for a dayan is whether the mother can be asked. A mother who says "my arus" or "a kosher man" makes the child kosher.
  • A mother is not believed to say that the father is a Mamzer, and the child remains a safek Mamzer.
  • For an arusah, relations with the arus that are known, together with no rumour about others, make the child kosher without her saying anything.
  • If an arus denies, the child is a definite Mamzer, but the woman is not made a zonah, except for the arus himself if he is a Kohen.
  • The Shulhan Arukh follows the stricter first version, with the Rambam and the Tur, not the Rosh.
