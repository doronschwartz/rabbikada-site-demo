Se'if 5 – Ke-Volo Kach Polto: Matching the Kashering to the Absorption

Shulchan Aruch: The way a utensil absorbed chametz determines how it must be kashered.

How It Was Used

How to Kasher

Kli Rishon (cooked on fire, or off fire but still boiling — yad soledet bo)

Kasher in a Kli Rishon — boiling water on/from the fire

Irui Kli Rishon (hot liquid poured onto it from a pot)

Kasher by pouring from a Kli Rishon (irui)

Kli Sheni (received food transferred from a Kli Rishon)

Kasher in a Kli Sheni (or of course in a Kli Rishon)

Examples:

  • A ladle/spoon used to stir a pot on the fire → absorbed as Kli Rishon → must be kashered in a Kli Rishon.
  • A pot that was on the fire → to kasher, place it inside a bigger pot of boiling water on the fire.
  • A bowl that received soup poured from the pot → absorbed as irui Kli Rishon → kasher by pouring boiling water over it.

Does Kli Sheni Absorb at All?

Background: We hold (especially Sephardim) that Kli Sheni does not cook (ein Kli Sheni mevashel) — with very few exceptions (kalei ha-bishul: heavily salted fish, pure tea leaves). So why kasher something used only as a Kli Sheni?

Answer: The Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh De'ah (Siman 105) rules that although Kli Sheni doesn't cook, there are opinions that it can absorb and expel flavor. Since this is a machloket Rishonim, we are machmir le-chatchila (in the first instance) and kasher Kli Sheni items as well.

However: This is only le-chatchila. Be-di'avad — if you didn't kasher a Kli Sheni item and used it on Pesach — the food is fine. You can rely on the lenient opinions.

Kli Shelishi — No Concern at All

A third vessel (Kli Shelishi) — e.g., food served with a ladle (Kli Sheni) into a bowl (Kli Shelishi) — does not need kashering. Just wash it.

Practical scenario: If you always serve soup using a ladle (not pouring directly from pot to bowl):

Item

Status

Kashering Needed?

The pot

Kli Rishon

✅ Yes — hag'alah in Kli Rishon

The ladle

Kli Sheni (if plastic/non-conducting)

✅ Le-chatchila — in Kli Sheni or Rishon

The bowl

Kli Shelishi

❌ No — just wash

Spoons used in the bowl

Kli Shelishi

❌ No — just wash

Note on metal ladles: Some Poskim say a metal ladle sitting in a hot pot takes on the status of Kli Rishon (because metal conducts). This is itself a machloket. Combined with the doubt about whether Kli Sheni absorbs at all → safek sefeika (double doubt) → one can be lenient.

Kashering On vs. Off the Fire

  • Shulchan Aruch: A Kli Rishon can be kashered in another Kli Rishon, even off the fire (as long as it's still boiling).
  • Later Poskim (what we do in practice): If the utensil was used on the fire, it should ideally be kashered in water that is on the fire — not just boiling water that's been taken off.
  • In practice at communal hag'alah events: The fire is kept on underneath the pot throughout. At home: keep the stove burner on while kashering pots.

Kashering by Pouring (Irui) — Practical Rules for Sinks

Since sinks absorb chametz through irui (hot water poured from pots hits the sink walls), they are kashered by irui — pouring boiling water over them.

Two critical rules for effective irui:

Rule 1: Direct, unbroken stream (nitzok)

  • The boiling water must hit the surface in a direct, continuous stream — not splash, drip, or run down from another surface.
  • If you pour onto the walls and it runs down to the basin — that does not kasher the basin. You must pour directly onto the basin.
  • Practical order: Start with the base/basin of the sink (pour directly in circular motions), then work up the walls/sides.

Rule 2: The surface must be dry

  • Irui can only kasher a dry surface. If there is cold water pooled on the surface, the boiling water loses its ability to kasher upon contact with the cold water.
  • This is another reason to start from the base first — if you start from the walls, water runs down and pools at the base, which you'd then need to dry before kashering the base.

Se'if 7 – Ivory Utensils (Precious Materials)

Shulchan Aruch: Spoons or utensils made from ivory (keren) — cannot be kashered.

Why? Not because hag'alah doesn't work on ivory in principle, but because ivory is so precious and delicate that the owner won't do hag'alah properly — they won't heat the water high enough or dip it in fully, for fear of damaging it.

This is a Rabbinic concern, not from the Gemara — the Rishonim added this safeguard.

Modern application: Difficult to think of a parallel today. Any utensil so expensive you'd be afraid to boil properly? Possibly certain decorative items — but if you only use them cold, no kashering is needed anyway.

Se'if 8 – Materials That Can Be Kashered

Shulchan Aruch: The following materials can all be kashered normally (hag'alah or libun as appropriate):

  • Wood
  • Stone (including quartz)
  • Metal
  • Bone

Practical relevance: Quartz sinks and countertops — these are stone and can be kashered. People building kitchens who want to use the same sink for Pesach: quartz is fine, kasher it normally.

Se'if 10 – Baskets Used for Salting Meat: Ein Machzikin Issura

Shulchan Aruch: Baskets used for salting meat — two opinions on whether they need kashering:

Opinion

Reasoning

First opinion: Need hag'alah

Perhaps chametz crumbs were mixed in with the meat during salting. Heavy salting can absorb and extract even cold.

Second opinion (halacha): No kashering needed

Ein machzikin issura — we don't assume chametz is lurking where there's no evidence of it.

The Shulchan Aruch rules like the second opinion. If you know there was chametz, kasher it. If you don't know — don't assume it.

This is the same principle from Siman 447:5 — ein machzikin issura. Combined with chozer ve-ne'or (Class 8), these are the two reasons why plain ingredients before Pesach don't need a hechsher.

Plastic

Modern question: What is the status of plastic?

Opinion

View

Extreme opinion

Plastic = like glass (zechuchit). For Ashkenazim who can't kasher glass → can't kasher plastic either.

General consensus

Plastic either doesn't absorb at all (most logical scientifically), or it can be kashered normally.

In practice

We kasher plastic. It works. The opinion that it can't be kashered is not followed.

Se'if 11 – Frying Pans: The Rosh vs. the Rashba

Shulchan Aruch: A pan used for frying with oil — can be kashered with hag'alah.

If it's very long and doesn't fit in a pot → do hag'alah on the sides, libun in the middle section.

The Core Machloket:

Opinion

Ruling

Reasoning

The Rosh (Shulchan Aruch follows)

Hag'alah suffices for frying pans

Even though oil sometimes burns and dries up, there's always some moisture (lachluchit) remaining. As long as you cook with oil/liquid, it's considered wet absorption → hag'alah.

The Rashba (Rama follows)

Needs libun

Sometimes the oil dries completely, the food burns → those moments are dry absorption → needs libun.

The Rama's compromise: Because of the combination of (a) Rabbeinu Tam — chametz is hetera bala anyway, plus (b) the Rosh's opinion that hag'alah suffices → the Rama allows libun kal (a lighter form of libun = putting it in the oven at high temperature for about 30 minutes).

For Sephardim: Hag'alah suffices for regular frying pans. Full stop.

Teflon / Non-Stick Pans

The problem: The entire purpose of non-stick pans is to cook without liquid — minimal or no oil. This is genuinely dry cooking.

Approach

Reasoning

Strict (recommended)

Non-stick = truly dry → needs libun. Libun will likely destroy the pan. Better to put it away and buy a Pesach one.

Those who do hag'alah

Not necessarily wrong — because: (1) hetera bala (Rabbeinu Tam) in the background; (2) is it really 100% dry? Some oil is still used; (3) what counts as libun? Maybe oven suffices. Multiple leniencies combine.

Practical advice: Non-stick Teflon pans — put them away for Pesach. This is one of the few things worth buying new.

Oven Trays / Cake Tins

Mishnah Berurah (65): Baking trays (tavnit) — used completely dry in the oven — need libun to kasher. Cannot be kashered with hag'alah.

  • Libun at highest oven temperature may ruin them.
  • Practical advice: Put oven trays away and use Pesach ones, or line with foil.

Oil Temperature Question

Interesting question raised: Oil fries at much higher temperatures than 100°C (boiling water). Shouldn't we need hotter water to extract what went in through hot oil?

Answer: None of the Poskim ever raised this concern. In practice, we use boiling water for hag'alah regardless. The question deserves investigation scientifically, but halachically, hag'alah with boiling water has always been accepted for oil-based absorption.

Se'if 12 – Handles of Utensils

Shulchan Aruch: You must also kasher the handles of utensils — not just the cooking surface.

Why?

  • Metal handles: Metal conducts heat — forbidden flavor travels through the pot walls into the handle.
  • Non-metal handles (wood, etc.): The Mishnah Berurah asks: why? Perhaps hot chametz touches the handle during washing, or your chametz-covered hands grip it while cooking. Some concern of absorption.

The Rama says: If you didn't kasher the handles — be-di'avad, the pot and its contents are still fine.

His reasoning: The same heat that travels from the pot to the handle during cooking also travels during kashering — so the handle gets kashered automatically along with the pot.

In practice: We do kasher handles (le-chatchila). But if you missed them — no need to re-do.

Se'if 14 – Lids

Shulchan Aruch: Lids of pots must be kashered.

Why? The lid receives evaporation and steam (zei'ah) from the food cooking below. This steam carries chametz flavor and absorbs into the lid.

If you placed an un-kashered lid on a Pesach pot on Pesach:

Timing

Consequence

On Pesach itself

All contents of the pot are forbidden — because on Pesach, even a drop of chametz is be-mashehu (no bitul)

On Erev Pesach after chatzot

Fine — if there's 60 times more food than the absorbed flavor (likely). Everyone agrees 1-in-60 works from chatzot until Pesach itself.

Lid not used for 24 hours

Sephardim: noten ta'am li-fgam — be-di'avad, the food is permitted.

Se'if 20 – The Origin of Covering Surfaces

Shulchan Aruch: Tables and storage boxes/cupboards where food is kept all year round — we pour hot water (irui) onto them to kasher, because sometimes soup spills from a pot onto the table.

Key insight: If you never bring pots to the table (only bowls — which are Kli Sheni), there is no need to kasher the table at all. A spill from a Kli Sheni onto the table is not a concern.

Where Does "Covering" Come From?

Magen Avraham: Even after kashering the table, put a tablecloth on it — perhaps some chametz remains.

The Rama and Eliyahu Rabbah disagree: For flat tables — not necessary. Just kasher it.

Eliyahu Rabbah (cited in Mishnah Berurah): For cupboards, shelves, fridge, freezer — where food sits directly on surfaces and it's very hard to clean every trace — cover them.

This is the origin of the entire practice of covering surfaces for Pesach.

Modern Application

Surface

What's Needed

Dining table

If pots come to the table: kasher with irui (or cover). If only bowls/plates: just wash.

Kitchen counters

Kasher with irui (hot spills from Kli Rishon likely during the year). Or cover.

Fridge / freezer shelves

If you place unwrapped food directly on them: cover the shelves. If all food is wrapped/packaged: no need to cover — just wash well.

Cupboard shelves

Same as fridge: cover if unwrapped food sits on them; wash if everything is packaged.

Important: Nowadays we have extensive plastic packaging. The old concern was about unwrapped food sitting directly on surfaces. If your food is packaged, much of the covering is unnecessary.

Se'if 22 – Cold Chametz and the Two Exceptions

Shulchan Aruch: All utensils — even earthenware — that were used only with cold chametz throughout the year → can be used on Pesach for even hot food. Just wash them.

Two exceptions where cold chametz still creates a problem:

Exception 1: Beit Se'or (Place of Extended Contact)

  • If chametz dough sat on a surface for 24 consecutive hours (me'et le'et) — it absorbs even though it's cold.
  • This is the principle of kavush ke-mevushal — prolonged soaking/contact is equivalent to cooking.
  • Example: A kneading board where dough sits overnight.

Exception 2: Beit Charoset (Sharp Ingredients)

  • If chametz was used together with sharp ingredients (davar charif) — garlic, onion, ginger — even cold, even without 24 hours.
  • Sharp foods have the ability to cause absorption and extraction even when cold.
  • Example: Cutting onions and bread together with a knife.

Even with these exceptions:

  • The utensil can be kashered (hag'alah for non-earthenware).
  • Even without kashering — it can be used for cold food on a one-off (temporary) basis.
  • Earthenware with these issues: cannot be kashered → don't use on Pesach.

Practical example: A knife used to cut onions on bread (cold, but sharp ingredient + chametz together) → needs hag'alah. A knife used to cut bread alone (cold, no sharp) → just wash it.

Se'if 23 – Glazed Earthenware (China)

Shulchan Aruch: Earthenware vessels covered with a glass glaze — have the status of earthenware (cannot be kashered).

Why? Even though glass doesn't absorb, when the glass glaze is fired together with the earthenware during manufacturing, it becomes porous along with the clay. The glaze doesn't provide independent protection.

Critical distinction — glass on ovens/hobs:

Scenario

Status

Kashering?

Glazed china (glass fused with clay during manufacturing)

= Earthenware

❌ Cannot be kashered

Glass cooktop / induction hob (separate glass panel on top)

= Glass

✅ Just clean well with soap — no kashering needed

Oven door glass (separate glass panel)

= Glass

✅ Just clean — no kashering needed

This is a very common misconception. People think they need to kasher their glass induction hob or oven glass. You don't — it's glass, it's a separate item, not glazed earthenware. Just clean it thoroughly.

Se'if 25 – Drinking Cups and Mugs

Shulchan Aruch: All drinking vessels — whether glass, wood, metal, or even earthenware — can be used on Pesach just by washing. No kashering needed.

Even if during the year, hot chametz was occasionally placed in them (e.g., a child poured hot soup with croutons into a cup).

Why? Because the rov tashmisho (majority use) of cups and drinking vessels is with cold liquids. We follow the majority.

Modern application — mugs:

Usage Pattern

Kashering Needed?

Used for coffee/tea throughout the year; once or twice had hot soup with chametz

❌ No — just wash. Rov tashmisho is cold/non-chametz drinks.

Regularly used for porridge, hot chametz soups, etc.

✅ Yes — needs hag'alah (rov tashmisho is hot chametz)

Used only for cold water and cold drinks

❌ No — just wash

The Rav's emphasis: This saves significant money and effort. People buy new cups and glasses for Pesach unnecessarily. If you understand rov tashmisho, most drinking vessels in your home need nothing more than a good wash.

Se'if 26 – Glass (זכוכית)

Famous machloket:

Opinion

Ruling

Reasoning

Shulchan Aruch (Sephardim)

Glass does not absorb at all. Even used with hot chametz all year → just wash and use on Pesach.

Glass is non-porous — it doesn't take in flavors.

Rama (Ashkenazim)

Glass is like earthenware — so porous it can't even be kashered with hag'alah. Cannot be used on Pesach if used with chametz.

Glass is a form of sand/earth — similar to clay.

For Sephardim: Glass is the ultimate solution. A set of glass dishes, cups, and cookware can be used for meat, milk, and Pesach — year-round. Just wash between uses. No kashering, no separate sets needed.

Practical tip from the Rav: For people who struggle with the expense of multiple sets of dishes — invest in glass. One set works for everything: meat, dairy, and Pesach. This is fully halachically valid for Sephardim.
