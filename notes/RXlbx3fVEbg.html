These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Two separate prohibitions

Following the order of Friday night: we made Kiddush, ate, and sat down to read by the candles. Now there is not enough light and a non-Jew lights another lamp. Siman 276 uses light as the example, but its principles apply to every melakhah a non-Jew does for a Jew.

There is a common mistake: "if I only hint, I can have anything done." Hinting avoids one prohibition, amirah, instructing a non-Jew to do melakhah. But there is a second rabbinic prohibition, hanaah: benefiting from melakhah a non-Jew did for a Jew. If he did it for himself or for other non-Jews, you may benefit as much as you like. If he did it for a Jew, you may not, even if nobody said a word.

So before relying on a hint, ask two questions. Am I telling him? Am I benefiting?

What counts as benefit. The Hakhamim forbade going from nothing to something. Adding to what you already have is not benefit in this sense. Removing something that bothers you, such as switching off a light so you can sleep, is not benefit either. Benefit means direct, physical gain from what he actually did.

  • Power cut, but the Shabbat candles are lit: you may hint for the electricity to be switched back on.
  • The heating is on at 19 degrees and you would like 22: you may hint.
  • The house is completely dark, with no light from outside: even if you only hint and he switches it on, you may not use that light.

When you may ask outright

Rabbinic melakhah may be requested explicitly in three cases: for a misvah, to prevent a great loss, or for someone slightly unwell.

  • Your £400 coat is hanging on a tree branch and rain is coming. Taking something off a tree is rabbinic, so you may ask a non-Jew to fetch it. If it is in the park and there is no eruv, carrying it home would be Torah-level: "You have to say bye to your 400 pound coat."
  • On Rosh Hashanah the shofar is stuck up a tree. A non-Jew may be asked to climb up for it. A Jew may not, even with a thousand people waiting to hear the shofar.

Torah-level melakhah may only be requested for someone ill but not in danger, meaning bedridden, for example with flu or a stomach bug. A non-Jew may be asked to drive to buy medicine or boil the kettle.

Oneg Shabbat alone never permits asking for Torah-level melakhah.

Electric lights. The Rabbi said that modern lights are rabbinic; only filament bulbs that glow red-hot are Torah-level. So if the lights go out in shul on Friday night, a non-Jew may be asked outright, because prayer is a misvah. At home, if it is pitch dark, there is usually some misvah involved (eating the Shabbat meal, learning with the children), so it is usually permitted. Just to read the newspaper, no. He rejected the argument that sitting in the dark spoils oneg Shabbat: if that worked, "you can throw away everything."

He stressed how important it is for a community to have arrangements for a non-Jew to help on Shabbat, as in some communities in London, America and Israel.

Seif 1: what a non-Jew did for a Jew

If a non-Jew lit a lamp for a Jew, no Jew may use its light, not even someone it was not lit for. This applies whether he is paid by the hour or by the job. The Mishnah Berurah says candles are only the example: this is true of every melakhah.

Children. The Rema adds that a child, under bar or bat misvah, has the status of a sick person. A child who says "I'm cold" or "I'm too hot" already counts as ill, and a non-Jew may be asked outright to turn on the heating or air conditioning. A very elderly person is the same.

Seif 2: who was it done for?

If Jews and non-Jews are sitting together and a non-Jew lights a candle: - Most of them non-Jews: permitted. - Half and half, or most of them Jews: forbidden, since we assume it was done for the Jews. - If he clearly did it for himself, for example he is giving a speech and wants more light, even a hall full of Jews may benefit.

The Itur. The Rema brings a minority view of the Baal HaItur: for a misvah one may ask a non-Jew to do even Torah-level melakhah, and many relied on it for lights at a communal Shabbat meal or a Shabbat Hatan. The Rema says not to rely on it except in great need. Most poskim agree it may be relied on for a whole community, for example to repair the eruv when it is down, since otherwise many people would desecrate Shabbat.

The Rabbi gave examples. An ordinary Friday night in shul with filament bulbs: no; go home and pray. Yom Kippur night, with people who come once a year: he would be inclined to permit. It depends on the community, which is why a ruling has to fit the person asking. "Rav Moshe Feinstein always said: the fifth section of the Shulhan Arukh, which you don't learn in yeshivah, is common sense."

Seif 4: adding to existing light

If there was already light in the house and a non-Jew lit another candle, you may use the second light as long as the first is still burning. If he added oil to a lamp, you may use it for as long as the original oil would have lasted. Once the first light goes out, you may not use the second, because now you are benefiting directly from what he did.

Seif 5: cold

The Shulhan Arukh lived in Safed, but in cold countries a non-Jew may be told to make a fire for the children, and for adults too when it is very cold, because "everyone is ill with regard to cold."

Do not wait until you are in bed. "Bedridden" means a situation that, if left, will put you in bed. Someone who gets severe migraines from fasting should eat as soon as the feeling starts, not wait until the migraine has taken hold. The same applies to cold.

A locked door. A child locked in a toilet: even a Jew may break the door, as the Gemara in Shabbat says. An adult with no particular condition should wait. If there is a known anxiety condition, that is a medical question to weigh up.

How cold is cold? The Magen Avraham and the Taz debated this in Europe. The Mishnah Berurah is strict and requires real, freezing cold, though it depends, for instance on whether warm clothes are available. The Rabbi added that people today live with heating and cannot bear cold as people once did, so the bar is lower; he would argue that about 9 or 10 degrees inside the house already qualifies.

Practical summary

  • Hinting avoids instructing, but you still may not benefit from melakhah a non-Jew did for you.
  • Adding to what you already have (more light, warmer heating) is not "benefit": you may hint.
  • You may ask outright for rabbinic melakhah for a misvah, a great loss or a minor illness; for Torah-level melakhah only for someone sick in bed, a child, or real cold.
  • Modern lights are rabbinic; when the lights go out at a Shabbat meal or in shul, a non-Jew may usually be asked.
  • If a non-Jew clearly did something for himself, anyone may benefit.
