Topic: The three misvot concerning Amalek, and why the Torah is so severe with Amalek

Sources: Parashat Ki Tesse; Parashat Beshalah with Rashi; the Gemara (the three misvot on entering the land); the book of Shemuel (Shaul and Agag)

Daily Halacha: Purim, Tuesday 12 March 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • To understand Purim we need its context. There are three misvot about Amalek at the end of Parashat Ki Tesse: Zakhor, to remember what Amalek did; Lo Tishkah, not to forget; and, when we are able, to wipe out the remembrance of Amalek.
  • The Torah is sharper with Amalek than with Egypt, who enslaved us for years, because Amalek is the root of evil in the world. Amalek introduced what we now call antisemitism: right after we left Egypt, before we had become a nation, they attacked us without provocation. Their very existence expresses hatred of Israel, of Torah and of the world becoming a better place.
  • Hence "Ki yad al kes Yah": Rashi explains that the throne (kes, without an aleph) and the Name (Yah, only yud and hey) are incomplete until Amalek is blotted out.
  • The misvot normally cultivate middot tovot such as kindness and compassion, so one might think we should forgive Amalek. But being extreme in compassion can be wrong: without balance it allows those who want to annihilate us to survive.
  • The Gemara says that on entering the land Israel had three misvot: to appoint a king, to wipe out Amalek and to build the Beit HaMikdash. So Shemuel told Shaul to destroy Amalek entirely, but Shaul had pity on King Agag and the livestock and lost his kingdom.
  • Because of this weakness, Amalekites survived. They later attacked Siklag and took David's people captive, and even as king David could not destroy them, as they were scattered.
  • The Hakhamim say Haman descended from Agag: because Shaul spared Agag, Haman was born generations later and sought to annihilate the Jewish people. When we see what is happening in the world, we must remember that too much compassion can allow further attacks.

In one line

Amalek is the root of hatred of Israel, and Shaul's misplaced compassion for Agag led to Haman, a lesson that compassion without balance can be dangerous.
