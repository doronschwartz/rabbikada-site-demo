Topic: The story of Hanukkah, lighting and its blessings, and the laws of Hallel Source: The Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Megillah VaHanukkah, Chapter 3 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at his Thursday-night halacha class, Thursday 11 December 2025, three days before Hanukkah These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why the Rambam this year

Last year the class learnt the laws of Hanukkah in the Shulhan Arukh; this year the Rabbi turned to the Rambam. Sefer Zemanim, the second of the Mishneh Torah's fourteen books, ends with Hilchot Megillah VaHanukkah: four chapters, the first two on Purim and the last two on Hanukkah. The class learnt chapter 3.

The story behind the festival (halachot 1-2)

The Rambam opens with history, and the Rabbi stressed why: you cannot understand the halachot without knowing what Hanukkah is about, and many people who celebrate it cannot say when or why it happened. In Second Temple times "the kings of Greece" made decrees against Israel. The plural matters: there was more than one Antiochus, and the struggle went back and forth over many years. A Midrash says children were given drinking horns inscribed "you have no portion in the God of Israel". They barred Torah and misvot (Shabbat, Rosh Hodesh and milah above all, as the archetypes), laid hands on Jewish property and daughters, entered the Temple and defiled what was pure. Then the Hashmonaim, the sons of the high priest, defeated them, appointed a king from among themselves, and Jewish rule returned for more than two hundred years, until the destruction. The events were around 165 BCE. What the Greeks wanted. The persecution was of religion more than of Jews. Why did the Greeks defile the jars of oil rather than simply smash them? Because they were happy to keep the Temple and the culture, "but it has to be done our way." For a philosophy of the tangible, purity and impurity mean nothing. The Hashmonean kings. The Ramban in Bereshit criticises the Hashmonaim for taking the kingship, which belongs to the tribe of Yehudah. The Rambam presents it favourably, with no word of criticism. The oil, and the eighth day. When they won, on the twenty-fifth of Kislev, they entered the Temple and found one sealed flask of pure oil, which lasted eight days until new oil could be pressed and brought. Ask nine people out of ten and they will say Hanukkah is about the oil. That is true, but for the Rambam the essence is the military victory and the return of sovereignty; the oil comes second. Most Rishonim say the fighting ended on the twenty-fourth and they rested on the twenty-fifth: "Hanu-kah", they rested on the 25th. The Rambam says the war ended on the twenty-fifth itself. The Pri Hadash asks: then they may have lit only on the night of the twenty-sixth, so why does Hanukkah begin on the twenty-fifth? He answers that this also solves the famous question of why eight days when the miracle was only seven: the first day marks the victory and the next seven mark the oil. For the Rambam, "Hanukkah" simply means rededication.

Days of joy and praise (halachot 3-4)

The Sages made these eight days "days of joy and Hallel", and lighting in the doorways each night, to publicise the miracle, is part of that. Only then does the Rambam give the name: "these days are called Hanukkah". Eulogies and fasting are forbidden, as on Purim; at a funeral the eulogy should be kept short. The Rabbi drew out the order. Before the name and before the candles, the Rambam calls them days of joy and praise. Hanukkah is not a Hag, but the joy is intrinsic to the days, perhaps for the victory and the Hallel for the miracle of the oil. So "Hanukkah Sameah" is a fitting greeting. This is also why the Rambam sets all the laws of Hallel here rather than under Pesah or Sukkot. Who lights. Whoever is obligated to read the Megillah is obligated to light. The Rambam holds men and women equally obligated in the Megillah, so a husband or a wife may equally light for the household and say the blessings. If the husband will be home very late, it is right for the wife to light rather than wait. The blessings. The one lighting says three blessings on the first night and two afterwards. The Gemara asks how we can say "who commanded us" for a rabbinic misvah, and answers from "do not turn aside" from the words of the Sages. One who sees the lights says two blessings on the first night and one on the others. For the Rambam this applies even if your family lights for you at home: a student in university who passes a menorah in a shul or a Chabad house would say the blessing. The Rashba limits it to someone who has not lit, will not light and has no one lighting for him, and because of the doubt the Shulhan Arukh follows him. That case is rare: someone alone in a hotel where he cannot light.

The laws of Hallel (halachot 5-9)

All eight days of Hanukkah one reads the full Hallel with a blessing, alone or with a congregation. It is rabbinic, but a blessing is said on a definite rabbinic misvah, like the Megillah or an eruv. On something instituted out of doubt, like tithing demai, no blessing is said, although the Sages required blessings on the second day of Yom Tov so it would not be treated lightly. The Rambam stresses that all Hallel is rabbinic, even on the Torah festivals. He is excluding the Behag and others who saw it as a Torah misvah, learnt from the verse in Yeshayahu, "the song shall be for you as on the night the festival is sanctified". The Rambam does not count a misvah that comes only from the Prophets. Eighteen or twenty-one days. The full Hallel is read on eighteen days in Israel: eight of Sukkot with Shemini Aseret, eight of Hanukkah, the first day of Pesah and Shavuot. Outside Israel it is twenty-one. Not on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, days of repentance and awe; there is joy on Rosh Hashanah, but not the special joy of the festivals. Not on Purim, because the Megillah is its Hallel; someone with no Megillah would say Hallel without a blessing. Rosh Hodesh. Hallel on Rosh Hodesh is a custom, not an obligation. The Gemara tells how Rav, visiting Babylonia, saw them saying it and wanted to stop them, until he saw they skipped parts and realised it was a custom. So it is said in shortened form, and an individual praying alone does not say it, though if he began he may finish. The intermediate and last days of Pesah are treated the same way. Does a custom get a blessing? The Rambam says never, as the Gemara says of beating the aravah. Tosafot say a lengthy recital does, even alone. He said he had no proof, but was "almost certain". On Hanukkah the blessing is said even alone, because it is not a custom. Since Rosh Hodesh Hallel is not an obligation, someone who must rush to work may say it later or leave it out. When he leads, following a responsum of the Hida, he says the blessing aloud for everyone to answer amen. How it is said. Hallel may be said any time of day, in order, and a break in the middle does not spoil it. The Rambam, following the Gemara, allows talking between the psalms; other Rishonim compare it to Shema, and the Shulhan Arukh rules strictly, though in an urgent case there is what to rely on. Repeating the closing verses is an old custom, and if the hazan says a verse once and the congregation once, that is fine. In the Gemara's time the leader said the blessing and the text, and the congregation answered "Halleluyah", 123 times, the years of Aharon's life, repeating the opening verse of each psalm. If a woman or a child leads, one must repeat every word. At first it bothered the Rabbi that his congregation only listened and sang certain parts; then he realised this was the original practice. The Rambam ends by noting that each place follows its own custom. The class ran out of time before chapter 4, which he hoped to teach during Hanukkah.

Practical summary

  • For the Rambam the heart of Hanukkah is the victory and the return of Jewish rule, and the days are defined by joy and praise; "Hanukkah Sameah" is a fitting greeting.
  • Men and women are equally obligated in lighting; if the husband will be late, the wife should light for the family.
  • Today, follow the Shulhan Arukh: say the blessings on seeing a menorah only if you have not lit, will not light and no one is lighting for you.
  • On Hanukkah say the full Hallel with a blessing, even alone.
  • Most Sefaradim say no blessing on Rosh Hodesh Hallel; in the Rabbi's view, those whose custom is to say it in shul should not say it at home alone.
  • Avoid talking during Hallel, as the Shulhan Arukh rules; in an urgent case there is room to be lenient between psalms.
