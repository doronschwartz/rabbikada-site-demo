The question from the Gemara

The last shiur of the series covered the principles of Shevut (rabbinic prohibitions): when we may be lenient and when not. The source is the Gemara about a Brit Milah where the hot water was spilled. They proposed asking a non-Jew to bring more, although there was no proper eruv, so he would carry in violation of a rabbinic prohibition.

The Gemara objects: sprinkling the purification water on Shabbat is forbidden rabbinically, even to purify someone, so why may we tell a non-Jew to do a rabbinic act for a misvah? The answer: "Is there no difference between a rabbinic prohibition with an act and one without an act?" And then: "We never told him to heat the water, only to carry."

The two statements pull in different directions. The first suggests the distinction is who acts: the Jew himself may not do a rabbinic act for a misvah, but telling a non-Jew is speech only. The second suggests we may not tell a non-Jew to do a Torah prohibition such as heating water. A reminder from the Rabbi: telling a non-Jew to do something is itself forbidden only rabbinically, even if the act is a Torah prohibition.

Three opinions in the Rishonim

  • The Rif, the Rambam and the Rosh read "act" to mean a melakhah. A Jew may tell a non-Jew to do a rabbinic act for a misvah, but never a Torah prohibition, even for a misvah. This is the principle of Shevut DeShevut BeMakom Misvah: a double rabbinic rule. The Rambam adds that it applies for even a minor illness, or a pressing need. His examples: climbing a tree, swimming, bringing a shofar or a knife for the Milah.
  • The Baal HaHalakhot Gedolot, the Geonim and the Baal HaItur (quoted by the Ran) have a text of the Gemara without the last sentence. For them the distinction is only who acts. A non-Jew may be told to do even a Torah prohibition for a misvah.
  • Tosafot is the strictest: a non-Jew may be told to do a rabbinic act only for something as great as Brit Milah, which itself overrides Shabbat. Even so, the Rabbi explained, the preparations for the Milah do not override Shabbat. That is why the knife should be prepared before Shabbat. A student asked whether Tosafot considers any other misvah as great; the Rabbi said only Milah.

What the Shulhan Arukh rules

Surprisingly, Maran follows the three pillars, Rif, Rambam and Rosh, as his main opinion and permits telling a non-Jew to do a rabbinic act for a misvah, or when there is illness or great need. But he then brings Tosafot as "some forbid". Later, in the laws of Rosh Hashanah, he permits getting a shofar this way and does not mention Tosafot, so the poskim say the "some forbid" was out of respect only.

What is "great need"? Maran elsewhere calls it "tarud venihpaz alav": so pressing that you could not relax on Shabbat. That alone allows a double rabbinic act. But under the Shulhan Arukh you may not tell a non-Jew to do a Torah prohibition, even for a misvah.

The Rema and the synagogue

The Rema quotes the Baal HaItur: some allow telling a non-Jew to do a Torah prohibition for a misvah, and many are lenient. But he himself says it should be relied on only with a big need. The Mishnah Berurah gives an example: the eruv of the town falls on Shabbat. A Jew may not repair it, but a non-Jew may be told to, even though repairing is a Torah act, because the public need is great.

Hazon Ovadia, though he generally follows the Shulhan Arukh, concedes here to the Mishnah Berurah: for a public misvah one may rely on the lenient opinion. This is why in most synagogues a non-Jewish caretaker is told explicitly to switch on lights, heating or air conditioning.

But the Rabbi stressed this is not the simple reading: the Rambam, the Rif and the Rosh would not allow it, even to prepare for a Brit Milah. He pictured Kol Nidrei in a packed synagogue when the lights fail. "Thank God we have a diversity of opinions and we have the Itur to rely on."

Two rabbinic prohibitions done by the Jew

A student asked whether Shinui matters here. The Rabbi answered that the double rabbinic rule does not apply only to a non-Jew: a Jew may do two rabbinic prohibitions himself for a misvah or an illness.

Examples. Carrying a siddur through a karmelit, where there is no eruv, in an unusual way: carrying in a karmelit is rabbinic, and carrying with a Shinui is rabbinic. So too carrying paracetamol for someone with a headache. At a Friday night meal where the lights went out, with LED lights, you may even ask a non-Jew directly to switch them on, since that is one rabbinic rule for the telling and another for the act.

A question. Why may you not pull a heavy bench over the grass for a misvah, when it will inevitably make holes? That seems to involve several rabbinic rules: plowing is done in an unusual way, destructively, and without intention. The answer was that all the reasons of melekhet mahshevet (an unusual way, a destructive act, no intention) count as one single rabbinic prohibition and do not add up. Two rabbinic rules are needed from different categories: one from melekhet mahshevet, and one in the melakhah itself. Trapping a mosquito in an unusual way is an example: it is not normally trapped, and the method is unusual, so for a misvah (such as stopping it disturbing people learning) it may be permitted.

Ben HaShemashot

The Mishnah we say every Friday night allows rabbinic acts at twilight (Ben HaShemashot) for a misvah. At that time you may tell a non-Jew to do even a Torah prohibition, and the Jew himself may do a rabbinic one: for instance making an eruv, if he could not before Shabbat. The Rabbi stressed that the Jew may do this himself only if he has not publicly accepted Shabbat (for instance in the synagogue). Telling a non-Jew remains allowed.

What about Motsaei Shabbat? The Magen Avraham (400 years ago) held that the leniency applies only on Friday evening: when going out of Shabbat you are already in a "Hezkat Shabbat", a Shabbat mode, so you may not do a rabbinic act. The Rambam does not say so, and the Shulhan Arukh repeats the halakhah at the end of the laws of Shabbat, where in simplicity he means Motsaei Shabbat: a Jew may climb a tree to bring a lulav or shofar. So Hazon Ovadia and the Aharonim rule it is permitted in both directions.

Examples. A large sum of money left in the street, which would worry you all Shabbat: with an eruv, bringing it in is only rabbinic, and at Ben HaShemashot it may be allowed (not a ruling for practice). And the shofar after Yom Kippur: since blowing an instrument is rabbinic, and there is a misvah (as a memorial), it may be blown a little before the end of Yom Kippur, as many Sefaradi synagogues did.

Tosefet Shabbat. The same applies to the added time before Shabbat. In addition, you may tell a Jew who has not yet accepted Shabbat to do anything, even a Torah prohibition. For example, someone who realises the hot plate is off may ask a neighbour who has not yet accepted Shabbat to switch it on.

Other principles, briefly

Kevod HaBeriyot (human dignity). A rabbi comes to address a large community and finds a large visible stain on his jacket. Normally telling a non-Jew to wash it with water would be forbidden, but for the sake of dignity we allow a non-Jew to clean it. Tsaar Gadol (great pain) also allows rabbinic prohibitions.

In reply to questions. Relying on the Itur: do not put yourself in such a situation, but if you must rely on it, it is a heter. Praying Arvit early on Motsaei Shabbat does not end Shabbat; the Shulhan Arukh says one may pray Arvit and make Havdalah from Plag HaMinhah when needed, for instance before a journey straight after Shabbat.

Why does "Hezkat Shabbat" matter? A status quo is harder to change than to begin. The Magen Avraham's principle is a good one, but it seems not accepted by the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh.

Practical summary

  • A Jew may tell a non-Jew to do a rabbinic prohibition for a misvah, an illness or a pressing need (Shevut DeShevut).
  • The Shulhan Arukh does not allow telling a non-Jew to do a Torah prohibition, even for a misvah. For a public misvah, as in the synagogue, we rely on the Itur, but those who follow the Rambam would not.
  • A Jew may himself do two rabbinic prohibitions of different types, such as carrying in a karmelit in an unusual way. Reasons that all come from melekhet mahshevet count as only one.
  • At Ben HaShemashot, on Friday evening and Motsaei Shabbat, one may do a rabbinic act for a misvah or a great worry, if Shabbat was not publicly accepted.
  • Human dignity and great pain also allow rabbinic prohibitions.
