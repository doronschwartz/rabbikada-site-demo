Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 4, Seifim 10-14 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 27 December 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi recalled the earlier classes. We had finished the discussion of Kohanim and a rumour about a family's past: by itself it is not enough to create a safek, but together with other substantial factors it can. Then we began Siman 4 and the Torah prohibitions on Egyptians, Edomites, Ammonites and Moabites. The point that matters today is that a child of a non-Jewish father and a Jewish mother is Jewish. The Rif and the Rambam add that a daughter should not marry a Kohen, but if she did, she may stay with him, since it is only a safek.

Today he finished Seif 10, covered the two seifim on a slave, and began the long discussion of the Mamzer.

Seif 10: the old nations no longer matter

The Gemara says that since Sancheriv mixed up the nations, anyone who joins the Jewish people is assumed to come from the majority, who are not Ammonite, Moabite or Edomite. For Ammon and Moab the Torah prohibition only applies to a definite case, so a safek is already permitted. On Egypt there is a mahloket. The Rambam says the Egyptians are mixed up too. The Rosh says the prohibition still applies and one must wait three generations. The Beit Shemuel explains the Rosh: the prophet Yehezkel says Egypt will be resettled after 40 years. The Rabbi said that today the halachah follows the Rambam. Nobody knows who is Egyptian, so any convert may marry into the Jewish people.

Seifim 11-12: a slave

A slave (eved) is a quasi Jew. He may not marry a Jewish woman, and a Jewish man may not marry a shifhah. There is a mahloket on whether this is Torah or rabbinic law, and the Rabbi noted that the sources are not simple. Once freed, the slave is a full Jew.

When the master does things that suggest he freed him, such as giving him an aliyah, putting tefillin on him, or marrying him to a Jewish woman, the Gemara says "he has gone out to freedom". The Rosh and the Ramah (Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia) differ:

The Shulhan Arukh follows the Ramah. For money the evidence is enough: he no longer has to work for his master. For kiddushin it is only a safek. The Tur adds that it is the same when the slave does these things in front of his master, who stays silent. The Shulhan Arukh brings this as a single view, so we are concerned for the kiddushin.

Seif 13: the child of a niddah

Now the Mamzer. We follow Shimon HaTemani: a Mamzer is created only by a union that carries karet or the death penalty of Beit Din, such as incest or adultery.

Why is a niddah different? A niddah carries karet too, so every such child should be a Mamzer. The Rabbi said we can imagine what our communities would look like if it were so. The Gemara learns from the verses at the end of Kedoshim that a niddah does not create a Mamzer. But the child is pagum, "blemished".

What does pagum mean? Some understand it as a prohibition to marry a Kohen. Most, including the Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel, understand it as a spiritual blemish, not a prohibition. The Rabbi compared it to the advice in Siman 2, which is not binding but is still recorded.

The Steipler's letter. A father was happy with a young man who was a talmid hakham, but worried that he was born to parents before they became observant. The Steipler answered that pagum means a propensity to a blemished life. If the young man has shown he has risen above it, there is no concern. The Rabbi said this is the practice today: a good person is not a concern. If there is another doubt on top of it, one can think again.

Seif 14: a wife whose husband was abroad

There were no airplanes then: a man who returned by sea could not come in secret.

Keep three categories apart. A Torah Mamzer may marry only a Mamzeret or a convert. A safek Mamzer is in some ways worse off, because his status is unresolved, but leniencies exist later in the Siman. A rabbinic Mamzer is a definite status, which is rare today.

Section 1: born after 12 months

The husband has been away more than 12 months. The Rambam says the child is a Mamzer. The Bahag says he is not. The Beit Shemuel understands the Bahag as saying he is a fully kosher child. The Shulhan Arukh's wording can mean a safek, so he is concerned for the Bahag and treats the child as a safek Mamzer. The Bahag's reason is that the husband may have come secretly and left again.

The Beit Yoseph tries to reconcile them. Here is the Rabbi's summary:

  • If the husband is present and says "I never came home", everyone agrees the child is a Mamzer, because a father is believed to say his child is a Mamzer.
  • If he says it is his child and she is silent, the child is kosher.
  • If the husband is absent, the Beit Yoseph suggests the Rambam only spoke of a present, silent husband. But the Shulhan Arukh seems to present a real mahloket, and the Rambam seems to say the child is a Mamzer even if the husband is absent.

Today. The Bahag is much stronger now, since people can fly home for a night unnoticed, though everything is also tracked digitally. A dayan could ask the husband by email, but the Rabbi thought he almost certainly need not investigate. The Hida (in the Pithei Teshuvah) adds that a birth at 12 months and up to three days is kosher even for the Rambam, since conception can take up to three days.

Section 2: within 12 months

A birth within 12 months is fine, because Rabba Tosfaah said a baby can stay in the womb for up to 12 months. The Rif, the Rambam and the Rosh follow him. The Beit Shemuel and the Helkat Mehokek quote Tosafot, who are hesitant, but we are lenient. Any birth between 9 and 12 months is a kosher child. The Pithei Teshuvah brings further cases:

  • If the wife became a niddah soon after her husband left, the child is still kosher.
  • Some brought a view that a woman can conceive again from the same seed, so a second child is kosher. The Rabbi said this is clearly not true, and the Beit Yoseph and the Hida rejected it. The Hida suggested instead twins: one born early, the second staying until about 12 months. The Rabbi said he does not think this is scientifically true, but it is interesting.

The Rema's condition. All this applies only if there was no davar meohar, improper behaviour. If she was seen behaving improperly, we do not say the baby stayed in the womb, and the child is a safek Mamzer. The reason, said the Rabbi, is that we rely on the lenient rule only because the woman has a hazakah of being kosher.

What counts as improper? Rav Akiva Eger, in the Pithei Teshuvah, says it must be grave misconduct, and yihud alone is not enough. The Beit Meir says any improper behaviour such as yihud counts. The Rabbi said he would not call flirting enough, but yihud is substantial enough to be considered, at least according to the Beit Meir.

The Rema's last case

A woman became pregnant at the end of Sivan and gave birth at the beginning of Kislev. That is only five full months plus a few days, but the child is kosher, since part of a month counts as a month. The Rabbi noted this is hard today, since a baby survives from about 24 weeks only with serious medical help.

Questions from the class

DNA. DNA is not used in the whole Siman on Mamzerut. Scientifically it is close to certain. But Mamzerut is the most severe area, and the chance of a doubt is enough not to rely on it. It can be used for inheritance.

A ten-month absence with a full digital record. The Rabbi asked what we would say if the husband was abroad for ten months and the wife gave birth. Scientifically the child came from another man. Halachically the child is kosher, because the Gemara says a baby can stay up to 12 months. He did not think any posek today would call the child a Mamzer, however uncomfortable that is. He compared it to kinim on Shabbat, where the Hazon Ish said halachah has decided they do not multiply even though our eyes see they do: "Halachah has decided. That's the reality of halachah." We are not burying our heads in the sand. This is how we are lenient in Mamzerut.

A full-term baby six months after the husband returned. The class found this hard, since a doctor could tell. The Rabbi pointed out that the Rema was lenient even though it was almost impossible then too. A student asked whether an ultrasound that shows a fully developed baby would count. They discussed this at length. The Rabbi could hear the argument, but said an ultrasound is a step removed even from a video recording, that he would need to look into it, and that where there is no davar meohar he would go with the halachah that the child is kosher.

Practical summary

  • Today a convert may marry into the Jewish people, following the Rambam, since no one knows who descends from Ammon, Moab or Egypt.
  • A slave who was freed is a full Jew. If the master only did acts like an aliyah or tefillin, it frees him from work but leaves a safek for kiddushin, following the Ramah.
  • A child of a niddah is not a Mamzer. He is pagum, which we understand as a spiritual blemish, and a good person is not a concern.
  • A baby born up to 12 months after the husband left is kosher unless there was a davar meohar. After 12 months the Shulhan Arukh holds a safek Mamzer, out of concern for the Bahag.
  • Keep apart a Torah Mamzer, a safek Mamzer and a rabbinic Mamzer.
  • DNA is not used in Mamzerut, and the Rabbi left the ultrasound question open.
