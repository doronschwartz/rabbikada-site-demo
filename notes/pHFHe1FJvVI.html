Topic: Rosh Hashanah and the two systems of judgment: Hanhagat HaMishpat and Hanhagat HaYihud Source: The Zohar on praying for personal needs; the Ramhal, Derech Hashem and Daat Tevunot Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Wednesday 21 September 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A pause before Rosh Hashanah

The group had just finished the first part of Daat Tevunot, on the purpose of creation and why human beings are born with deficiencies. Before moving on to the next part, the Rabbi used this evening to talk about Rosh Hashanah. He said it was something they had discussed before, but so vital that it is always worth going over, because it can change the way we spend the day.

Three questions

He opened with three puzzles. - The Zohar. A famous Zohar says that a person who prays on Rosh Hashanah for children, life and livelihood (banei, hayei umezonei) is like a dog barking at its master. Yet from childhood we are taught that Rosh Hashanah is the day everything is decided. If you are being judged, surely you should pray for everything you can think of, from morning to evening. - No viduy. For the whole month of Elul we say viduy every morning, and on Yom Kippur we say it ten times. In the entire liturgy of Rosh Hashanah there is not one viduy. - The order of the days. If Rosh Hashanah is the day of judgment, why does the day of forgiveness come ten days later? Surely you should be forgiven first and judged afterwards.

He added a fourth observation: if you look through the prayers of Rosh Hashanah, you will not find a specific request for a good, healthy and happy life. When someone in the class pointed to Zokhrenu LeHayim and Besefer Hayim, he answered that these were not part of the original prayer. The proof is that if you forget them you do not go back. The hakhamim instituted the core berakhot of Rosh Hashanah; these lines were added later, in the time of the Geonim.

Two systems: Mishpat and Yihud

All of this, he said, is explained by an idea learnt in Derech Hashem and developed further in Daat Tevunot: Hashem runs the world through two systems.

Hanhagat HaMishpat, the system of justice. This is how Hashem runs the world about 99% of the time. Mishpat means there are consequences for our actions, built into the blueprint of creation. He compared it to gravity. If a baby rolls off a table and falls, nobody says gravity is nasty. It is simply a rule of nature, and the fall is its consequence. In the same way Hashem has set out that good actions have good consequences and bad actions have bad ones, and, as it were, He is bound by these rules.

His analogy was a parent with a system of discipline at home. If you tidy your plate you get this; if you do not, you get that. If the child misbehaves and the parent lets it go, "the whole system will break down within a few days." Once the rules are made, the parent's hands are tied.

Hanhagat HaYihud, the system of unity. After the sin of the golden calf, Hashem told Moshe that there would be times when He would be gracious and merciful even to those who do not deserve it. This is the second system. Yihud means one: Hashem is in full control and acts as He wishes, and the ordinary rules can be set aside. Someone who deserves good may receive it; someone who deserves punishment may not receive it. It must be rare, because if it happened all the time the system would collapse.

The analogy here: a child has just run across the road. Any responsible parent has to show that this is terrible. But the child looks up with a smile and a twinkle in his eye, and the father says, "You deserve to be punished so badly, but I just can't bring myself to it. This time only."

Someone asked whether this was simply about the child or the nation being remorseful and crying out. The Rabbi said no. Remorse is taken into account within Mishpat: Hashem weighs how sorry you are when deciding how to judge you. That is what our crying on Yom Kippur does. Yihud is something else: the system itself is put on hold.

Rosh Hashanah and the shofar

There is one time in the year most suited for Hashem to move from Mishpat to Yihud, and that is Rosh Hashanah, through the shofar: "Alah Elohim biteruah, Hashem bekol shofar." Hashem rises from the throne of judgment to the throne of mercy. The throne of mercy means that the regular rules are not being followed.

This answers the questions. - Why not pray for specific things? When you ask for children, health and money, you are engaging with Mishpat: please make sure I get this and that. If instead we succeed in bringing Hashem to act through Yihud, these things happen by themselves. So the work of Rosh Hashanah is to recognise Hashem's sovereignty: He is One, He is the King, He is in control, there is nothing else. That is why the central berakhah of the Amidah is the third one, with its vision that everyone will recognise Him, all evil will dissipate like smoke, and He alone will reign. - Why no viduy? Confessing means asking: did I deserve this, did I do that, I am sorry for this but not for that. That is Mishpat, and it reinforces Mishpat. Stepping back from viduy on Rosh Hashanah reinforces Yihud. The details belong to Yom Kippur, where there are 24 hours to confess and to ask. - Why Yom Kippur afterwards? If you are very fortunate you can have everything settled on Rosh Hashanah. But most of us remain within Mishpat, so we continue in Mishpat for ten more days, and Yom Kippur is the ultimate day of Mishpat. There we try to work within the system; on Rosh Hashanah we try to go beyond it.

He also pointed to Yom Kippur Musaf, where we recall the prayer of the Kohen Gadol asking for a good year in all its details. Yom Kippur is the time to ask and to confess.

Why the Geonim added personal requests

If so, why did the Geonim add lines asking for life, and why do we open the ark for a prayer for parnasah? His answer was simple: for most people Rosh Hashanah, as described, is not relatable. Talking about esoteric ideas of God's unity is too much. So the rabbis made the day relatable by adding prayers about your own future, money, health and children. These are meant as a means to inspire you towards the bigger picture.

The problem is that they become the main thing. He cited the Hida, who wrote that he did not hear people shouting amen to the greatest parts of the prayer, but when it came to the requests, especially for parnasah, everyone shouted amen. He added that the prayer said after the psalm on opening the ark is from the Hida, about 250 years ago, while the custom of saying the psalm comes from the Arizal; neither was part of the original service. "I shouldn't say this publicly," he joked, "it'll kill the auctions."

Coming to Rosh Hashanah calmly

His closing message: people spend hours praying for this and that and miss the whole point. "The whole Rosh Hashanah is be very calm. Just coronate HaKadosh Barukh Hu." Recognise that everything comes from Hashem, and everything else will fall into place by itself. It is hard, which is why we end up adding requests, but understanding this changes our whole approach. It makes us calmer and more mature in our tefillah, and it applies to the whole day, not only to the prayers. Do not come to Rosh Hashanah like the dogs in the Zohar, barking "give me, give me." Come to recognise Hashem as King.

Summary

  • Hashem runs the world through Mishpat, a system of consequences that binds Him as a parent is bound by house rules, and rarely through Yihud, where He sets the system aside and acts with mercy even for the undeserving.
  • Rosh Hashanah, through the shofar, is the time most suited to move Hashem from the throne of judgment to the throne of mercy.
  • Praying for personal needs and confessing sins both engage Mishpat, which is why the original Rosh Hashanah prayers contain neither; the details belong to Yom Kippur.
  • The personal requests in today's mahzor were added later, from the Geonim onwards, to make the day relatable, but they should not become the focus.
  • The work of the day is to coronate Hashem calmly and recognise that everything comes from Him.
