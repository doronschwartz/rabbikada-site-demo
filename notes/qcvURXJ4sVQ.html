Topic: The practical side of officiating at a wedding: preparing the couple, the address, the ketubah, the witnesses and the order of the huppah Sources: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Simanim 55 and 61 with the Rema; Tosafot on the hinuma; Hoshen Mishpat on relatives as witnesses Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada to the Montefiore semikhah students, Tuesday 22 November 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why it matters so much

This was a one-off class for a new group of semikhah students, mainly on the practicalities of a wedding, "touching on the halacha a bit". You may be asked to step in at short notice, the Rabbi said. The first time he officiated, about eight years earlier, it was two days before the wedding, when the rabbi who was meant to do it could not come, and he could not sleep the night before.

A wedding is the moment you gain a couple for life. If you invest time in them, before and on the day, they will stay close to you, call you when they have a baby, and support you. People with little affiliation keep reaching out. He urged the students to see these as friendships, not only rabbi and congregant. Weddings and funerals are the two times people remember forever, and weddings are probably the more powerful. So it must be done with professionalism, sensitivity, understanding and empathy.

Know the couple

Most people in the UK are not religious, and for many congregants the huppah is "a ceremony", something to get through before the party. Many couples treat a get the same way. So they will not remember how well you sang or which maqam you used. They will remember how you spoke to them.

That means getting to know both the hatan and the kallah. Ideally the rabbi and rebbetzin meet the couple together, since the rebbetzin can build a relationship with the bride that the rabbi cannot. For a more religious couple, offer marriage classes, the hatan taught by the rabbi and the kallah by the rebbetzin. At the very least, meet both of them for an hour before the wedding and go through the whole ceremony: the bedeken, walking down, the two berakhot of the erusin, the address, the tallit, the nisuin. Tell them when to answer amen. He gives couples a translation of the sheva berakhot to read together in advance.

The address under the huppah

Speak to them, not to the guests. Look at the couple and use their names. Get their names, and their parents' and grandparents' names, exactly right. He starts the final meeting by asking for them and memorising them. "The worst thing is you get the wrong name under the huppah, then it's over." When he reaches the general devar Torah, he has noticed, couples start looking around; when he says their names, they look back at him and feel it is their ceremony.

Keep it short. Five or six minutes is most effective, eight the absolute maximum. People want to get to the party. Even a Shabbat sermon should rarely pass ten minutes. He quoted Churchill: a ten-minute speech takes an hour to prepare. He told of a guest who was given barely two minutes before Adon Olam and made one point from the parashah: that Yishak loved Rivkah, so even our forefathers made an effort in their marriages. It was "nothing, a translation of a word in the pasuk", and people were still talking about it a week later.

Make at least half of it about them: their parents, grandparents, character, what you enjoyed in your lessons. Humour should be natural, never forced. He recalled a hatan who, at their first meeting, brought a treat for each of the Rabbi's two children; by the wedding he had three, and he opened by telling the story as a way into how thoughtful the groom was.

The ketubah

For your first weddings, fill in the ketubah in advance. You will make at least one mistake. Always bring two or three blank copies, because witnesses sign in the wrong place and correcting a ketubah gets complicated. Get the Hebrew names of the couple and their fathers. Whether the secular name is added depends on the community; unlike a get, the ketubah is not so exacting about names. London is written as the city on the river Thames, because there is another London in Canada.

The witnesses

Many couples will barely know anyone who keeps Shabbat, so arrange two kosher witnesses in advance. Usually the rabbi is one. Explain to the couple why it matters, ask directly whether their candidate is fully observant, and ask whether he is related to either of them: "If you don't ask the questions, they won't tell you." First cousins cannot serve; second cousins can. Without two kosher witnesses to the giving of the ring, "the wedding is not a wedding." Never turn up expecting a witness simply to appear. Often he brings someone himself, and he invited the students to come along.

If the family insists on a witness you are unsure about, let him sign the ketubah, which may have different witnesses. The witnesses to the ring must be beyond doubt. Those who sign the ketubah must be the ones who saw its kinyan.

The tish and the ketubah kinyan

Arrive a good half hour early, and send the couple a message the night before. Today almost every wedding has a tish before the huppah, though the true Sefaradi custom, as in Israel, is to do the ketubah under the huppah between the erusin and the nisuin. Some Sefaradi couples now ask for Ashkenazi customs they have seen at friends' weddings, like the bride circling the groom, which he called a Hasidic custom. His advice: be firm when something is halacha, and relaxed when it is not. "You want to do it? Fine, we'll do it."

About twenty minutes before the huppah, take the hatan and two witnesses. Explain the ketubah briefly. Its three basic obligations are food, clothing and onah. People think onah means relations; the word means time. He tells couples that the point is sacred time together every day, without phones or distractions. Then the hatan lifts a handkerchief or pen as a kinyan, gives it back, and the witnesses sign. He does not need to understand the mechanics of the kinyan.

Bedeken and huppah

What is the huppah? There are several views among the Rishonim. Tosafot hold it is the hinuma, the covering of the bride's face, which Ashkenazim call the bedeken. The Shulhan Arukh, following the Rambam, holds it is yihud. The Rema holds it is the canopy. Today we do all of them, which is why some rabbis ask the witnesses to watch the bedeken.

It has become fashionable for the groom to come halfway down the aisle to meet the bride and walk her in. The Rabbi said it is a completely new custom, not mentioned anywhere in the poskim. Asked whether a groom may leave the huppah once he is standing there, he said there is nothing halachic against it. His only reservation is that walking in together before they are married may not be appropriate, though for many couples it hardly makes a difference.

Under the huppah

Give a running commentary throughout, so everyone knows what is happening. Use white wine or white grape juice, in case it spills on the dress. Go through in advance who brings the cup, the wine, the glass to break and the ring.

The groom should really say the two berakhot of the erusin himself, but since the Rishonim the rabbi says them so as not to embarrass those who cannot. Tell the couple you are saying them on their behalf and they should answer amen.

The erusin and the nisuin were once separate events, each with its own berakhah over wine, so today there has to be a break (hefsek) between them. Sefaradim and Israelis make the break by reading the ketubah there. Where the ketubah was done at the tish, the rabbi's address fills that role.

Finally, the ring must belong to the groom. If the couple plan to use a family ring that belongs to the bride's side, he must first acquire it, paying for it in front of the rabbi. If you do not ask, there may be no kiddushin.

Practical summary

  • Invest in the couple beforehand; meet both of them and walk them through the ceremony.
  • Speak to the couple by name for five to eight minutes, at least half of it about them.
  • Fill in the ketubah in advance and bring spare copies.
  • Secure two kosher, unrelated witnesses for the ring; a doubtful one may sign the ketubah only.
  • Check that the ring belongs to the groom, and arrange the cup, wine, glass and ring in advance.
