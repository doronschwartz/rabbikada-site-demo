A joint evening

This was an online evening hosted by the Wembley Sephardi community, where the Rabbi had served for almost seven years, with neighbouring communities taking part. After his talk, two Ashkenazi rabbis joined a question and answer panel. He had proposed a later date, but the host rightly asked for an earlier one "so that we can put into practice what we're going to learn tonight." He invited the Ashkenazi rabbis to interrupt if anything did not fit Ashkenazi halacha.

1. We search for pieces, not crumbs

"If we learn the halacha correctly with regards to cleaning, I believe we will save hours and hours." People arrive at Pesah worn out. "We're not meant to land in Pesah and fall flat out."

The Torah forbids owning hames on Pesah. From the Torah, though, it is enough to relinquish ownership: even expensive bottles of whiskey could stay in the house if truly made ownerless. The Hakhamim required us to search for and remove hames for two reasons:

  • We may not relinquish it sincerely. If a builder came in over Hol HaMoed and began taking the whiskey off the shelf, would we really feel it was not ours?
  • Eight days is a long time, and we may come to eat it.

Since searching and removing is rabbinic, the authorities debate how far it goes. One view requires searching for everything. Rav Ovadia Yoseph and others rule that we search only for a kazayit or more, around 18 grams on the stricter side. The Rabbi's understanding was that the Mishnah Berurah speaks of destroying smaller pieces, not of searching for them, so for Ashkenazim too one searches for pieces.

So the whole search "shouldn't take that long". Spring cleaning is fine, but do not blame Pesah for it. The caveat: where food is eaten or prepared, a crumb matters for eating, so the kitchen and dining area need a deep clean.

2. Not the whole house

The Shulhan Arukh says we search places where hames is brought during the year, not, for example, a wine store where no one ever brings food. Bathrooms, toilets and sheds do not need checking. If you never bring food upstairs, "you can go into Pesah with a clean conscience" without searching there.

  • Young children are like the mice in the Gemara that carry hames from place to place. With toddlers, check wherever they can reach.
  • The garden and the car need checking for pieces, especially after a picnic or a Shabbat lunch outside.
  • Pockets. It is a good custom to check them, but adults who never put food in their pockets need not.

3. Not only the night before

The search is on the night of the fourteenth because during the day we are busy and a flame shows the corners best. But it is not limited to that night. If you missed it, search the next day; if you were ill or forgot, search even during Pesah. And if all of Pesah passed without a search, you must still search afterwards, because hames owned over Pesah is forbidden even for benefit for ever. "Bizarrely", someone could be going round with a candle the night after Pesah.

4. Not a hunt for ten pieces

The Raavad, in twelfth-century Provence, called the custom of hiding pieces a custom of the unlearned. People feared that a berakhah on a search that found nothing would be in vain. But the misvah is to search, not to find. Later authorities also said it was unnecessary. Through the Arizal it became the custom of most Sephardi and Ashkenazi communities.

Two points. Do not turn the evening into a search for the ten pieces: "that defeats the whole purpose." And make each piece less than a kazayit. Then, when only nine turn up and the phone calls to rabbis begin, there is no need to turn the house upside down.

5. What needs a hekhsher (Sephardim)

Here Sephardim and Ashkenazim differ. On Pesah even a drop of hames forbids a dish, but only if it falls in on Pesah. Before Pesah it is nullified in sixty, and for Sephardim it stays nullified. Ashkenazim are strict that it reawakens.

Rav Ovadia Yoseph gives "a radical example". If, a few days before Pesah, a child drops his sandwich into the barrel of wine meant for all the Seder guests, strain out the pieces. Any remaining crumbs are nullified, and the wine may be used even for the four cups.

So pure products such as water, salt, sugar and oil, made before Pesah, need no hekhsher for Sephardim. At the start of Covid the London Beth Din allowed Ashkenazim to rely on this. In normal years, the Rabbi said, Ashkenazim should buy with a hekhsher; for Sephardim this holds "year in, year out".

6. Sephardim do not eat hames

Hames is only the five grains. Rice and beans are kitniyot, not hames. So an Ashkenazi may eat in a Sephardi home on Pesah, even food cooked in a pot used for rice an hour before. The Ashkenazi custom forbids eating kitniyot themselves, not the taste absorbed in a pot. One of the Ashkenazi rabbis on the panel agreed, citing the Rema. The guest simply needs to tell the hosts not to serve actual kitniyot.

7. Medicines

Following the Rambam, tasteless or bitter medicines may be taken on Pesah even if they contain hames, and need no hekhsher. The concept of giving importance by eating it does not apply to medicines. Rav Moshe Feinstein ruled the same. Flavoured medicines, such as children's syrups, must be checked.

From the panel and the questions

  • Ashkenazi view on crumbs. One panel rabbi understood the Mishnah Berurah to require removing even crumbs. The Rabbi noted the Mishnah Berurah speaks of destroying smaller pieces, not of searching for them.
  • Advice from the panel. Wrap the ten pieces so they do not crumble. Use a battery torch in the car. Prepare the Haggadah before the night.
  • Candle or torch? A battery torch is fine; electric room lighting alone is not enough.
  • Feathers and spoons are an Ashkenazi custom; Sephardim do not use them.
  • Young children at the Seder. Let them sleep in the afternoon and start promptly. Rav Ovadia Yoseph allows, in pressing circumstances, beginning Kiddush from sunset, but Maggid must wait until nightfall.
  • Going away. Search your home before leaving, without a berakhah, especially if you are not selling all its hames. Where you stay, search your room on the night with a berakhah, both for your own hames and for what earlier guests left. You may rely on your host's berakhah if you search together.
  • Offices and workplaces need checking too.

Practical summary

  • Outside eating and cooking areas, search for pieces of about a kazayit, not crumbs.
  • Search only where hames is brought during the year, plus the car and garden; with toddlers, everywhere they reach.
  • A missed search is made up the next day, during Pesah, or even after Pesah.
  • Hide ten pieces only if you keep the custom, each smaller than a kazayit, and search the whole house.
  • Ashkenazim may eat in Sephardi homes as long as they are not served kitniyot themselves.
