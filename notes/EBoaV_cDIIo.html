Topic: How a candidate is prepared and tested, the requirement of leshem shamayim, and a conversion done for marriage

Source: Gemara Yevamot 24b and 47a; Tosafot, Kiddushin 70b; the Rambam, Issurei Biah 13-14; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268

Shiur: Kabbalat Misvot Part 1, the second class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, on Zoom, Wednesday 5 May 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

What we tell the candidate

After reviewing milah and tevilah, the Rabbi turned to the spiritual and emotional preparation of a convert, before reaching the third requirement, kabbalat misvot.

The Gemara in Yevamot (47a) sets out the conversation. We tell the candidate that the Jewish people are persecuted and suffering. If he answers "I know, and still I want to," we accept him at once. We then inform him of some of the light misvot and some of the severe ones. The Meiri gives tying one's shoes as an example of a light misvah, and Shabbat and avodah zarah as severe ones; the Bayit Hadash understands "light" as easy to do, such as sitting in the sukkah. We tell him the punishments and also the reward, but not at length: as the Shakh explains, even punishments such as sekilah and karet are mentioned only in broad terms. There is a tension, the Rabbi explained. We want to accept someone sincere, but not someone who will not keep the misvot, so we neither push too hard nor encourage too much.

Why converts are "hard for Israel"

Tosafot in Kiddushin (70b) gives several explanations of the saying that converts are as hard for Israel as a sapahat:

  • Rashi: they may not keep the misvot, and Jews will learn from them. Tosafot rejects this.
  • The Torah warns us about the ger in many places (the Midrash counts 24, 36 or 48), so it is easy to sin against him.
  • The Gemara in Pesahim says Israel was scattered in exile so that converts would join, so converts are the reason for the exile. Someone in the class pointed out that this explains the name sapahat rather than a reason not to accept them, and the Rabbi agreed.
  • Rabbi Avraham HaGer, one of the Baalei HaTosafot and himself a convert: converts are so good that they show the rest of Israel in a poor light, as the widow of Sarefat said to Eliyahu, "you have come to remind me of my sin."
  • Converts do not bring yihus.

The Rambam: principles of faith first

The Rabbi called this "one of the most famous Rambams." The Gemara speaks only of teaching some light and some severe misvot. The Rambam (Issurei Biah 14:2) writes that we inform the candidate of the principles of faith, the unity of Hashem and the prohibition of avodah zarah, and spend a long time on them; then we teach some of the misvot, and do not spend long on that. He has reversed the emphasis of the Gemara, the Rabbi said, because for him it was unthinkable to teach a convert without teaching the framework first. "Judaism isn't about robotic actions, just doing this and doing that. It's about the understanding, the framework, the context." This rejects the idea that Judaism is only about practice. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam.

Someone asked why the Rambam mentions only one or two principles. The Rabbi answered that the unity of Hashem and the prohibition of idolatry are really one principle, given as the most important example; he holds that all thirteen principles are essential. Practically, a Beit Din should not get bogged down in details. No one expects a convert to know everything first: the Gemara itself speaks of a convert who was never taught Shabbat. People learn as they go.

Leshem shamayim

A convert must come for the sake of Heaven. The Gemara says converts were not accepted in the days of David and Shlomo, and will not be in the days of Mashiah, because people would join out of fear or for status. The Rabbi added that, as the Rambam makes very clear, the only difference between now and the days of Mashiah is shibud malkhuyot, subjugation to the nations; the other passages of the Gemara are to be understood metaphorically. The difference will be the political sovereignty of the State of Israel, and that, he said, is essentially what Mashiah is, which is why it is impossible not to see the State of Israel now as ikveta diMeshiha, the footsteps of Mashiah. When Israel is fully sovereign, other nations will want to be part of it, and that is why converts will not be accepted then. The Shulhan Arukh (268:12), following the Rambam, says the Beit Din checks whether the candidate is coming for money, position or fear, and whether a man has a Jewish girlfriend or a woman a Jewish boyfriend. If there is no such reason, he is accepted.

The Rabbi offered a modern reading of "fear." Some candidates have emotional or psychological difficulties and imagine Judaism will solve their problems. A Beit Din should be careful with them. Someone asked whether wanting a better life is not a fair reason. It depends, he said, what a better life means: if it means coming close to Hashem, that is fine. A Beit Din must also beware of people converting for money, and this happens. A couple of years ago a man came who, meaning it as a compliment, spoke of how clever, wealthy and successful the Jewish people are and said he wanted to be part of them. The Beit Din pushed him away for some time until he showed that he was coming leshem shamayim; in the end he came back and has now converted.

"Everything according to the judgment of the Beit Din"

Tosafot asks how Hillel could convert the man who wanted to become Kohen Gadol, and how the student in Menahot (44a) could convert the woman who came for marriage, since neither was leshem shamayim. Tosafot answers that Hillel could see they would end up sincere: by the time they converted, they would be leshem shamayim. On this the Beit Yoseph writes the words the Rabbi called probably the most important line in Hilchot Gerut: hakol lefi reut einei beit din, everything depends on what the Beit Din sees.

There is almost nothing like this elsewhere in halacha, and it is why conversion is so open-ended. What one Beit Din judges to be sincere another may not, and this is why one Beit Din sometimes does not accept another's conversion. The principle also works the other way: once the three are reliable people, we accept what they did, even if we would have judged differently. The sincerity must be there at the time of the conversion.

A conversion for marriage

What if the Beit Din converted someone who came purely for marriage? Rav Moshe Feinstein already wrote that most conversions in his time were for marriage. The Gemara in Yevamot (24b) records a dispute, and the halacha is that bediavad such a conversion is valid. The Rabbi admitted he has always struggled with what a "bediavad conversion" means for the children. Someone asked about a person who wants to marry because they want a religious Jewish marriage. That, he said, is leshem shamayim. But converting only to have an Orthodox wedding and be accepted by the family is unacceptable.

For shidduchim he drew a clear line. A convert is fully Jewish, and declining a shidduch because a family has converts would be wrong. But if there is a real question whether a conversion was only bediavad, a family may decline, and the child can always convert again lehumra. He gave a case from a couple of years earlier. A man asked him about a shidduch suggested for one of his children: the other family is now fully religious and keeps Torah and misvot, but the mother's first conversion was questionable; she became more religious and converted a second time. The Rabbi said he could only give the facts: the child was conceived after a bediavad conversion. If the child now wants to convert again, that would be absolutely fine and certainly leshem shamayim, and the family is religious, so there is no problem, but it is something to bear in mind.

Nor is every marriage conversion second best. A Beit Din never accepts the non-Jewish partner alone; both partners learn, and very often the Jewish partner becomes religious too.

Rejecting three times?

There is no halachic requirement to turn a candidate away three times. The only source is the Midrash on Ruth, where Naomi says "shovnah benotai" three times, and the same Midrash teaches that one should push away with the left hand and draw near with the right. A mixture of discouragement and encouragement is enough. Asked about a rabbi who ignores a candidate's first emails, he said he strongly disagrees: it is unprofessional and impolite. Tell the candidate about antisemitism, as the Gemara does, but also about the great reward of keeping the misvot.

A thought for Shavuot

Chapter 1 of Ruth teaches Hilchot Gerut, which implies that Ruth and Orpah were not Jewish when they married; chapter 4 teaches Halisah, which needs a Jewish marriage. The Rabbi suggested that Naomi held like Rabbi Nehemiah, that a conversion for marriage is invalid even bediavad. She tested them. Orpah turned back, showing she was never Jewish; Ruth persisted, showing that she was.

Questions from the class

  • Converting again. Someone who converted bediavad and became religious repeats the conversion before three, with hatafat dam berit lehumra for a man, and without a berakhah.
  • Rejected by one Beit Din, accepted by another. That is acceptable if both are reliable; standards and expectations differ.
  • Belief without revelation. Would a candidate who believes in the God of the philosophers but not in revelation be accepted? "I don't know, it's a difficult point," the Rabbi said, but he leaned towards no, since all the principles of faith are required, unless one sees the person will learn more.
  • Discouraging a sincere candidate. Balancing discouragement and encouragement is very difficult. The Rabbi told of a Muslim girl a few years earlier whose family took her to the imam to do a tikkun on her and rid her of the spirits that made her want to become Jewish. It did not work, and she became Jewish: a very inspiring story, he said, and to the best of his knowledge she still keeps Torah and misvot, very sincerely. At the beginning the Beit Din discouraged her, because her large family had disowned her, just as in a Hasidic family the other children will not speak to a son who has gone off the derech.

Beginning kabbalat misvot

The Gemara in Bekhorot (30b) says a candidate who accepts the whole Torah except one thing is not accepted. Yet the Gemara in Shabbat speaks of a convert who was never taught Shabbat, and the Ramban explains he was converted before three and taught only some of the misvot. The class ended with questions for next week: a woman who will not cover her hair; a convert who says the next day she was not sincere; and Rav Moshe's case of a divorced convert who claimed years later that she never accepted the misvot.

Practical summary

  • The candidate is told of the hardships, some light and some severe misvot, and the reward, briefly and in a balanced way.
  • Following the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh, the main teaching is the principles of faith; the details come with time.
  • Lekhathilah a convert must be leshem shamayim, and the Beit Din judges this: hakol lefi reut einei beit din.
  • A conversion done for marriage is valid bediavad, and often leads to a sincere Jewish home.
  • There is no requirement to reject a candidate three times; discourage with one hand and draw near with the other.
