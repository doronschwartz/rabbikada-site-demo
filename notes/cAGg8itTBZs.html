Introduction

Siman 154 lists the cases in which Beit Din may force a husband to give a get (kofin lehosi). The next class will take Seifim 6 and 7, on a husband who cannot have relations, which the Rabbi called the hardest part of the siman.

A theme runs through the whole siman. If Beit Din forces a get when it had no right to, the get is not a real get, and this raises a safek mamzerut for any later children. So it is not only a question of whether the wife has a good complaint. The Shakh, who wrote a work on this subject (Gevurat Anashim, quoted often by the Pithei Teshuvah), is the most stringent voice in the siman, and he changes how we think about force today.

The Gemara: which defects allow force? (Seif 1)

There is a mahloket in the Mishnah in Ketubot.

  • The Tanna Kamma: if a husband develops a blemish (mum) after the wedding, he cannot be forced to divorce. She took him "for better or for worse".
  • The Rashbag: the Gemara's examples are one eye blinded, one hand cut off, one leg broken. In each case he has a second limb, so he can still function. Even for these "big blemishes", the Tanna Kamma says no force.

The next Mishnah lists cases where everyone agrees he can be forced:

  • A mukeh shehin (boils over the body). Relations make the illness worse and are dangerous. This is the one case where even if she says "I am happy to stay", he must divorce, unless she agrees to have no relations at all.
  • Bad breath, a bad body odor, or a smelly trade (collecting dog dung, working copper, tanning). The Rabbi said these are mainly about smell.

Rabbi Meir adds that even if she knew when she married, she can say "I thought I could manage, but I cannot". The halachah is not like Rabbi Meir where there was an explicit condition (tenai). If she made a condition at the wedding, she must keep her word.

The Shulhan Arukh (Seif 1)

The Shulhan Arukh says we force a get and a ketubah in these cases. Note the wording: the husband who had the problem after marrying.

What if she knew beforehand but there was no explicit condition? The Rambam says it must be something she did not know about, and the Shulhan Arukh follows him. The Rabbi noted that the Mishnah seems to speak of a case where she did not know. There are Rishonim who say that without a stated condition she may still go back, but the Shulhan Arukh does not rule like them.

If she clearly knew (it was plain to anyone who spent time with him), then by agreeing to marry she accepted it (sevara vekibla), so she cannot demand a get.

The meshumad (Rema)

The Rema says we can force a meshumad, in those days someone who converted to Christianity, to give a get. The reasoning: we are concerned he would say he does not need a get. The Rema adds that it is better to live with such a husband than alone. The exception: if he is affecting the home, for example the home is no longer kosher and she cannot live a religious life, then everyone agrees we can force. If he does not affect the home, we should not force. The Rabbi said many couples today have only one partner who is observant, and it would be wrong to tell them to divorce. "You stay together and try to grow together", as long as the spouse is not preventing the lifestyle.

Seifim 2 and 4: a hereditary defect, and "one" or "two"

Seif 2 is straightforward from the Gemara. If the bad breath or odor is part of his nature (hereditary), she can say she never agreed to live with him, or with her husband's brother in a case of yibum. She does halisah and receives her ketubah.

Seif 4 continues Seif 1. The Rosh and the Meiri each draw an inference (diyuk) from the Mishnah.

  • The Rosh: the Mishnah uses the singular (one eye, one hand, one leg). If someone loses both eyes, both hands, or both legs, it is so grave that everyone would agree he can be forced.
  • The Meiri: the Mishnah's examples are of defects where he can still function. He agrees with the Rosh, and adds that if a person loses an organ of which he has only one (the sense of smell, the nose), he can also be forced.

The Rema brings both. The Gra, the Pithei Teshuvah and the Shakh take the stringent view that we do not decide to force even then. The Rabbi's conclusion, following the Gra and the Pithei Teshuvah: nowadays we would not force even if he lost both eyes, because of the safek mamzerut. Whenever there is a doubt about whether force is allowed, there is a danger of a mamzer. But the Rabbi added that this applies to forcing the get. If a couple already live apart, we do not use the Rema alone to excuse a husband from paying the ketubah and support.

The Pithei Teshuvah adds that a bad smell must be very strong and constant. The Rabbi explained why Hazal saw it as worse than blindness: a blind person can still have a loving, intimate relationship, but constant odor makes intimacy very hard.

Seif 3: refusing to support, or refusing relations

The siman now moves to a husband who says he will not support his wife. The three obligations are food, clothing and marital relations (mezonot, kesut, onah).

Rav and Shmuel.

  • Rav: we force him to divorce (kofin lehosi), since he is not keeping his ketubah obligations.
  • Shmuel: we do not force a divorce, we force him to provide (kofin lazun). In the Gemara Shmuel responds sharply to someone who quoted Rav: if you are going to force him anyway, why not force him to feed her?

The Shulhan Arukh says "ha'omer", someone who says he will not support her. It is not enough that he has been lax for a few months. It must be a declaration that he has had enough.

  • The Rif and the Rambam rule like Shmuel: force him to provide. If he continues to refuse, one keeps forcing him. Perhaps eventually he will divorce willingly. One cannot force a get before trying to force support.
  • The Bahag and the Rosh rule like Rav. They cite the Yerushalmi, and argue that her need for food is constant, one cannot send her to Beit Din every month, she may be embarrassed to come, and there is a danger of starvation. The Tur rules like his father, the Rosh.
  • The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam.

If he has no money and will not work, he is forced to divorce. The Shulhan Arukh says he "does not want" to make money, which implies that if he tries and cannot, we do not force. The same applies to relations: if he says "I will not have relations any more", one must first try to compel him by other means, which is hard to enforce.

The Shakh. It seems clear that refusing food or refusing relations is each enough. But the Shakh (in Gevurat Anashim) says the Rishonim spoke only of a refusal of both together. One alone is not enough. The Rabbi said he is "the most stringent in this whole siman", and that we do not always follow him, but he is a factor to keep in mind, because of the question of mamzerut.

The Rema: throwing out a wife, and hitting a wife

Throwing her out. The Rema says that a man who regularly gets angry and throws his wife out of the house can be forced to divorce, as this combines refusing food and relations. The Shakh objects: the Rashba wrote this only as a possibility (efshar), and one cannot rely on it.

The Rabbi said he felt uncomfortable with the stringency today, since a woman who is thrown out would be told to suffer. "Nowadays it could actually be the right thing" to follow the Rashba and the Rema rather than the Shakh. "We get very scared with these things, but we also need some confidence that this is morally the right thing." Still, he added, "what would we do in Beit Din today? I don't know."

Hitting. The Rema continues with a man who hits his wife.

  • It is the same sin as hitting any other person, and the Torah limit of 40 applies.
  • Beit Din should impose sanctions to make him stop.
  • If he still does not listen, the Rishonim equate this with bad breath (re'ach hapeh), though it is not in the Mishnah, and he can be forced to divorce.
  • The Rema adds that if she was cursing or provoking, it is different.

The Rabbi said that when people claim the Shulhan Arukh allows hitting a wife, "that is the rumor speaking, not the Shulhan Arukh".

In practice. In cases of domestic abuse, the husband will usually say she provoked him. Beit Din should not just accept this. It should put someone in between to verify what is happening. But once it is clear that he is the sole cause, it seems from the Rema that Beit Din can force a get.

Seif 5: a man who is getting worse

The Shulhan Arukh describes a husband who is getting worse and worse mentally. His wife says her father made her marry him because he could not support her, she thought she could live with him, and now she fears he will kill her in his anger. The ruling is that this is not accepted.

The Gra says she knew beforehand, and others add that the facts were unclear, so one cannot learn much from this case.

Nowadays. Take a man who develops a diagnosed illness after the marriage, such as bipolar disorder, which she did not know about. The later poskim say he can be forced to divorce while he is lucid, and that this does not contradict the Rosh and the Shulhan Arukh, who spoke of a person who was simply angry and not clearly insane. A man who is completely insane (a shoteh) cannot give a get at all, "one of the most tragic cases". For someone with a diagnosed disorder such as schizophrenia, they also say he can be forced when he is lucid.

Practical summary

  • A husband can be forced to divorce for the Mishnah's severe categories (bad odor, mukeh shehin), but not for an ordinary physical blemish.
  • If the wife knew beforehand, even without an explicit condition, she cannot demand a get (the Rambam, followed by the Shulhan Arukh).
  • Force without the right to force creates a safek mamzerut. So the Beit Din must be sure the case fits, and the Shakh and the Pithei Teshuvah are stringent.
  • If a husband refuses to support his wife, the Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam: first force him to provide, and only then to divorce. Refusing relations is treated similarly.
  • Hitting a wife is forbidden. If sanctions fail, Beit Din may force a get, but must first verify who is the cause.
  • Where a husband has a diagnosed mental illness, force is possible while he is lucid, though the Rabbi noted that this is a delicate area.
