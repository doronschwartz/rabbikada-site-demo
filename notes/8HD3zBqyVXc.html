Sefer: Emunot VeDeot (The Book of Beliefs and Opinions) by Rav Saadiah Gaon Chapters: Introduction, Chapter 7 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Monday series on Emunot VeDeot, Monday 18 March 2024, in person and on Zoom

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Introduction has eight chapters, and this class finished the seventh. The Rabbi began with a quick recap. Chapter 1 thanks Hashem for the ability to write the book. Chapter 2 explains that people go wrong in belief mostly because they lack the patience to see things through, and that Rav Saadiah wrote the book for those who keep flipping from one view to another. Chapter 3 answers the complaint "why is it so hard?": anything human needs time and cultivation, and wise people are patient. Chapter 4 defines emunah as an idea we have examined and then store in the mind. Chapter 5 gives the sources of knowledge: the senses, pure reason, inference, and verified tradition. Last week's chapter 6 argued that we are not only allowed but obliged to think about our faith, once the Torah is our foundation.

Chapter 7 now asks the opposite question: why do people reject Torah and reject Hashem? Rav Saadiah gives eight reasons. The Rabbi believes most of them are very relevant today.

First, the class's own answers

Before opening the text, the Rabbi asked the class what they thought was the main reason people deny Torah. The suggestions were a lack of proof, the influence of a secular society that leaves God out, the fact that belief is inconvenient and unbelief is easy, and a culture of individualism and the pursuit of happiness through material things. The Rabbi said Rav Saadiah touches on several of these, and asked everyone to keep in mind which of the eight they find most true.

Reasons 1 to 4: the person who will not think

1. It is inconvenient to accept the truth. When a proof would make a person responsible and commit him, he runs away from it. Rav Saadiah reads the verse in Yehezkel about those who "went far away" as people who went far from the proofs for Hashem. The Rabbi brought Aldous Huxley, a famous atheist, who admitted near the end of his life that he had wanted the world to have no meaning, because meaninglessness was for him "an instrument of liberation". We are very good at bending the truth when we do not want to see it. The Rabbi's example was Yosef's brothers: Yosef gave them hint after hint, and they missed them all, because they had told themselves for twenty years that he was a nobody. Admitting that the viceroy of Egypt was their brother was too hard.

2. People simply do not think. Rav Saadiah is blunt: some people say there is no God without ever having considered the question. The Rabbi told of someone who said he did not believe in God. Asked whether he had read and weighed the views, or simply decided one gloomy afternoon on the bus, the man admitted, "I haven't really thought about it." These people are the hardest to talk to, because there is nothing to argue with. But a person would never talk so lightly about a king or a dictator, and there are consequences to saying such things without thinking.

3. People are too busy. They are able and willing, but life is full of earning a living, children, property and pleasures, and they have no time for the big questions. "I don't know, I'm an agnostic, I'm busy." Rav Saadiah compares it to someone too busy to look after his health: he will pay for it eventually. The Rabbi thinks this is especially true of our generation, with its gadgets and constant noise.

4. It is too tiring. Here the person has the time and the ability and is not stupid, but he cannot sit through the long process of going back and forth through the arguments. He takes a shortcut, a summary, "whatever Wikipedia says", and treats that as his conclusion. As in any career, shortcuts do not get you there.

Reasons 5 and 6: pride and surroundings

5. Arrogance. "I believe only what I can see. I am a scientist. Show me proof of an infinite creator." Rav Saadiah says such a person will not admit that there are things humans do not yet know, and a proud person will not learn from others. The Rabbi noted how remarkable it is that Rav Saadiah was already describing this eleven hundred years ago.

6. Influence. You hear something, or people around you keep telling you something, and it settles in your heart. The Rambam says plainly that a human being is shaped by his surroundings. If you live among people who deny Torah, it will affect you, just as you cannot walk out into freezing cold or burning heat and say it makes no difference to you. Choosing your company matters.

A question: what about suffering?

Someone suggested another reason: people who have seen terrible things in their lives conclude that there is no God. The Rabbi agreed this is a very strong point. It is the question the prophets themselves asked: Yirmiyahu, "Why does the way of the wicked succeed?", and Moshe, "Why do the righteous suffer?" Rav Saadiah does not list it among the eight, perhaps because he believes it has an answer, which would put it under reason 4: a person must be willing to go through the texts and the arguments back and forth before deciding. The Rabbi plans a separate class on the question. Later in the evening another participant raised it again, and the Rabbi agreed that it is a real obstacle to belief for many people.

Reasons 7 and 8: what religion looks like from outside

7. A shallow presentation of Torah. Some people hear a poor lecture or a thin explanation, decide that this is what Judaism is, and dismiss it. Rav Saadiah's picture is a man selling expensive furs and fine wool who has no idea of their worth. His ignorance does not lower the value of the goods. In the same way, the people presenting Torah may misunderstand it, but that takes nothing from the Torah. The Rabbi called the modern version "fast food Torah": simple stories, a laugh, and a simplified picture of what the Sages said. Intelligent people are sometimes put off by the way Torah was presented to them, and it is our duty to present it in a way that makes sense.

8. The bad behaviour of religious people. When a person sees believers acting badly, he comes to dislike them, and then he dislikes their God. Rav Saadiah quotes David: "my zeal has consumed me", meaning that because of his battles people hated him and so forgot Hashem's words. The Rabbi's response was that nobody can take your faith from you except yourself, but we have a responsibility to be seen doing the right thing, not only to do it. He cited the Rambam's definition of Hillul Hashem: a Torah scholar who buys on credit, which is acceptable for others, is held to a higher standard because people are watching him. Every Jew is a light to the nations, and good behaviour brings people closer to Hashem while bad behaviour pushes them away. A few bad examples can shape how a whole community or country sees Judaism.

The class's answers, and the Rabbi's own

When asked which reason matters most today, participants gave several answers. Some said that sheer stupidity, or a combination of reasons 1 and 2, explains why people think the world made itself. Another said reason 3, that we are all more distracted than ever, though others observed that more people are also learning Torah than before. Another pointed to reason 4: people who can think, but take the shortcut. Another said the real problem is people who were never properly taught, and who remember some poor answer they were given years ago. And one linked it to reasons 4 and 6, together with the problem of suffering.

For the Rabbi, in his own experience, it has been reason 7, combined with 6 and 8. Many people carry a wrong picture of Torah and of Hashem, and reject it because the picture does not make sense. "If I was taught the things people tell me they were taught, I probably would reject Torah as well." So it is on all of us to present Torah to our children, friends and students in a way that makes sense.

The last paragraph of the chapter

Rav Saadiah ends the chapter with the people who are genuinely torn. They read verses they do not understand, they pray and do not feel answered, they see wicked people prosper, they see death strike everyone alike, and they are troubled by the unity of Hashem or by reward and punishment. For all of these he promises a full treatise, each in its proper place, and he hopes to be of help. If you are searching for the truth, he says, this book is for you. If you have already made up your mind, you can leave now.

Chapter 8 is very short, a summary of the plan of the book. It was covered at the next class, together with a review of the whole Introduction, before the book proper begins with Treatise 1.

Summary

  • Rav Saadiah gives eight reasons why people deny Torah: it is inconvenient, people do not think, they are too busy, they find thinking tiring, pride, influence, a shallow presentation of Torah, and the bad behaviour of believers.
  • Most of the eight are about the person, not about the evidence: it is easy to distort the truth when we do not want to see it.
  • A teacher or community that presents Torah badly does not make the Torah false, but we are responsible for presenting it well and behaving well.
  • The book is for those who are genuinely searching and willing to think things through to the end.
