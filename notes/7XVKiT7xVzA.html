Topic: Who is obligated in the sukkah, who is exempt, and how the misvah of dwelling is fulfilled Source: The Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Shofar, Sukkah VeLulav, Chapter 6 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Thursday 24 September 2026, the day before Sukkot These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Women, children and Hinukh

In past years the Rabbi taught Sukkot from the Shulhan Arukh; this year he turned to the Rambam, as he had for Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Chapter 6 deals with who sits in the sukkah and how. Women are exempt because it is a positive misvah bound to a time: seven days a year. A person whose gender is uncertain is obligated out of doubt, and someone half slave and half free is obligated because half of him is free. Children are exempt, but a child of about five or six, who no longer needs his mother, is obligated rabbinically, to train him. The Gemara gives no age; it describes a child who manages on his own and does not wake in the night calling for his mother. "There's no fixed or set age of Hinukh. It depends on each child." A very mature child could be four, another seven. Whose obligation is it? For Rashi the child is exempt and the rabbinic duty of Hinukh falls on the father. For Tosafot the rabbis placed the duty on the child himself. Their proof is the Gemara in Berakhot: a child who ate a kabeisah of bread can say Birkat HaMazon for an adult who ate only a kazayit, since both obligations are rabbinic. The Rambam could be read either way: he says the child "is obligated", not the father, yet opens by saying children are exempt. The question runs through all the laws of Hinukh. In any case, young children should be encouraged to sit in the sukkah.

Dwell as you live: the sick and the uncomfortable

The sick. A sick person and those looking after him are exempt, even for a headache or sore eyes. The verse says "You shall dwell in sukkot seven days", and the Gemara reads it as: "You dwell like you live." At home, if you felt unwell, you would lie down and make yourself comfortable, and a sick person is more comfortable in his own bed. The Rabbi said even a slight cold would exempt someone from eating or sleeping in the sukkah during the rest of Sukkot, though he would not rely on it on the first night. The helpers are exempt only while they are actually helping. The mistaer (one who is bothered). Someone bothered in the sukkah is exempt, but not those serving him. The Rambam's examples are wind, flies and insects, or a bad smell that stops him sleeping there: just as at home you would leave a draughty room. Rain is a separate exemption. Two points: - The examples are all about sleeping. The poskim learn that it takes more discomfort to leave for a meal than for sleep: to eat, you can put on a coat. - The discomfort must come from outside and be unexpected. If the sukkah is built where a constant problem makes it unfit to live in, such as a garden infested with rats and mice, there is a strong argument that you do not fulfil the misvah even when you eat there. A smell that starts on the second day because a neighbour begins building is different. "You build your sukkah in a place where it's possible." The mourner. An avel, after the burial, is obligated (an onen, before it, is exempt from all misvot). He is not exempt as one who is bothered, because that exemption is for discomfort caused from outside; a mourner's distress is within him.

The groom, the traveller and the guard

The hatan. A groom, his companions and all who celebrate with him are exempt all seven days of the feast, for example someone who married between Yom Kippur and Sukkot. The Rabbi linked this to the Rambam's view that marriage is completed by bringing the bride into one's home: the huppah was a small canopy that served as the couple's home for the week, too small for everyone. So there is strong room to hold a Sheva Berakhot meal during Sukkot in an ordinary hall if it cannot fit in a sukkah, but only while the guests are celebrating with the groom. On the first nights he was unsure and thought not. On the way to a misvah. Someone travelling for a misvah, such as redeeming a captive, bringing charity, visiting his rav or going to learn Torah, is exempt day and night. An ordinary traveller is exempt only while travelling: by day if he travels by day, and then he must find a sukkah to sleep in. Tosafot limit the misvah exemption to where finding a sukkah would get in the way of the misvah; if a public sukkah is available, why be exempt? The Rambam makes no such distinction. The Rabbi read him as holding that the whole journey is one misvah: "Even when you're resting, you're busy in a misvah." Leisure trips. Can someone out all day on a Hol HaMoed trip eat lunch outside the sukkah as a traveller? Rav Ovadia Yosef and Rav Moshe Feinstein both say no: the exemption is for a journey with a purpose. But someone taking the train from London to Manchester to inspect a property may eat sandwiches on the train. Guards. City guards on duty by day are exempt by day and obligated at night, and the reverse. Guards of orchards are exempt day and night, since a fixed sukkah would show a thief where the guard is.

How to dwell in the sukkah

The misvah is to eat, drink and live in the sukkah all seven days, day and night, as one lives at home. The Gemara learns "day and night, seven days" from the kohanim at the inauguration of the Mishkan, at the end of Parashat Sav. Living means everything: phone calls, emails, relaxing, reading. With fine weather forecast in London: "there's absolutely no excuse to be at home reading the newspaper. Read the newspaper in the sukkah." The idea of the sukkah as a place only for meals, he explained, came from Tosafot and the Ashkenazim of northern Europe, where it was too cold to live outside. It also led to the custom of saying the berakhah only when eating, which he called a shame. In Israel people really live in the sukkah; in London it is harder, but when the weather allows, do everything there. | | The Rambam | Tosafot (northern Europe) | |---|---|---| | Heart of the misvah | Living in the sukkah, day and night | Eating and drinking | | When the berakhah is said | Whenever you come in to dwell | When you eat | Make the sukkah your main home: nice tablecloths, cutlery and cups, not plastic, unless you must carry everything down from a fourth-floor flat. Pots stay outside, and read simply the Rambam means plates too: once the meal is over, do not leave dirty plates in the sukkah. Candles belong inside, unless the sukkah is small and they would be a fire hazard.

Eating, the first night, learning and rain

Snacks and meals. One may not eat outside the sukkah all seven days except a casual snack, about a kabeisah. Since the Rambam says "all seven days", the Rabbi did not think he held that Shabbat turns even a small amount into a fixed meal. One may not sleep outside, even a nap. Water and fruit are fine outside. In his commentary to the Mishnah the Rambam defines a casual eating as something eaten between meals to settle one's hunger, like an apple while working. The poskim allow a full meal of meat and rice outside the sukkah if there is no kabeisah of bread; the Rabbi did not think the Rambam would agree. On his reading, sitting down to a full meal requires a sukkah even without bread; snacking through the day does not. Drinking even water only in the sukkah, as a sage in the Mishnah did, is praiseworthy: "a good humrah". The first night. Eating on the first night (and the second outside Israel) is an obligation, learnt from Massah on Pesah: "the fifteenth" here and there. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say one kazayit of bread is enough; the Mishnah Berurah says a kabeisah. To satisfy all views, eat a kabeisah of plain hallah within five or six minutes, then add salads and spreads. Learning and prayer. One reads in the sukkah, but someone studying in depth may learn outside so his mind is settled, or at home if he needs many books open. One may pray inside or outside. Rain. Go in when the rain would spoil a dish, not at the first drops. If it stops, you may finish the meal inside; the same applies to sleep. Practical summary

  • Children from about five or six, depending on the child, should sit in the sukkah for Hinukh.
  • Someone unwell, even with a slight cold, may eat and sleep indoors; the Rabbi would not rely on this on the first night.
  • Discomfort exempts only if it comes from outside; a sukkah that can never be lived in may not count even for eating.
  • A leisure day trip does not exempt you from eating in the sukkah; a purposeful journey does.
  • Live in the sukkah, not just eat there; in the Rabbi's reading of the Rambam a full meal needs a sukkah even without bread.
  • On the first night eat at least a kazayit of plain bread in the sukkah, ideally a kabeisah within a few minutes.
