A short follow-up

This is a brief morning recording, a few minutes long, adding "a few further points" to a talk on bedikat hames given the day before. It answers a question about books, adds the garden to the places that need checking, and explains the custom of putting out ten pieces of bread.

The principle carries over from the earlier talk: in the search we are looking for pieces of hames, roughly a kazayit, not crumbs. The exception is anything used together with food, which must be free of even tiny crumbs.

Do books need to be checked?

Books that may have crumbs in them do not need every crumb removed, since we are looking for pieces. But a book used together with food is different. If you read it at the table or in the kitchen during the year, and will do so again on Pesah, it needs checking for even the smallest crumbs.

This is why it is advised not to use the year-round booklets for Birkat HaMazon on Pesah. They have been used with food all year and will be used with food again. Unless every crumb is taken out of them, use separate ones.

The garden

The garden also needs checking. If hames is regularly brought into the garden, have a quick look around for pieces, again not crumbs. The garden is part of your property, and anything there could be eaten on Pesah.

The ten pieces: a debated custom

Many put out pieces of bread around the house before the search. The Rabbi explained that this custom was at first written against.

  • The Raavad, Rabbi Avraham ben David of Provence in the twelfth century, called it a custom of the unlearned. The purpose of the search is to begin removing the hames, which is why the berakhah is al biur hames, "on removing hames", not on searching. So even if you search and find nothing, you have done the misvah. "The misvah isn't to find hames, the misvah is to search," and to remove it the next day.
  • The Hakham Svi, of Amsterdam in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, who was influential among the Spanish and Portuguese community, also wrote that the custom is unnecessary and opposed it.

Others argue that today, when the house has been thoroughly cleaned long before the night of the search, the pieces are needed so that the search is not in vain. In practice the custom is to put them out. The number ten comes from the Arizal, and many Sephardim and Ashkenazim follow it.

Two points to keep

1. Each piece should be less than a kazayit. Pieces sometimes get lost. If each is less than a kazayit, it counts like crumbs, and you can rely on the nullification the next morning. "You don't have to turn the house upside down" to find it.

2. Search the house, not the ten pieces. "Unfortunately, this sometimes happens." The excitement turns into finding ten pieces, and we forget that we are meant to be searching the house. The pieces are meant to be found along the way while we look for any other hames. If finding them becomes the whole purpose, "it'd be better not to put these pieces away."

Practical summary

  • Books do not need to be cleared of crumbs unless they are used with food; use separate Birkat HaMazon booklets on Pesah.
  • Check the garden for pieces of hames if food is brought there.
  • Putting out ten pieces is an old custom, from the Arizal; the Raavad and the Hakham Svi opposed it.
  • Make each piece less than a kazayit, so a lost piece is no cause for worry.
  • The misvah is the search of the whole house, not finding the ten pieces.
