The source: the Torah, the Sifra and the Gemara

It happened to be the week of Parashat Emor. The Torah lists blemishes in an animal brought as a korban (crushed, crushed in another way, torn, cut) and then says "do not do this in your land" (Vayikra 22:24).

  • On a simple reading, the verse is about animals that could be brought as offerings, in Eres Yisrael.
  • The Sifra extends it widely: to every animal, even those with blemishes, and to every place, not only Eres Yisrael. The Rambam counts this as a Torah prohibition everywhere. The Sifra also applies it to humans.
  • The Sifra then quotes Rabbi Yehudah: there is no sirus for females. This applies to women, and the Rabbi said later that the commentators ask what it means for female animals.

The Rambam: direct castration

The Rambam writes that castrating a male of any species, human or animal, is forbidden. The three methods (crushing, detaching and cutting off) all carry malkot (lashes). It applies even if the victim is already unable to have children. If the organ has already been cut, to remove what remains still counts as sirus. Even a person who is physically incapable of having children is covered.

Indirect actions (grama): the drink and the cockerel's crest

The Gemara asks about taking a "drink" (kos ikarin) that causes sterility. This is not directly cutting an organ. The Gemara brings a proof from cutting off the crest of a rooster: it makes the rooster depressed so that it does not want to reproduce, and that is permitted. But the Gemara moves away from that proof, and concludes that taking the drink is still forbidden.

So the Gemara teaches that even doing something indirectly (grama) is forbidden. The Rambam rules: no malkot for it, because it is only derabbanan, not a Torah prohibition. The Tur adds that this applies even for medical reasons (except in real danger to life).

The important distinction. What is forbidden is anything that acts on the reproductive organs themselves, directly or indirectly. What is permitted is something that only weakens the desire to reproduce, without acting on the organs. This is the point of the crest. The Rabbi noted the Shiltei Gibborim, who permits putting a mark on a female animal to make her unattractive to the male. Nothing is done to the reproductive organs, only to desire.

He said this is the key question for vets today, when they give pills or hormones to reduce the animal's desire.

Seif 11: what the Shulhan Arukh rules

  • Castrating a male, whether a person or an animal, anywhere in the world, is forbidden by the Torah. The Shulhan Arukh lists each method (crushing, detaching, cutting) and says all carry malkot, whether the victim is a person, an animal or a bird.
  • Actively castrating a female is forbidden only derabbanan, according to the Shulhan Arukh. So Rabbi Yehudah's statement that there is no sirus for females is read as saying there is no Torah prohibition.
  • A woman may drink a kos ikarin, and one may give it to her.
  • The indirect act for a man (the drink) is forbidden derabbanan with no malkot.
  • Taking away the crest, which weakens desire and not the organs, is permitted.

The Rabbi said we do not follow the Bah: indirect action on a female is permitted, for people and for animals. The Shulhan Arukh's opening wording speaks of males. Doing something directly to the organs of a female, however, is rabbinically forbidden.

Pithei Teshuvah: extreme cases and the wife

  • An earlier class showed that someone who took a drink is a saris but may still marry, since his organs are intact. In a situation of danger that is not full danger to life, the Pithei Teshuvah suggests there may be room to permit a drink. The Rabbi gave an example as an illustration of the kind of case: a man with a severe mental illness whose doctors say another child would push him to suicide. He said that perhaps there is room to permit medication there.
  • Then a question about a woman who takes a kos ikarin because she fears bad children. The teshuvah says that if there is pain (sar), it is permitted, and it may even be permitted without it. But what about her husband's misvah of peru urevu (the Rabbi called it the misvah of "to inhabit the world", lashevet)? The teshuvah says she should ask her husband. If he does not agree, the teshuvah does not obligate her to have relations if it will cause her pain. In those days there were no regular birth-control pills that regulate easily, so the case was harder. The teshuvah concludes that it all depends on the judgment of the ruling authority (hakol lefi raut einei hamoreh).

Seif 13: asking a non-Jew to castrate your animal

The Gemara asks: can I ask a non-Jew to do it? This turns on whether the rule against telling a non-Jew to do work for you (amirah lenokhri) applies outside Shabbat. The Gemara tells of a case in Eres Yisrael where non-Jews stole oxen and castrated them, because castrated oxen work better. The answer given: they are doing a haramah (a sly arrangement), since the non-Jew knows the owner wants it. So the owner has to sell the animal and cannot use it for plowing.

Two approaches among the Rishonim. 1. The Rosh: the rule of amirah lenokhri applies to other prohibitions too, so asking a non-Jew to castrate is a rabbinic prohibition. 2. The second approach: amirah lenokhri applies only on Shabbat, and the problem in the Gemara was lifnei iver, because the people of Eres Yisrael held that the sons of Noah are commanded against castration. We do not hold that. According to this, there is room to permit asking a non-Jew.

The Rabbi noted the Rambam in Hilchot Lifnei Iver, who says the prohibition of lifnei iver applies to a non-Jew as well.

What the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say. - If the non-Jew does it on his own, without your involvement, it is permitted. - If the Jew gave a hint, even without saying it clearly, he must sell the animal. - The Tur adds that even without a hint, if the non-Jew knows the owner will be pleased, the owner may not keep the animal. This is a very loose kind of haramah, derived from the Gemara's "they stole it". - He may sell it to another Jew, even to his grown son, but not to his minor child, since that is like himself. - The Rema, following the Beit Yoseph: one may share an animal with a non-Jew; if the non-Jew decides on his own to castrate it, it is his own decision and permitted.

The Helkat Mehokek brings the Raavad, who follows Papa. The Rabbi's summary: the general consensus is that there is a prohibition, and we do not tell a non-Jew to castrate our animals.

A modern teshuvah on vets

With the remaining time, the Rabbi turned to a teshuvah about veterinary practice today. He explained that the great difficulty is that much of a vet's work is neutering and spaying. The teshuvah's principles, as the Rabbi summarized them: - Physically operating on a male animal's organs is forbidden. - Giving hormones that weaken the drive, or weaken the potency of the sperm, without touching the organs, is fine. - Something that prevents the act itself (kishui) is more difficult. The teshuvah is strict on that, because it is a physical interference and not only a hormonal or emotional one. The Rabbi discussed this with the class and suggested that it is better where the effect is not permanent. He noted that one authority is an outlier on this. - For female animals, indirect action is permitted, even if the effect is permanent, like a woman's drink, since we do not follow the Bah. But a direct physical act on the organs of a female is forbidden derabbanan. - All of this applies to asking a non-Jew too, because of amirah lenokhri.

He said this is a practical area where a dayan will be asked, and it needs expert medical input.

Practical summary

  • Castrating a male person or animal, directly, is a Torah prohibition anywhere in the world. Each method carries malkot.
  • An indirect act on the male's organs (a drink or a medication that affects them) is forbidden derabbanan, with no malkot.
  • Weakening only the desire to reproduce, without acting on the organs, is permitted.
  • For females, a direct act is rabbinically forbidden, but an indirect act is permitted for women and for female animals; we do not follow the Bah.
  • A non-Jew may not be asked to castrate your animal, and if he does it because you hinted, the animal must be sold.
  • Pills or hormones for a pet or farm animal need a careful look at what exactly they affect, and the Rabbi said we need expert medical input.
