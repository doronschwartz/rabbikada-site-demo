Topic: The afterlife in Jewish thought: Olam HaBa, Gan Eden, Gehinnom and karet Sources: The Rambam, Hilchot Teshuvah, Chapters 8-9; the Ramhal, Derech Hashem; Pirkei Avot 4:2 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Monday 23 May 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Five terms that people confuse

The Rabbi opened by going around the room and asking each person what Jews believe about the afterlife. The answers mixed together paradise, Gehinnom, judgment, Mashiah and the dead rising. That, he said, is exactly the problem: "this is probably one of the most confused topics."

So he set out the terms and asked everyone to write them down:

  • Mashiah, the Messiah.
  • Tehiyat HaMetim, the resurrection of the dead.
  • Gan Eden and Gehinnom, usually translated as paradise and hell.
  • Olam HaBa, the World to Come.
  • Gilgul, reincarnation, which he set aside for another time.

These are separate ideas, not synonyms. Many people think Gan Eden and Olam HaBa are the same thing; they are not. He also made a bold opening claim: compared with Olam HaBa and with Gan Eden and Gehinnom, Mashiah and Tehiyat HaMetim are "trivial". Everything he would say was based on the Rambam and on the Ramhal, Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto, in Derech Hashem. On one point the two disagree.

Throw out the bank account

Before describing the World to Come, the Rabbi asked his listeners to unlearn something. Because we have lived so long in exile, we often read the Torah through a non-Jewish lens, and the afterlife is the clearest example: for centuries it has been seen through Christian theology's simple picture of heaven or hell. The picture many of us grew up with is a bank account. A big misvah puts a hundred pounds in, a small one puts in a pound, a big sin takes a lot out, and at the end you are paid in paradise or hell.

"That's the first thing. You have to throw that out of your head." That is not how the classical Jewish philosophers understood reward.

The Torah's idea is put in Pirkei Avot, which the group had read the week before: the reward of a misvah is the misvah, and the result of a sin is the sin. He asked that we stop using the word punishment. Punishment suggests a vengeful God paying back a wrong. But God is entirely good, and He created us. Instead we should speak of consequences.

Olam HaBa is the identity you build

A human being is a hybrid. Our bodies are animal: selfish, hungry, driven by instinct. What sets us apart is the neshamah, the soul. All day long there is a tug of war between them. He gave the example of Neilah on Yom Kippur, where the body says eat and the soul says pray harder, and the everyday example of how we treat other people: instinctively and selfishly or in a way that is divine, giving and caring.

God gave us this struggle so that we make the choices and own the result. "Olam HaBa is the identity you have created for yourself in this world. Full stop." The more spiritual the life we have lived, the less of a barrier there is between us and God. The more physical and selfish the life, the thicker the barrier.

He gave two pictures:

  • The stadium. Almost everyone gets a ticket, but where do you sit? Some are right at the back, some are in the box at the front.
  • Meeting the Queen. (Her Platinum Jubilee was a few weeks away.) One guest has spent years learning royal protocol and how to behave; another has done nothing. The Queen will naturally spend longer with the first. She is not punishing the second guest. They simply do not click.

Several people pushed back: surely the person at the back is being punished? The Rabbi insisted that if it still sounds like punishment, he had not explained it well. "It's not a punishment. It's reality." Nobody is blocking anyone: it is about being able or unable to connect.

Someone asked whether a person who only started keeping misvot at thirty is worse off. He answered that it is about quality more than quantity. The Talmud speaks of a person who can change his whole identity in a single hour, and tells of a sinner who turned around completely just before he died and was declared to have a portion in the World to Come.

Body or soul?

Is Olam HaBa physical or spiritual? Here the Rambam and the Ramhal disagree. For the Rambam it is purely spiritual: the body stays in the ground, and the soul is who we really are. His analogy was an astronaut who spends ten years in a spacesuit and starts to think the suit is him. On earth we need hands and a mouth, but who we really are is our consciousness, our soul.

The Ramhal felt it unfair that the body, which served for 120 years, should not share in the result. But even he speaks of an elevated body, like that of Adam before the sin, without our present needs and urges. So in practice, the Rabbi said, either way it is essentially spiritual.

He added that the Rambam held that a person who never trains his mind to understand God has a limited share, since you cannot be close to what you do not understand. Someone asked whether that is fair to people who are not clever. He accepted that this is a real and separate question, and said he was using the point only to show that what we build in our minds is what we will be able to enjoy.

What about the rewards in the Shema?

Someone asked: if there is no reward, what about the second paragraph of the Shema, with rain in its season and plenty? The Rabbi said the Rambam asks exactly this question (Hilchot Teshuvah, Chapter 9), and the Gemara says plainly that there is no reward for misvot in this world. The blessings in the Torah are not payment. They free a person from labour so that he has more time to serve God and learn. As the verse says, "give to a wise person and he will become wiser still."

Asked whether kohanim will have a higher place, he said no: the order will follow each person's level. Non-Jews can be ahead of Jews.

He also pointed out that the much-loved line "All Israel have a share in the World to Come", said before Pirkei Avot, is only half a Mishnah. The Mishnah goes on to list those who do not.

Gan Eden, Gehinnom and karet

Olam HaBa is not yet here. It begins as a collective event at the end of this world, which is then recreated in a spiritual form; for the moment, he said, accept that it begins at the year 7,000. So where is the soul of someone who dies today?

  • Gan Eden is a waiting room, like the Garden of Eden before Adam sinned. It is not the eternal bliss of Olam HaBa, and a person's place there matches his future seat in Olam HaBa.
  • Gehinnom is a cleansing process, at most twelve months, which is why a child says Kaddish for a parent for eleven months and not twelve. It is the closest thing Judaism has to punishment, and even then it is a cleansing. He put it positively: if you are in Gehinnom, you already have a seat in the World to Come. Much of that cleansing can also happen through the troubles of this life; the Gemara counts even reaching into your pocket and pulling out the wrong coin as a small suffering.
  • Karet, being cut off, is the ultimate consequence: the soul simply does not continue. He read from the Rambam (Hilchot Teshuvah 8:1 and 8:5) that the reward of the righteous is to have this life and the retribution of the wicked is that they are cut off, and that the prophets' images of the pit and the fire are metaphors for this loss.

His analogy was a bruised apple. Some bruises are cut away: a month, three months, twelve months. But if the whole apple is rotten, you throw it out. That is karet, and someone who receives karet does not go to Gehinnom at all. In his closing review he called Gehinnom "a kind of security check before you get into the stadium."

All of this refers to a person's final identity at the end of life. Teshuvah is always possible until then. And cleansing can remove barriers, but it cannot give a person what he never built.

Someone asked how the judgment works: is it ten good deeds against ten bad ones? "There's no scales. Get out of it." It is a judgment of the whole person, though some sins are so serious that they damage a person's character unless he puts them right.

Tehiyat HaMetim, Mashiah and the judgment were left for the next session. He noted that the Rambam struggled most with resurrection: if the ultimate is spiritual, why bring the soul back into a body?

Summary

  • Mashiah, Tehiyat HaMetim, Gan Eden and Gehinnom, and Olam HaBa are separate ideas.
  • Reward and punishment are not a bank account. The reward of a misvah is the misvah; there are consequences, not revenge.
  • Olam HaBa is the closeness to God that matches the identity we have built. It is essentially spiritual.
  • Gan Eden is a waiting room, Gehinnom a cleansing of at most twelve months, and karet the end of the soul's existence.
