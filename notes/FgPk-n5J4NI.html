Why this topic, and a warning

The class was meant to be on another subject, but after the United States Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, the Rabbi felt it worth analysing what halacha actually says. The issue touches the sanctity of life on one side and personal autonomy on the other. When you really investigate it, he said, "you see how things are very nuanced", and anyone who claims Judaism is simply for one side or the other is mistaken. The class had three parts: the source of the prohibition, the stages of pregnancy, and specific cases.

He was emphatic at the end: nothing in the class should be applied in practice without advice. Anyone facing such a situation should "seek medical and rabbinical advice as soon as possible", because the sooner one asks, the more can be done.

Three ways to understand the prohibition

That killing a fetus is forbidden is clear. The Gemara (Sanhedrin 57b) forbids it to non-Jews, and since nothing is permitted to a Jew that is forbidden to a non-Jew, the Rishonim conclude it is forbidden to Jews as well. But what kind of prohibition is it? There are three possibilities:

  1. Murder, the most severe view.
  2. Destroying potential life, related to the misvah to have children and the ban on wasting seed.
  3. Havalah, injury, the least severe view: harming the mother, like any injury.

He then went through four sources:

  • Shemot 21:22. Two men fight and one strikes a pregnant woman, who miscarries. The punishment is financial. This suggests a matter of damages rather than homicide.
  • Mishnah Ohalot 7:6. When a birth endangers the mother, the fetus may be removed, "because her life comes before its life". Once its head or most of its body has emerged, it may not be touched, "for we do not set aside one life for another". Rashi explains that before birth it is not yet a full human being. The Rambam instead calls the fetus a rodef, a pursuer of the mother. Rav Moshe Feinstein read this to mean the fetus is a full life. The Arukh HaShulhan found this reading difficult, and the Ahiezer understood that even the Rambam meant only a birth already under way.
  • Arakhin 7a. A pregnant woman sentenced to death is not kept alive until she gives birth, unless she is already in labour. The Gemara calls this obvious, which is hard to understand if the fetus were a full life.
  • Shabbat. We violate Shabbat to save a fetus. Rav Moshe saw this as proof that it is a life; the Ramban explains it as "desecrate one Shabbat so that he can keep many", which suggests it is not yet a full life.

The overall picture, he said, is an intermediate status: the fetus may not be destroyed without justification, and we break Shabbat to save it, but it is not a full nefesh.

The opinions

  • Murder at every stage: Rav Moshe Feinstein and Rav Unterman.
  • Destroying potential life: Rav Uziel.
  • Injury: the Maharit, Rabbi Yosef Trani, and in our time the Sis Eliezer, Rav Eliezer Waldenberg. Rav Ovadia Yosef, the Rabbi said, does not hold it is murder.

Four to seven months

The Sis Eliezer permits for serious reasons, preferably by indirect means

From seven months

Only where the mother's life is at risk

Rav Ovadia's double doubt: perhaps the prohibition is only rabbinic; and even if it is biblical, many hold that it applies only once the fetus has formed, after three months. Before forty days the fetus is described as "mere water". The Sis Eliezer worked closely with Hadassah hospital and was a great authority in medical halacha; his line at seven months is linked to viability, which the Rabbi noted may be earlier today. He admitted he was unsure whether that means the beginning or the end of the seventh month.

Hard cases

The Rabbi stressed that "permitted" here never means for convenience. He is speaking of serious distress for the parents and the child.

  • Tay-Sachs, where a child will not live beyond early childhood. For Rav Moshe it would be forbidden. Yet if the mother's distress is so severe that there is a risk to her life, such as suicide, everyone agrees that saving the mother comes first. The Sis Eliezer permits until seven months; Rav Ovadia until three months. The Rabbi said this is the view of the majority.
  • Down syndrome is different, since the child will live. Rav Ovadia permits within three months and the Sis Eliezer until seven, in serious circumstances.
  • Rubella early in pregnancy, with a high chance of defect: the Sis Eliezer explicitly permitted it.
  • A pregnancy from a forbidden relationship. The Ben Ish Hai brought differing views, of the Havvot Yair and of the Yaavets, and leaned towards leniency because of the family's shame, without ruling. Rav Uziel permitted it.
  • Rape. Room for leniency, because of the trauma, and for a married woman also because of the child's status.
  • An unmarried woman who became pregnant: most poskim do not permit, though extreme circumstances might still be raised with a rav.

On doctors, he said Rav Ovadia required a religious doctor or else the agreement of two doctors, because a doctor's outlook on life shapes his advice. On testing during pregnancy, Rav Asher Weiss told him personally that there is nothing wrong with it, since there are cases where halacha permits an abortion.

Roe v. Wade and questions

The Rabbi's view was that Judaism strongly values life, but halacha permits abortion in a range of situations. A blanket ban may prevent some women from doing what halacha permits. When someone asked about those who welcomed the ruling because it would save many lives, he accepted that this may well be true and is a fair reason, and that the ruling sends a strong message about the value of life.

Asked whether a Jewish or non-Jewish doctor is preferable, he said he did not know. He also said many people do not know the details, because we are rightly taught from a young age how serious the prohibition is.

Summary

  • Abortion is forbidden, but the poskim differ over whether it is murder, the destruction of potential life, or an injury.
  • The Rabbi taught that "abortion is murder" is the view of Rav Moshe Feinstein and others, not the only Torah view.
  • Halacha weighs the stage of pregnancy: before forty days and before three months are treated much more leniently than later stages.
  • In serious cases the poskim differ widely, so anyone facing this should ask a rav and a doctor without delay.
