Topic: Kabbalat misvot, what a convert must accept, and how many people must be present at a conversion Source: Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268 Shiur: Third class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, Wednesday 12 May 2021 (Rosh Hodesh Sivan)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where the course has reached

The first class covered milah and tevilah, the two physical foundations of conversion. The second covered the preparation of the candidate and the motive: a conversion should be leshem shamayim, and in the first instance a Beit Din will not accept anyone whose motive is different, though bediavad, after the fact, such a conversion stands.

Tonight's subject is the third element. Even after milah and tevilah, the convert still needs kabbalat misvot, the acceptance of the misvot.

Accepting everything without knowing everything

The Gemara in Bekhorot (30b) is strict. A person who wants to become a haver, someone trusted on terumot and maaserot, and who says "I will keep everything except one thing," is not accepted. The same is true of a non-Jew who wants to accept the whole Torah except one thing. Even if the one thing he refuses is rabbinic, he cannot be converted. The Rabbi stressed that this is the halacha.

That seems to mean a convert must know everything. Yet other sources point the other way. The Gemara in Shabbat discusses a convert who was never taught the laws of Shabbat at all, so that he brings one korban for many violations. The Beit Yoseph quotes a ruling that a convert who was not told the misvot is still a convert, and the Darkei Moshe objects that this contradicts the requirement of kabbalat misvot. The same tension appears in the Rambam (Issurei Biah 13:17), who says that a convert who was not informed of the misvot is still a valid convert if milah and tevilah were done before three, while elsewhere he requires kabbalat misvot. The Shulhan Arukh (268:3 and 268:12) contains both statements.

Two later authorities, writing independently, gave the same answer: the Melamed LeHoil of Rav David Zvi Hoffmann in Germany, and the Hemdat Shlomo of Warsaw. One must distinguish between two things:

  • Kabbalat misvot, accepting in general to enter the Jewish faith and do everything the Torah requires. This is meakev: without it there is no conversion.
  • Hodaat misvot, informing the candidate of the details, how each misvah is done, its severity, its reward and punishment. This is not meakev.

Someone in the class put it simply: the convert says naaseh venishma, "I accept it all, whatever it is." That, said the Rabbi, is exactly the point. A person who has been taught very little but sincerely says "I accept everything a Jew must do" has a valid kabbalat misvot. A person who says "I will keep 612 misvot, but not this one" does not. The more a candidate learns the better, but there is always more to learn, and no one expects a convert to know everything before beginning a Jewish life.

When a candidate rejects one misvah

Rav Moshe Feinstein (Igrot Moshe, Yoreh Deah 3:106). A Beit Din in Holland asked about a non-Jewish woman married to a Jewish man. She was ready to accept everything except dressing modestly. Rav Moshe made two points. First, bediavad such a conversion would be valid. His proof: if rejecting even a rabbinic misvah invalidated a conversion after the fact, the person would be Jewish by Torah law but not by rabbinic law, and the Hakhamim would constantly have to annul such people's kiddushin. No one ever mentions such a thing, so the rule in Bekhorot must be lekhathilah. Second, he still would not allow it, because two problems come together here: a conversion for marriage and a rejected misvah. One leniency may be taken in a given case, but not two. The rabbis of Holland were right to refuse her.

He then considers a further thought. Perhaps she believes modest dress is only a humrah, because her religious Jewish friends do not dress that way. If so, she is really accepting all the misvot. Even so, he concludes that it is better not to rely on this.

The Ahiezer (Rav Hayim Ozer Grodzinski). He disagrees with Rav Moshe in one direction and is more lenient in another. Rejecting a misvah does invalidate a conversion even bediavad. But this applies only to someone who rejects the misvah in principle. A person who believes it is right, intends to keep it, and admits she is not yet ready, for example to cover her hair, or to wait six hours between meat and milk rather than one, has accepted the misvah. This does not extend to the central pillars of Jewish identity such as Shabbat and kashrut. That, said the Rabbi, is the difference between such a candidate and the Reform and Conservative approach, which rejects misvot ideologically.

Someone asked how this fits with the declaration at the mikveh. The Rabbi answered that no Beit Din would convert someone who says there "I accept all the misvot except." The case is a candidate who accepts everything and has said in conversation that one misvah is hard for her.

Judged at the moment of conversion

Kabbalat misvot is assessed at the time of the conversion itself. Rav Moshe was asked about a woman who converted, had children, and after a bitter divorce claimed she had never meant to accept the misvot. He ruled that devarim shebalev einam devarim, unspoken thoughts do not count. A conversion can be cancelled retroactively only with an umdena demukhah, clear evidence that at the time of conversion there was no acceptance. The Rabbi cited a case reported at a Beit Din conference: a woman in Israel was converted midweek, and it emerged that she already had a flight booked for that Shabbat. If, however, someone simply stops keeping the misvot later, the conversion stands.

This explains a policy in England, followed by the London Beth Din and by his own Beit Din: the conversion certificate is issued only a year after conversion, so that if evidence emerges that the whole process was deception, it can still be addressed. It is also why good batei din meet candidates every few months, to see that they are learning.

Questions from the class

  • Rejecting korbanot. If a view has someone to rely on, it is not a rejection. The Rambam describes sacrifices as a way of weaning the people away from idolatry, even though he rules that they will be restored. A candidate who follows a recognised opinion cannot be refused for it.
  • Rejecting the principles of faith. That is different. The Radbaz and the Sefer HaIkarim counted the principles differently, but no one held that a principle may be denied outright. A person who wants Jewish practice without its beliefs cannot be accepted.
  • Should a candidate keep Shabbat before converting? There is a debate, since a non-Jew may not keep Shabbat fully. The practice suggested today is to keep Shabbat completely but end it ten to fifteen minutes early, not just five, which would only be the added time of Shabbat.

How many people must be present

The Gemara in Yevamot (46b) requires three for a convert, because the Torah uses the word mishpat, judgment. Yet a page earlier, the Gemara suggests that a person whose parent was seen immersing for niddah or keri may be presumed to have converted, which implies that three are not needed.

For the Rif and the Rambam, seeing someone live as a Jew is enough to accept their children, but not the person himself. The Shulhan Arukh brings the first view without names and then the second. Everyone agrees, the Rabbi stressed, that kabbalat misvot needs three, and this will matter when discussing Reform and Conservative conversions.

Who are the three? The Gemara speaks of talmidei hakhamim, but the Shulhan Arukh does not say this. The words in brackets in the printed text are neither Maran nor the Rema, and later (268:12) he writes that three hedyotot, laypeople, suffice. Where he says dayanim, the Rabbi explained, he means people who understand conversion.

Practical summary

  • A convert must accept all the misvot in principle. He or she does not need to know them all before converting.
  • Rejecting a misvah blocks a conversion lekhathilah. Rav Moshe holds that bediavad it is still valid; the Ahiezer holds that an ideological rejection invalidates it, but not an honest "I am not ready yet."
  • Acceptance is judged at the moment of conversion. Only clear evidence from that moment can undo it, not later behaviour.
  • Kabbalat misvot needs three people according to all opinions. Whether milah and tevilah need three bediavad is disputed.
  • The three need not be rabbis or formal dayanim, but should understand what conversion involves.
