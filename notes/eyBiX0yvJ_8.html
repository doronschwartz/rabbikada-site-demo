Sefer: Derech Hashem by Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto (the Ramhal) Section: Part 1, Chapter 4, paragraph 5 (continued discussion) Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Derech Hashem series at Tiferet Eyal, Wednesday 17 February 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

This recording continues the previous one, from the same evening. It is an open discussion with a small group arising from paragraph 5: if the misvot are designed to perfect us, why do women have different obligations?

If the world does not end in the year 6000

The recording opens on the question of the messianic deadline. A participant asked: what if the year 6000 comes and Mashiah has not come? The Rabbi answered that then we would know the Rambam was right. For the Rambam there is no end to the universe; the Gemara's statement that the world lasts six thousand years is one opinion, but not the consensus. In the Rambam's view we continue as pure souls, without a body.

Back to the question: what about women?

A participant returned to the earlier question: if halacha is stuck in this moment, does that mean women can never reach that perfection?

The Rabbi called this the question he began with, and brought "a beautiful piece" of Rav Kook. Where was man created from? From the earth. Where was woman created from? From Adam, at least according to one interpretation. Man came from the ground, the inanimate and the mundane, while woman was created from a human being, a step further removed from the physical. So a woman's physical side is loftier, and a closer reflection of the spiritual world from which it derives.

Rav Kook supports this from what we see around us. Generally speaking (the Rabbi stressed that this is a generalisation with exceptions, such as a doctor who gets used to blood), more women than men are repelled by the crude physical side of the world. Far fewer women than men are murderers or criminals. The Rabbi said the Maharal had already made this point, and Rav Kook elaborates it: women are closer to Hakadosh Barukh Hu than men.

"I really believe in this. I don't think this is apologetic." In his view women are generally more refined, kinder and less selfish than men.

His personal view on raising children

The Rabbi said that, for this reason, he strongly believes it is the mother who should raise children, with her altruistic capacity to show love to babies and children. He expressed regret that mothers often return to work a few months after birth, or that fathers stay home instead. A woman should have a career, he said, but not at the expense of being, most importantly, a mother. He stressed that this was his personal opinion, based on Rav Kook and the Maharal.

The group pushes back

The participants challenged this vigorously:

  • People might equally argue that women are more materialistic, caring more about clothes and accessories.
  • Gender roles may be conditioned by society rather than nature, and men may be prevented by that conditioning from being as nurturing.
  • If women are more spiritual, why have men been at the forefront of religion for two thousand years, and why are the main protagonists of the Torah men?
  • Spirituality is intangible and hard to measure; one participant said she did not see women as "born more holy" compared to her peers, though she agreed men and women process emotions differently.
  • When many of these laws were set, women had to be with their children constantly; science and modern life have changed that.

The Rabbi answered some of these points. Women were historically kept at home, which is why they were not at the forefront. Sarah was a prophet, and Hashem told Avraham, "Whatever Sarah tells you, listen to her voice." He also asked that the word "spiritual" be set aside as misleading. The purpose of creation is to resemble Hashem: as He is compassionate, be compassionate; as He is kind, be kind. Generally speaking, women are more compassionate and more giving, and in that sense more similar to the Divine. A participant replied that this picks only some attributes, and that men might be stronger or more likely to lead.

He agreed that biological differences between men and women are real, and that there is no question that there are differences in how men and women generally think.

The sin of Adam and progress

Why are men the protagonists? The Rabbi said that since the sin of Adam, women have been placed in a subordinate role, but that the world is evolving and progressing into a better society where this is no longer so. "The whole concept of women's equality is an excellent evolution and extreme progression." He linked this to his earlier point that many results of the sin of Adam are being undone as the world moves towards the times of Mashiah and becomes more perfected.

He added that today rabbis do much pastoral work, and he strongly believes women would be better suited to many such roles.

Living within the system today

A participant asked what to say to Orthodox women who are told that much in the Gemara reflects its time but cannot be changed. The Rabbi said the answer is to agree with them: "You're right. It's a real problem. And that's the reason why we're so desperate for Mashiah to come and for the Sanhedrin to be rebuilt."

Practically, if women want to do more misvot, "great, fantastic, why not." He cited Rav Moshe Feinstein, who wrote that if a woman wants to wear sisit out of true appreciation of Torah, not as a feminist protest against the Torah's fairness, that is acceptable. The problem arises only when it comes from a belief that the Torah was unfair to women.

Asked whether women now need these misvot to become more divine, he said he was not sure they need them. As we come closer to messianic times the world is progressing in a positive way, and some of these things may no longer be needed for women or for men.

The group ran out of time as the next session arrived. The Rabbi acknowledged the class had become sidetracked, "but it was worthwhile," and promised to send the group the text of Rav Kook.

Summary

  • If the year 6000 passes without Mashiah, that would support the Rambam, for whom the world has no end.
  • Following Rav Kook and the Maharal, the Rabbi argued that women, created from Adam rather than the earth, are generally more refined and more like Hashem in compassion and giving.
  • Participants strongly challenged this, raising social conditioning, the difficulty of measuring spirituality and the prominence of men in the Torah.
  • The Rabbi sees women's equality as a positive development and part of the world's progress towards the messianic era.
  • Women who wish to take on more misvot out of love of Torah should be encouraged; the deeper changes await a Sanhedrin.
