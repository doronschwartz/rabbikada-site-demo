Topic: The five names of Shavuot, the stages in which the Torah was given, and three ways to understand "the Torah was given at Sinai" Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal, on Zoom, Tuesday 27 April 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The festival with five names

This was the second class in a Shavuot series. The week before had been Pirkei Avot, and the next week would be modern conversion questions, in preparation for Ruth.

Shavuot has more names than any other festival. Pesah has four: Pesah, Hag HaMassot, Hag HaAviv, and in the tefillah, zeman herutenu. Sukkot has four: Sukkot, Hag HaAsif, zeman simhatenu, and simply HeHag in the book of Melakhim. Shavuot has five:

  1. Hag HaShavuot, the festival of weeks (Reeh).
  2. Hag HaKasir, the festival of harvest (Mishpatim).
  3. Yom HaBikkurim, the day of first fruits (Pinehas).
  4. Aseret, the name used by Hazal in the Mishnah. It means to hold back. Every other festival has its own misvah: shofar, lulav and sukkah, massah, the fast. Shavuot has no intrinsic misvah of its own beyond refraining from work. Cheesecake and the other customs are lovely, but the real feature of the day is the stop.
  5. Zeman Matan Toratenu, the time of the giving of our Torah, in the tefillah.

In the Torah, Shavuot is purely agricultural: thanking Hashem for the harvest. As that became less central, the dimension of the giving of the Torah was added. The earliest mention the Rabbi knows is the dispute in Shabbat: the Hakhamim say the Torah was given on the sixth of Sivan, the day of Shavuot; Rabbi Yose says the seventh.

He also distinguished three uses of the word Torah: the five books; all twenty-four books of the written law, as opposed to the Mishnah; and in the broadest sense, the whole body of Torah ideas, as when we say "I'm going to a shiur."

The day of the Revelation

The Rambam's eighth principle says the whole Torah came to Moshe from Hashem, "in a manner metaphorically called speech," and that Moshe wrote it like a scribe taking dictation. "He doesn't say, though, that all of Torah was given to Moshe at Har Sinai."

To see what was given when, we go to the verses. The Spanish and Portuguese read Shemot 19 and the end of Mishpatim with a special ornate tune, because both are central to this story. In chapter 19 the people arrive at Sinai on Rosh Hodesh Sivan; in chapter 20 they hear the Ten Commandments. After the first two, they could not bear to hear Hashem directly, and Moshe relayed the other eight.

Straight after come the laws of Mishpatim. Then in chapter 24 Moshe tells the people all the words of Hashem, they accept them, and he writes them down. Rashi alone reads this chapter as earlier than the Revelation. His own grandson the Rashbam, Ibn Ezra and the Ramban all read it in order. So on the day itself, Moshe received the Ten Commandments and the laws of Mishpatim, taught them to the people in broad terms, and wrote down the Torah from Bereshit to that point. Perhaps he only began writing that day, the Rabbi suggested; he also had to teach.

Moshe's three ascents

Then Hashem says, "Come up to Me on the mountain, and I will give you the tablets of stone, and the Torah and the misvah." Moshe did not need forty days to collect tablets. The Gemara in Berakhot explains that "the Torah and the misvah" means all the interpretation of the Torah. Rashi on Behar says the details of every misvah were given at Sinai, just like Shemitah.

Then the calf. Moshe breaks the tablets and the next day goes up for a second forty days to plead for the people. He comes down and is told to carve new tablets and go up again. A participant worked out the date he returns: Yom Kippur, which is why it became the day of atonement and new beginnings.

So in 120 days Moshe spent perhaps a day or two with the people, and had no chance to teach anything. Only after Yom Kippur, with his face shining, did he begin. Rashi describes the order: Moshe taught Aharon, then Aharon's sons, then the elders, then all Israel, so the people heard it once and the leaders more times. Over the following months he was also given new material, such as the whole book of Vayikra, which opens with Hashem calling to Moshe. The people left Sinai on the twentieth of Iyar of the next year (Bemidbar 10:11), after almost a full year there.

Even then, more came on the journey: hallah in Shelah, the sacrifices in Pinehas, Pesah Sheni. The last verse of Bemidbar says these were commanded "in the plains of Moav."

Three stages and three interpretations

So the Rabbi summarised three stages: the day of Shavuot, with the Ten Commandments and Mishpatim; the months at Sinai, with the interpretations and Vayikra; and the forty years after, until the plains of Moav.

What then do we mean by "the Torah was given at Sinai"? He offered three possibilities.

  1. Most of the Torah was given in the vicinity of Sinai, during that year, though not on the day of Shavuot and not all of it.
  2. The Ramhal in Daat Tevunot: the Torah was not all given at the Revelation. What happened there was that we were given the kelim, the vessels, the spiritual capacity to receive the Torah over the time that followed.
  3. Saadiah Gaon, quoted by Rashi in Mishpatim: all 613 misvot are hinted in the Ten Commandments. We could not yet extract them, but since the tablets were given at Sinai, it is as if the whole Torah was.

Writing it down

The Gemara in Gittin records two views. Rabbi Yohanan: "the Torah was given scroll by scroll," written section by section and joined at the end. He brings David's words, "in the scroll of the book it is written of me." The Rabbi explained the background, also a Shavuot theme: Shaul and Doeg cast doubt on David's lineage because of Ruth the Moabite, but the Torah forbids only a Moabite man, not a Moabite woman, and David found he was already hinted at in the Torah. Resh Lakish: the Torah was given sealed, written at the Revelation and then completed at the end of Moshe's life. Either way, this is about when it was written, not when it was given. On the last eight verses, the Gemara in Bava Batra says either Yehoshua wrote them, or Moshe wrote them in tears.

"Torah is a process," he concluded. Moshe had to work for it, and so did the people. It does not arrive overnight on a plate.

Questions from the class

  • "We died at each commandment": the Midrash means the people were overwhelmed and lost consciousness, and that concerns only the first two commandments.
  • Misvot given earlier: asked whether Shabbat and other laws given at Marah, before Sinai, undermine the third view, he said those were a few specific laws, not a body of literature, and had nothing to do with Shavuot.
  • "Zeman": he liked the translation "season" of the giving of the Torah, since it was the beginning of a season of learning and transmission.
  • The nations: to the claim that Hashem had promised the Torah to Avraham, he answered that the promise to Avraham was about his descendants, not the Torah. Whether the Midrash of offering the Torah to other nations "happened," and what it means for Hashem to address a nation, needs thought; he pointed to the Ramhal in Derech Hashem, section 2, chapter 4.

Summary

  • Shavuot has five names; in the Torah it is a harvest festival, and Hazal added the giving of the Torah.
  • On the day itself Israel received the Ten Commandments and Mishpatim; the interpretation came only after Moshe's three ascents, from Yom Kippur on.
  • Vayikra and much else were given later at Sinai, and more in the plains of Moav.
  • "Given at Sinai" can mean the year at Sinai, the spiritual preparation (the Ramhal), or the whole Torah latent in the Ten Commandments.
  • Torah is a process of learning and teaching, and Shavuot prepares us for it.
