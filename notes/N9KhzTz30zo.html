What do we mean by "mazal tov"?

Dayan Kada titled the final class of the series "Faith and Folly", after a book by Rabbi Yaakov Hillel. The aim was to look at things we say and do all the time without thinking about what they mean. He opened with an example he promised to return to: we wish each other mazal tov. What does it mean?

The Torah's prohibitions

The Torah says lo tenahashu velo teonenu (Vayikra 19:26). Rashi, followed by almost everyone, explains nihush as deciding what to do from chance events in nature: a piece of bread falls from your mouth, or a black cat crosses your path, so you put off your journey until tomorrow. Onen means believing that certain times or dates are lucky or unlucky for starting something. Both are Torah prohibitions.

He gave a modern example. If a hotel offers you room 13 on the thirteenth floor and you turn it down because it is unlucky, that would seemingly fall under this prohibition.

Parashat Shofetim (Devarim 18) adds divination, witchcraft and consulting the dead, and ends tamim tihyeh im Hashem Elokekha, be wholehearted with Hashem your God. Rashi explains: walk with Him wholeheartedly and look only to Him. Do not try to find out the future; accept whatever comes with simple faith, and then you are with Hashem and in His portion. Someone who lives by omens has left Hashem's portion, as it were.

Tamim does not mean naive, he said. It means living without superstitions, without thinking "if I wear this, that will happen." He asked everyone to think about red strings on the wrist, or the widespread belief that wearing something blue wards off the evil eye. On what we have seen so far, such things seem to be attempts to manipulate fate rather than walking with Hashem.

The Gemara in Pesahim (113b) learns from tamim tihyeh that one may not consult astrologers about the future. So if mazal tov means "good luck" or "a good constellation", it seems to be a problem. Someone suggested it simply means congratulations. That is what almost all of us mean, he said, and that is absolutely fine. But where does the phrase come from?

Mazal in the Gemara

The Gemara in Moed Katan (28a) records Rava's remarkable statement that life, children and livelihood depend not on merit but on mazal. Someone suggested that mazal here might mean one's God-given lot. He agreed that is a possible reading.

But the Gemara in Shabbat (156a) goes much further. A person born on a Monday will be short-tempered, because on that day the waters were divided, a day of strife. One born on Wednesday will be wise, since the lights were created that day. One born under Mars will shed blood, so he should become a shohet or a mohel and use that temperament for good. Then Rabbi Yohanan says ein mazal leYisrael, Israel is not subject to mazal, and the Gemara brings many sages who agree.

Even according to those who hold there is mazal, he said, it can at most give a general picture of a person's temperament. Everyone agrees that we have free will. Someone born with a hot temper cannot say "this is who I am, you can't blame me." That goes against every principle of the Torah. The Rambam calls free will a great fundamental, and in Shemonah Perakim he explains that people are born with different temperaments, and that a hot-tempered person faces a harder test. Hashem takes that into account, but the choice is always his.

The Rambam: there is nothing in it

The Rambam wrote a famous letter on astrology to the sages of Montpellier, around 1194, about ten years before his death. He is unequivocal. Astrology is not forbidden because it works and Hashem does not want us to use it. It is forbidden because it has no truth at all. In the Mishneh Torah (Hilchot Avodah Zarah 11) he writes that these were the practices of the ancient idolaters, and that anyone who thinks they have real power but are forbidden by the Torah lacks understanding. The Torah forbade them because they are empty.

At the same time he praises astronomy, which he knew well; his laws of sanctifying the new moon depend on it.

What does he do with the statements in Hazal? Near the end of the letter he writes that he knows one can find statements by individual sages that a person's nature depends on his star. But a person should not abandon the roots for the branches, or abandon clear reason. Perhaps those individuals were mistaken on that point, or meant something deeper, or were answering people according to their own beliefs. "Don't ever throw your intellect away," because your eyes are in front of your head, not behind it.

The Rambam does this throughout his works whenever something goes against reason. The Gemara says a warning before a transgression is valid even if it comes from a shed, a demon; the Rambam explains that the witness heard the voice of someone he could not see. The Mishnah we say on Friday night exempts someone who puts out a lamp mipnei ruah raah, because of an evil spirit; the Rambam explains it as a person suffering from melancholy who cannot bear light. For the Rambam, we do not live in an enchanted world. So for him, Dayan Kada said, mazal tov as "good constellation" makes no sense at all.

The Ramban: there is something in it

The Ramban disagrees. He holds that astrology does have some effect. Everyone agrees that one must not seek out astrologers or fortune tellers. But suppose someone offers you unsolicited advice: "I can see that next Monday will be bad for you; stay at home." According to the Rambam, staying home because of this would be forbidden by the Torah. According to the Ramban, once the information has come to you, it would be forbidden to ignore it and go out, because one may not rely on a miracle. Rather, you should increase your merits and become a better person.

The Shulhan Arukh (Yoreh Deah 179) rules that one may not consult astrologers or fortune tellers. Yet the next halachah records customs, in the Rema's gloss: not to begin things on Mondays and Wednesdays, to marry when the moon is waxing, and to begin new studies on Rosh Hodesh. Although divination is forbidden, yesh siman, there is a sign.

Living directly with Hashem

How does it all fit together? He gave an explanation he recalled from the Rishonim (he thought from the Ibn Ezra, or possibly Rabbenu Bahya). Mazal comes from nozel, to flow. Even those who believed in astrology held that everything comes from Hashem; they believed His influence flows through intermediaries, like the same sunlight looking different through different coloured glasses. A person who lives with these fears is affected by them. But one who lives directly with Hashem, believing that nothing happens against His will, bypasses all of it. That is ein mazal leYisrael, and that is the pure faith we all aspire to. Rav Moshe Feinstein said the same about the evil eye: if you believe in it, it affects you; if you ignore it, it does not. The same applies to the Gemara's warnings about pairs, which people no longer worry about.

Red strings and amulets are a person's own choice, he said, but he believes the Rambam would forbid them, and in any case they take us away from our direct relationship with Hashem. "It's far easier" to tie on a string or wear an amulet than to work on one's relationship with Hashem, and that is exactly the danger.

He illustrated this with the golden calf. Moshe broke the Luhot because he saw that a people ready to dance around a calf would one day turn the Luhot themselves into an object of worship. That is why the breaking of the Luhot and the breach of Jerusalem's walls are mourned together on Shivah Asar BeTammuz. Even the Beit HaMikdash was meant to direct our attention to Hashem, not to replace Him. The Kotel, too, is a place where people have focused on Hashem for centuries, not a wall with power of its own. If a string or anything else is a reminder to think of Hashem, like our sisit, "wear anything."

The same is true of the simanim on Rosh Hashanah. The Meiri says that eating dates or pomegranates does not bring a good year. A siman sets us off on the right foot, focusing our attention on what we want the coming year to be about. That is how he understands yesh siman in the Shulhan Arukh.

Summary

  • The Torah forbids deciding by omens and lucky times, and commands tamim tihyeh: walk wholeheartedly with Hashem.
  • The Gemara contains statements about mazal, but also Rabbi Yohanan's ruling ein mazal leYisrael, and everyone agrees we have free will.
  • The Rambam holds that astrology is nonsense; the Ramban holds it has some effect, but both say to stay away from it.
  • A person who lives directly with Hashem is not affected by mazal or omens; objects and simanim are valuable only as reminders that turn our attention to Him.
