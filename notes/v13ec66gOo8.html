Topic: What to do if you arrive late and the congregation has already accepted Shabbat, mistaken acceptance, and the laws of reading by candlelight Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263:15-16 and Siman 275 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 4 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (early 2014, estimated).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Arriving late: can you still pray Mincha?

The class continues the rule that on Friday, a minority of the community follows the majority once they have accepted Shabbat, even if there is a long time until nightfall. The Shulhan Arukh (263:15) applies this to prayer. If you arrive late, the congregation has already accepted Shabbat and you have not yet prayed Mincha, you should not pray Mincha in the same hall. Go outside, or upstairs to the women's section, and pray there. This is only if you yourself have not accepted Shabbat.

For a large shul with two minyanim, say 7.30 and 8.30 in the summer, there is no problem. The problem is praying Mincha in the same place as a congregation that is accepting Shabbat. If the first minyan has finished and gone home, a new minyan can pray Mincha and accept Shabbat afterwards.

If you arrive just as they are about to accept Shabbat, start Mincha at once. You may continue even though they say Barekhu while you are in the middle, because you started at the right time. Do not wait until they say Barukh Hu. The Mishnah Berurah rules that you should begin and not wait. If you know you will not finish before they accept Shabbat, it is a further stringency to go outside; if you think you can finish, you may stay.

The Rabbi explained why the majority rule does not stop you praying. That rule is only about doing melakhah. For prayer, even if the whole congregation has accepted Shabbat, you can still pray the weekday prayer as long as you have not accepted Shabbat yourself.

What if you already answered "Barukh Hu"?

If you have answered Barekhu, you have accepted Shabbat. You should then pray Arvit first, for Shabbat, and afterwards a second prayer as a make-up for Mincha. If you did them in the wrong order, you are still included, because it is still day. The Kaf HaHayim says that even if you prayed the full weekday text, you have fulfilled your obligation, since it is still daytime.

The Rabbi gave three views on whether answering Barekhu is itself acceptance. The Kaf HaHayim says that if you answer with the intention of not accepting Shabbat, it does not count. Another view is that you should not answer at all, and a third is that if you do answer you must say out loud that you are not accepting. His advice: it is best not to answer. If you do want to answer, have in mind, and say, that you are not accepting Shabbat. If you answered by mistake, acceptance by mistake is not acceptance. If you then need to do a melakhah, you can rely on the Kaf HaHayim as long as you had in mind not to accept.

Asking someone else to do melakhah, and Motzaei Shabbat

Even someone who has accepted Shabbat may, according to the Rema, ask another Jew who has not accepted Shabbat to do a melakhah, and enjoy the result. A similar case: after Shabbat, someone who has not yet said Havdalah, or who is still eating a long seudah, can ask a friend who has already prayed Arvit to cook more food. The reason is that on Friday you have no choice once you accepted Shabbat, but on Motzaei Shabbat you can end Shabbat yourself by saying "Barukh HaMavdil". This does not work if one person keeps Rabbenu Tam's time and the other does not.

If you say "Barukh HaMavdil" to cook and then bless after your meal, there is a doubt whether to say "Retseh", because this is not a full Havdalah. The poskim advise hearing the blessing from someone who did not do melakhah. Someone asked about a Purim meal that runs into the night. The Rabbi said that Purim and Hanukkah, unlike Shabbat, cannot extend their sanctity into the weekday, so Sefaradim do not say "Al HaNissim" in that case, while Ashkenazim, who go by when the meal began, do.

On Motzaei Shabbat the minority does not follow the majority. Even if most of the town has not ended Shabbat, you can say Havdalah and do melakhah. The Arukh HaShulhan explains that this time already has the status of a weekday, like the time before Plag HaMincha, when a congregation that accepted early would not bind you. The Rabbi admitted this is hard to follow.

Mistaken acceptance

The Shulhan Arukh discusses a cloudy day in which the congregation thought it was night, prayed Arvit, and then the sun came out. They do not repeat Arvit. Today the same question arises if someone reads the calendar wrongly. If the whole congregation prayed Arvit after Plag HaMincha, it is enough. An individual who normally prays Arvit only at nightfall must pray it again. If he normally prays Arvit after Plag during the week, he is fine.

As for melakhah, a mistaken acceptance does not forbid it. For a woman who accepted Shabbat by lighting candles, the Mishnah Berurah is stricter, and she should refrain if she can. But if there is a need, she may rely on the first opinion in the Shulhan Arukh.

After lighting: the candles become mukseh

Once candles are lit for Shabbat, they become mukseh because of the misvah. You cannot add oil, and you cannot move them. The Rabbi noted a further stringency, from the Ben Ish Hai, that you may not even touch them, in case you add oil. The Mishnah Berurah does not rule this way. Because mukseh applies to everyone, the husband may not move the candles his wife lit.

Siman 275: what you may not do by candlelight

The class moved to Siman 275. In the days of the Gemara people checked their clothes for lice by candlelight. The Shulhan Arukh forbids this and also reading, even silently. The concern is that you might tilt the wick to get more oil, which is the melakhah of mav'ir (adding to a fire). The Gemara tells of Rabbi Yishmael, who was sure he would not forget, and did.

The prohibition applies to a candle high up, to one in a box, and, according to Maran, even to one locked in a box, and by day or night, for example in a dark basement. It applies only to something that needs concentration (iyun), such as reading a book or checking tsitsit, not to ordinary acts such as clearing the table or looking at a watch with clear hands.

Wax candles: you might cut the burnt black wick, which is "mekhabeh". The Mishnah Berurah quotes many who say wax is only a rabbinic prohibition and relies on them. The Sefaradi poskim follow Maran and forbid it, and tea lights that melt into liquid are certainly like oil.

Electricity is different. There is no concern that you will increase the light. With a dimmer, the Rabbi explained that the worry was always that the flame would weaken and you would fix it. If you want to be stringent, put a note on the switch, though relying on the note alone is not enough for an oil candle. If a street lamp shines in and gives enough light to read by, you may read even with an oil candle there. Paraffin candles stay constant, so the poskim are more lenient.

Two people reading together (275:2)

It is permitted for two people to read by one candle if they are studying the same subject, because each will remind the other. Maran allows this even from two separate books. The Rema requires the same book. Ashkenazim follow the Rema; the Ben Ish Hai says to be stringent but to rely on Maran when needed for Torah study. The Magen Avraham limits the allowance to Torah or Shabbat matters, while the Taz says any reading is fine. The Rema shows that the prohibition applies even in a large group.

If one person is with you, he can be your shomer (275:3), but you must ask him to watch you. Without an explicit request, even a large household is not enough. A wife or child can be your shomer, but must be in the same room.

Practical summary

  • If you arrive after the congregation accepted Shabbat and you have not yet prayed Mincha, pray outside the hall, and do not answer Barekhu.
  • If you answer Barekhu, have in mind not to accept Shabbat; if you did accept, pray Arvit first and then the second prayer.
  • Mistaken acceptance of Shabbat does not forbid melakhah.
  • Candles lit for Shabbat are mukseh. Do not move or add oil, and note that the Ben Ish Hai also forbids touching.
  • Do not read near an oil or wax candle on Shabbat, unless there is other light or you have a shomer. Electric light is permitted.
  • Two people may read together by candlelight if studying the same subject, according to Sefaradi practice.
