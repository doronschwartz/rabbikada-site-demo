Back to the Torah reading

After the break for the Yamim Noraim, the class returned to the laws of the Torah reading, begun in the summer: the order of Kohen and Levi, what happens when the wrong person is called, and taking a Sefer Torah to people in prison. "This is a topic which you have to know on your fingertips."

Who gets the aliyot? (Siman 136)

After the Kohen and Levi, the Shulhan Arukh gives an order of honour:

  1. Hakhamim who hold positions of leadership.
  2. Hakhamim fit to be appointed.
  3. Sons of hakhamim.
  4. The heads of the community: presidents, treasurers and the like.
  5. Everyone else.

"So the president comes after the rabbi, not vice versa. Just in case anyone had any doubts about that."

Why is this not followed today? The Mishnah Berurah says this order applies where aliyot are not sold. Selling aliyot is a very old custom, and where money is involved for sedakah, whoever pays is called, even before a hakham. Of course there must be balance, so that the same people are not called every week; but when there is a choice, this is the order.

Which aliyah is the greatest? The third, the first Israel, except on days with one extra aliyah, like Rosh Hodesh, when the fourth is special. The third is the main aliyah among the Ashkenazim and the Spanish and Portuguese. In Kabbalah, the sixth is the most important, which is why in this synagogue the sixth is normally given to the hakham.

Competing claims. On a Monday with only one aliyah to give, who comes first? A bridegroom always comes first. Then the people involved in a berit milah, then a yahrzeit. A bar misvah is at the bottom of the list, since celebrating a bar misvah is relatively new.

Common sense is needed. Rav Ovadia Yoseph discusses twin bar misvah boys on a Monday, both Israelim, with Kohanim present who cannot be asked to leave; he allows a fourth aliyah. "I'm not suggesting that we do that on a regular basis," but if there will be a dispute, the rabbi and wardens must use their judgement. The same applies to a man who supports the synagogue, and to a member who comes every day compared with someone who walks in off the street. The halacha itself says that to be part of this order you must have lived in the town for thirty days.

Ten verses, three for each (Siman 137)

The Rabbi began with something "very important": the divisions of the parashah into aliyot (Rishon, Sheni and so on) were never fixed. They are very modern, which is why the Spanish and Portuguese often stop in different places.

The rules:

  • Ten verses at least on a day of three aliyot, such as Monday and Thursday. Reasons given are the Ten Commandments or the ten utterances of Creation.
  • Nine is enough when the topic ends after nine. The one case is the passage of Amalek read on Purim: exactly nine verses, three for each aliyah.
  • Three verses at least for each person. "There's no exception to this rule. You will never call up someone when it's less than three verses." Whoever reads four of the ten is praiseworthy.

On Purim it became the custom to repeat the last verse to make ten. The Rema records that the Ashkenazim do not, and the Rabbi noted that one could argue nine is right, since the Rishonim explain that the Amalek passage has nine verses because God's Name is incomplete while Amalek exists. So if the reader did not repeat the last verse, do not go back.

A missed verse

The reader skipped a verse and no one noticed in time. What now?

Day

If the verse was missed

Monday, Thursday, Shabbat afternoon

Only go back if fewer than ten verses were read

Yom Tov

Same: as long as ten verses on the theme of the day were read

Shabbat morning

Go back and read it, even after the Torah was returned and even after Musaf

Why is Shabbat morning different? On Shabbat there is a quota: the weekly parashah, so that the whole Torah is finished each year. On weekdays and Yom Tov the purpose is to read from the Torah, or about the festival, and there is no quota beyond ten verses. When going back, someone is called up with the berakhot, and the missed verse is read with others around it to make three.

Someone asked about the lockdown, when whole parashiyot were missed. There was no choice then. "You can't be expected to catch up everything."

Repeating verses

If the second person reads exactly what the first read, he counts as one of the seven only if he adds three new verses, or two where nothing else is possible. The Rabbi showed how we rely on this every Rosh Hodesh: the Kohen reads three verses, and the Levi repeats one and adds two new ones, because the passage does not divide any other way.

The exception is Sukkot, when the same offering is read again for each aliyah of Hol HaMoed, because there is no other way to do it.

Once the seven are complete, additional aliyot may repeat verses. This matters more than people think. An additional aliyah that repeats the same verses keeps the same number; one that reads new verses becomes the next aliyah. So if someone paid for the sixth aliyah and the gabbai adds extra aliyot after the fifth reading new verses, he is no longer getting the sixth. The Rabbi's own preference is to add new verses each time rather than repeat, since during repeated additions people tend to talk.

He described a common mistake in the Spanish and Portuguese community, where a bar misvah boy is usually called fifth and reads his portion. When the family adds aliyot by splitting the first portion, the boy is no longer fifth. Better to add the extra aliyot afterwards.

Never stop near the end of a paragraph (Siman 138)

You may not stop fewer than three verses before the end of a parashah, and you may not begin a new aliyah fewer than three verses before the end. The reason given: people leaving the synagogue would say the next person is reading only two verses.

"Parashah" here means a paragraph of the Sefer Torah, open or closed, marked in a Humash with a peh or samekh. There are 669 of them in the Torah. It does not mean the weekly portion.

Using the siddur, the class worked through that morning's Rosh Hodesh reading. Dividing it as three, three and four new verses looks neater, but it would mean stopping two verses before a peh, which is forbidden. That is why the Levi repeats a verse instead.

The Rabbi remembered learning this the hard way. At fifteen or sixteen, reading extra aliyot in Parashat Ki Tese, which has many short paragraphs, he stopped two verses before the end of one, and the rabbi stopped him and told him to continue. A paragraph that is only two verses long is no problem; you may stop at its end.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that it is good to avoid stopping two verses before the printed end of Rishon or Sheni, because people mistake those for paragraph breaks. The Rabbi explained that this is a stringency of the Mishnah Berurah's own. It is a good thing, but strictly it is permitted, and he sometimes does it when he needs to balance short aliyot.

Begin and end on something good

Finally, it is nice to begin and end each aliyah on a good subject. "It's not halacha. It's a humra." Many books mark exactly that stop.

His lesson: "Trust me, embarrassing him is probably going to be much worse" than any bad omen from where the aliyah ends. "Just remember that next time you want to correct someone."

Practical summary

  • After Kohen and Levi, aliyot go to hakhamim, then community leaders, then everyone; where aliyot are sold, whoever pays. A bridegroom comes before a berit, a yahrzeit and a bar misvah.
  • At least ten verses must be read, and each aliyah must have at least three new verses (two on Rosh Hodesh, by repeating one).
  • A missed verse on Shabbat morning must be read again, even after Musaf; on weekdays and Yom Tov only if fewer than ten verses were read.
  • Never stop or start fewer than three verses before the end of a paragraph of the Sefer Torah.
  • Beginning and ending on something good is a nicety. Never embarrass a reader over it.
