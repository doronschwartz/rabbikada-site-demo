Five different Kaddishes

Dayan Kada began by saying that Kaddish is one of the most misunderstood prayers. Most people think it is something said for a dead person, but there are five kinds, and only one is tied to death.

  • Half Kaddish. The oldest and simplest form. It separates one part of the service from the next: after the Zemirot, after the Amidah, after the Torah reading and haftarah, before Musaf. It has nothing to do with mourners.
  • Kaddish Shalem (Titkabal). Said once at the end of every service. Titkabal means "accept", the root of kabbalah: please accept our prayers.
  • Kaddish Yehe Shelamah. Said after verses of Tanakh, for example after the psalm of the day.
  • Kaddish DeRabbanan, the Rabbis' Kaddish. Said after rabbinic teaching, which is why it follows the passages of Mishnah and Talmud near the end of the Shabbat service, and why one would say it after a class if there were a minyan. It prays for the teachers and students of Torah.
  • The burial Kaddish. Said at a cemetery and at a siyum on a tractate of the Talmud, in the same form. Asked who says it at a burial, he answered that to the best of his knowledge it is anyone sitting shiva, whether for a first or second parent.

Kaddish is in Aramaic except for its last paragraph, which is Hebrew, which is why people stumble over it.

Where did it come from?

He stressed a habit he tries to teach: always ask whether something is from the Torah, from the Rabbis, or a custom. Waiting between meat and dairy is rabbinic, a fence around a biblical prohibition. Matzah on the first night of Pesah is biblical, on the second night rabbinic. Kitniyot is a custom. "Rabbinic" means the law of the Talmud, which has a status that rabbis today do not have.

Kaddish is not in the Torah or the rest of Tanakh. Its first mention is at the end of Sotah, which asks what keeps the world going despite all the troubles before the Messiah. The answer: the Kedushah of the Sidra and "Yehe shemei rabba" said after the study of aggadah. The Talmud, sealed around 500 CE, never mentions mourners saying Kaddish.

The earliest source he knew for mourners saying it is Mahzor Vitri, an eleventh-century French prayer book by a disciple of Rashi, who followed Rav Amram Gaon's ninth-century siddur, the first formal one. It tells how Rabbi Akiva met a man in a cemetery who was being punished. The man said he could be released only if his son led the congregation in "Barekhu" and they answered "Yehe shemei rabba". Rabbi Akiva found the boy, who had not even been circumcised, taught him Torah, and the boy led the prayer. The father then appeared to Rabbi Akiva in a dream to thank him. The author concludes: therefore the custom developed for a son to lead the prayers on Saturday night, when the souls are said to return. He noted that the story is not in the Talmud, though medieval sources repeat it.

From there the custom grew:

  • The Or Zarua (Rabbi Yitzhak of Vienna, thirteenth century) says it was now said also at the end of Shabbat morning prayers.
  • The Rif and the Rambam say nothing about mourners reciting Kaddish.
  • Rabbi Yoseph Karo records the custom in the Beit Yoseph, but not in the Shulhan Arukh.
  • The Rema, in his glosses, is the first to put it in a formal code: a son says the last Kaddish and leads the prayers.

He was clear that he was not saying this to play Kaddish down: "I want you to understand how things have come about." It is not biblical and not rabbinic. It is a custom, an important and powerful one, which began on Saturday nights, spread to every service, and began with the Rabbis' Kaddish. That is why it is called the mourners' Kaddish.

What Kaddish means

"Kaddish is a prayer of the living." There is no word in it about death. It says: may His great name be magnified and sanctified.

He tied this to the unity of God. Shema says God is one, which means that nothing in the world runs without His will, with no second power of darkness set against light. That is why the Shema blessings open with the one God who forms light and creates darkness. But we live in a time of hester panim, when God's hand is hidden, and it is easy to think people run the world. If we really lived with this belief we would not hold grudges, but in practice it is very hard.

Kaddish is the prayer that this unity will become clear to everyone, as we say on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. Shema is said quietly, with eyes covered, because we believe it but cannot see it. Kaddish asks for the day when it will be plain.

Why say it for the dead? A person who has died can no longer sanctify God's name. But the Talmud has the idea that a child can be a merit to his parents. "The apple does not fall far from the tree." A child who cares about the sanctification of God's name shows what the parent left in the world. "It is not some kind of magical prayer."

A participant asked why Shema would not do. He answered that Shema is a statement we must live by, while Kaddish prays for the day it is visible. Asked whether it is said "on behalf of" the dead, he said we cannot do misvot for a person once they have died, so what we say is for ourselves, but it reflects on the one who raised us. He added that many authorities see it as honouring one's parents, which the Talmud says one can do after death, and that it gives the mourner a moment each day to remember.

The twelve months, and who says it

The Rema writes that Kaddish is said for eleven months, so as not to suggest that a parent was wicked, since the judgement of the wicked is said to last twelve months. Customs differ: some Sephardi and Iraqi communities take a break of a week or more within the year. Mourning itself, he noted, is halakhah and not custom.

After the year one can still say it. "It is a myth" that one cannot. It is also said on the yahrzeit. Asked whether one may say Kaddish while one's own parents are alive, he said that since it is not only a mourners' prayer there is nothing against it, but because the custom has spread, it is recommended to get one's parents' permission.

The original custom was for a son. Over the last few centuries it came to include a daughter, siblings and spouses, probably first when there was no child. A child saying it remains the most powerful, because it shows the legacy.

Standing and sitting

The Rambam writes that one sits for Kaddish. The Spanish and Portuguese follow this, and sit even when Kaddish has started. The Eastern Sephardim, following the Arizal, add that if you are already standing, do not sit down. The Rema, for Ashkenazim, ruled that one stands, since it is such an important prayer, although the older Ashkenazi custom was to stand only at "Yehe shemei rabba". Standing is required for the Amidah and, in practice, Aleinu, and for a Sefer Torah in transit but not one standing in its place. One who says it, like the leader of the service, always stands.

The rule is to follow the custom of the place you are in. "If everyone is seated, you stay seated with them," and in an Ashkenazi shul, do not stand out.

Women saying Kaddish

The question was first discussed in the seventeenth century. Since the Rabbis often group women with children, and children may say it, why not women? Three views arose: why not; it is immodest for a woman to speak before men; and a woman may say it only in a minyan at home. These date from when one mourner at a time said it, which is still the custom in some German communities, so a single woman in front of all the men was thought inappropriate. Today all the mourners say it together. Mainstream authorities hold that where men are saying it too, a woman may say it with them, but alone she should ask a man to say it with her. When different people go at different speeds, you answer the one nearest you.

Kaddish, like Kedushah, Barekhu and taking out the Sefer Torah, is a davar shebikdushah and needs ten men. "Ten men means ten men."

Summary

  • There are five Kaddishes, and only the burial Kaddish is for mourners.
  • The mourners' Kaddish is a custom that grew over centuries, from Mahzor Vitri through the Or Zarua and the Maharil to the Rema.
  • Its words are about the sanctification and unity of God. It is a prayer of the living.
  • A child saying it shows the legacy a parent left and honours the parent.
  • Many details, such as standing, the months, and who says it, are custom: follow the place you are in.
