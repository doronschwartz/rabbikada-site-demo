Topic: The berakhah on seeing a non-Jewish king, and who counts as a king today

Sources: The Talmud (Rav Sheshet); Rambam; Shulhan Arukh; Hakham Ovadiah Yoseph

Daily Halacha: Peculiar Berachot, Wednesday 29 January 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The halacha

  • The Talmud gives a berakhah on seeing a Jewish king, not relevant today, and one on a non-Jewish king, "shenatan mikevodo levasar vadam", which the Rambam and Shulhan Arukh record for today. King and queen are equal.
  • Unlike the other berakhot, it may be said by a blind person or without seeing the monarch: the blind Amora Rav Sheshet said it, sensing the presence, pomp and ceremony, and could tell when the king arrived.
  • The Hakhamim said it is a misvah to try to see kings, even non-Jewish ones, and even allowed certain things otherwise forbidden for it; the Shulhan Arukh records this misvah.
  • It must be in person, seeing them or present among the entourage, never over television.
  • Hakham Ovadiah concludes that a king is one with power over life: to execute or to pardon from death. King Charles has the title but is more a head of state without these powers, so one would not say the berakhah on him in full.
  • The President of the USA lacks the title but can pardon even those on death row, so one says the berakhah.
  • Hakham Ovadiah did not say it on seeing President Nixon in Israel, because he was in ordinary dress without the pomp. At last week's inauguration of Donald Trump, present in the hall and sensing the ceremony, one would say it.
  • One with the title of king but none of the traditional functions: say the berakhah without Hashem's name, because of the doubt.

In one line

The berakhah on royalty is said in person on one with power to pardon from death, seen in state, while a titular king gets it without Hashem's name.
