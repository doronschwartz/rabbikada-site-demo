Topic: The first principle: the existence of the Creator, what it means, and the reasons for believing it Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 1:1-4; Moreh Nevuchim Shiur: Second class of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series at Tiferet Eyal on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Tuesday 19 November 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Defining God before believing in Him

After a show of hands on moving the start time (an equal vote, so it stayed at 7.45), the Rabbi began the first principle, the existence of God. "This is probably the most important of all the 13 principles," since nothing else can be discussed without it. Tonight, he said, he was only discussing a primal force before the universe, not yet the God of the Torah.

The Rambam does not just say "believe in the Creator". He spends several lines defining Him, because someone who believes God is a pencil, or an old man with a white beard, has not understood what God is. The Rabbi found five ideas in the Rambam's words: 1. There is a Creator, the cause of all that exists. 2. He is perfect. An imperfect God is a contradiction in terms. 3. He is self-sufficient, dependent on nothing and no one. 4. Everything else depends on Him. 5. His existence is necessary. Mount Everest happens to exist; God must exist.

The Mishneh Torah opens the same way: "The foundation of all foundations and the pillar of wisdom is to know that there is a First Being." If everything else ceased, He would remain. "Hashem Elohim emet": He alone is true, meaning He alone exists necessarily.

Perfection, and the "impossible"

If God is perfect, why do the righteous suffer? That question belongs to principle 11. But the Rabbi warned against one popular answer, from a book of the 1980s, that God wants good but lacks the power. For the Rambam that is not a weaker belief in God; it is not belief in God at all.

Then the old puzzle: can God create a stone He cannot lift, or a square whose diagonal equals its side? Someone answered that the question contains a contradiction. Exactly, said the Rabbi. Not being able to do the impossible is not an imperfection. The Rambam writes that we do not call a man weak because he cannot move a hundred thousand loads, nor God incapable because He cannot make Himself a body. Rabbi Saadiah Gaon had said the same.

Rabbi Avraham Azulai, the grandfather of the Hida, added a fine distinction: God can do anything that is impossible according to nature, since He made nature, but not what is impossible according to reason. He even advised not to say this among Christians, since it undermines belief in the Trinity, and a mistaken belief in God is better than none. A few, like Rabbi Nahman of Breslov, are reported to have said God can make a triangle a square. But the accepted view is that this is no imperfection.

Self-sufficient, and sustaining everything

"Adon olam asher malakh beterem kol yesir nivra": He was King before anything was created. God does not need us to be King, and He does not need our prayers. People sometimes say "Hashem needs our prayers"; the Rabbi cautioned that this cannot be meant literally. Prayer transforms us. He added that English words like "worship" can mislead us about what these ideas mean.

Everything, on the other hand, depends on Him at every moment. A silversmith who makes a cup can walk away; the cup still exists. But a ball thrown in the air falls as soon as the energy stops. So too the world: without God's constant input it would cease to exist. That is what we say each morning: He renews creation every day.

The Yigdal's first line holds four of the five ideas: "Exalted be the living God", a Creator, the source of all life, "His existence is unbounded by time", perfect and dependent on nothing. The Ani Maamin adds that He "alone has made, does make and will make" all things.

Someone asked whether God controls every event, even the Holocaust. The Rabbi answered that natural events are in God's hands, but what one person does to another is free will. As Rabbi Sacks put it: when people ask where God was in the Holocaust, the answer is, where was man? He noted that the Rambam holds some things in nature are left to chance, while the Ramban held that everything is under providence.

Can God's existence be proved?

The prophets never urge people to believe in God; they urge them to keep Shabbat and do justice. Belief was taken for granted. Proofs only became important in the Middle Ages, in response to challenges.

We never demand absolute proof in life; even for the gravest verdicts we ask for proof beyond reasonable doubt. The Rabbi said we can bring proofs beyond reasonable doubt that Hashem exists, and very strong indications that are very difficult to refute.

He also warned about hidden motives. Aldous Huxley admitted late in life that he and his friends wanted the world to have no meaning because it freed them from a morality that "interfered with our sexual freedom". The Talmud makes a similar point about idolatry. The Rabbi stressed that this is not true of every person who doubts, but we all have unconscious biases.

The first cause

The cosmological argument appears in Rabbi Saadiah Gaon, the Rambam, Rabbenu Bahya and Rabbi Yoseph Albo. This table had a maker, he had parents, they had parents, and so on: there must be a first cause.

The obvious reply: if everything needs a cause, why stop at God? Why not an infinite universe, as Aristotle held? The Rambam disagreed with Aristotle on this, though he wrote that if he had believed in an eternal universe, he would have reinterpreted Bereshit. Even this reply, the Rabbi noted, only brings you from atheism to agnosticism: God or an infinite universe, fifty-fifty.

The deeper form of the argument has three steps: 1. Everything in the universe is physical and finite. 2. What is physical and finite must have a cause, and cannot be infinitely old. 3. So there must be a first cause that is neither physical nor finite.

His key point: what we call "infinite" is usually just "indefinitely extendable". A phone doubling in size every second would soon be bigger than the universe, but it would always have a size and a weight. Every number is finite; the series just goes on. Time too: each hour is finite. The closest idea to true infinity is zero, which has no size or shape, but zero does not exist. God is like that, but exists. A universe, however vast or old, still has dimensions, so it cannot be the first cause. He added that modern physics, since the discovery that the universe is expanding, also accepts that it had a beginning.

How can something beyond space and matter create them? He shared an analogy from Rabbi Daniel Rowe. Imagine a perfect world in your mind; then someone asks you a hard sum, and the world vanishes. The universe exists, as it were, in God's thought, and needs Him to keep thinking of it. "Every second He wants us," he said: that is the depth of hashgahah peratit.

Design, and feeling

The second argument is from design. The world is so precise that randomness is hard to believe. The Rambam says that contemplating creation leads to love of God, and the Midrash says Avraham found God by asking who runs the palace. Rabbenu Bahya compares the world to a handwritten letter: it could be ink spilled by a dog chasing a cat, but who would believe that? Even if someone accepts evolution, the Rabbi said, there must be a mind behind it and an explanation for the first matter.

He ended with feeling. Rabbi Soloveitchik quotes Kierkegaard: does a bride in her bridegroom's arms need proof that she is loved? Descartes said we can only be sure that we ourselves exist, yet nobody in the room doubted the Rabbi existed. "Taste and see that God is good." Someone immersed in Torah, prayer and misvot simply feels it.

His closing parable, from Rabbi Shochet: two twins in the womb argue about life after birth. "Have you ever seen Mother?" asks one. The other answers: be quiet and listen, and you will feel Mother's hand. "I believe that there is a truth out there... and you just have to try to listen, and it's there."

Summary

  • The first principle requires understanding who God is: a Creator, perfect, self-sufficient, the source of all, and necessary.
  • God's inability to do the logically impossible is no imperfection.
  • God needs nothing, not even our prayers; the world needs Him every moment.
  • There is no absolute proof, but the first-cause and design arguments establish God beyond reasonable doubt.
  • Ultimately, emunah is also felt, through a life of Torah, prayer and misvot.
