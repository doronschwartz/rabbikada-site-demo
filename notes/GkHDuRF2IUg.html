Topic: How the Rosh Hashanah simanim fit with the Torah's prohibition of superstition

Sources: The Gemara (Abaye; Hullin); the Torah ("lo tenahashu"); the Rambam; the Ramban; the Raavad; the Meiri; Shulhan Arukh

Daily Halacha: Rosh Hashanah, Monday 30 August 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • The Rosh Hashanah Seder comes from the Gemara: Abaye says that since an omen has significance, one should eat on Rosh Hashanah squash, beans, leeks, spinach and dates (as commonly understood).
  • Yet the Torah forbids superstitious practice, "lo tenahashu", the ways of the pagans. The Rambam holds such practices have no power but are forbidden as gentile ways; the Ramban holds they may have power, but the Torah forbids them so we direct ourselves to HaKadosh Barukh Hu. Either way it is a Torah prohibition.
  • The Rambam reads the Gemara in Hullin as distinguishing past from future. Reflecting that success came since buying a home, marrying or buying an animal is permitted; using an omen to predict the future is forbidden.
  • So the Rambam holds that Eliezer's sign for choosing a wife for Yishak, the girl who would offer water, was forbidden after the Torah was given. The Raavad disagrees.
  • The simanim must not be seen as determining the year. The Meiri, about 100 years after the Rambam, explains they arouse us to teshuvah: "our enemies" being cut off are our sins, and the good things are our misvot.
  • Notably the Rambam omits the simanim altogether; the Shulhan Arukh includes them, hence the near universal custom.
  • We should use them not superstitiously, but to stir teshuvah and start the year well.

In one line

The simanim are not omens that decide the year but prompts to teshuvah and a good start.
