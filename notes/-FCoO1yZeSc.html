Four parts to "Torah from heaven"

After principles 6 and 7 on prophecy, the class reached the Torah itself: principle 8, that it is from heaven, and next time principle 9, that it does not change.

The Rabbi set out the Rambam's text in four paragraphs: 1. The Torah we have today is the Torah given to Moshe. 2. Moshe wrote it like a scribe taking dictation, every narrative and law. The Torah calls him "mehokek", which can mean a copyist. 3. Every verse is equally divine. "And Timna was a concubine" is as holy as "I am Hashem your God" or "Shema Yisrael". The Rambam says King Menasheh's sin was to think the Torah had "a kernel and a husk". We pray, "Open my eyes, that I may see wonders from Your Torah." 4. The Oral Law is equally divine. What sukkah, lulav, shofar, sisit and tefillin are is just as God commanded Moshe.

The Rabbi found the fourth part striking: you cannot believe in a divine written Torah without its divine interpretation. The Ani Maamin, "the whole Torah now in our hands is the one given to Moshe", covers the first three parts but not the Oral Law, unless "the whole Torah" includes it. The Yigdal, "God gave His people a Torah of truth through His prophet", covers mainly the second.

Part one: the same Torah

This affirms the Masoretic text, used by every synagogue in the world, over other versions. The Samaritan text, for example, says God finished His work "on the sixth day" instead of "on the seventh". The principle concerns the words themselves. It does not cover the crowns on the letters, which the Rambam himself says were no longer known precisely, nor the cantillation notes, whose age is debated, nor the large and small letters.

Even with the words, the Talmud (Kiddushin) says the earlier generations were expert in "full and defective spellings" and "we are not". Yaakov can be spelled with or without a vav, "hosetikha" with or without a yud. These make no difference to meaning, and today every Sefer Torah in every community is identical. The one known exception is a single letter in the Yemenite scrolls, and "the exception proves the rule". That is remarkable after so many exiles and cultures.

But because of that doubt, a Sefer Torah with such a spelling error is not replaced, as we cannot be sure the other is better. The Shaagat Aryeh even held that the misvah to write a Sefer Torah can no longer be fulfilled exactly, and the Hatam Sofer explained that no berakhah was instituted on writing one because of this uncertainty. So the Rambam cannot mean the exact spelling. He means the words.

The Talmud (Bava Batra) also asks how Moshe could write "and Moshe died there". Rabbi Yehudah says Yehoshua wrote the last eight verses. Rabbi Shimon says God dictated and Moshe wrote with tears. Some say those eight verses have a slightly different status.

Part two: is the Torah from God?

"There'll never be conclusive proof," the Rabbi repeated, but we look for the most rational explanation, proof beyond reasonable doubt. His main argument was the Kuzari's.

Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi, the 12th-century Spanish poet and philosopher ("my heart is in the East and I am at the edge of the West"), made aliyah and died soon after. In his book, the king of the Khazars questions a Christian and a Muslim, is unconvinced, and finally asks a Jew. The rabbi answers: "I believe in the God of Avraham, Yishak and Yisrael, who brought Israel out of Egypt with signs and wonders." The king is unimpressed: I asked about the Creator of heaven and earth and you tell me about Egypt?

That is exactly the point. No one witnessed creation, and claims like a miraculous birth are by nature unprovable, private events. But the Torah says, as in last week's parashah, "You yourselves have seen what I did to Egypt." Rashi: I am not sending you this through messengers or witnesses; you saw it yourselves.

So consider the alternative. The Torah is a masterpiece that has shaped humanity; if not from God, someone fabricated it. The Rabbi worked through the possibilities: - A brilliant forger wrote a highly ethical book (far more ethical than Hammurabi's code, as its slave laws show), all while lying. Possible, but why would anyone accept it? It demands that you be stoned for lighting a fire on Shabbat, and it has laws like kashrut that seem strange even to believers. - A charismatic leader persuaded them. But "you can sell people things about other people... you cannot sell things to people about themselves which they know isn't true." A demagogue can persuade a nation that others are vermin, he said, but not that it was itself liberated from slavery when it never was. No one can tell two million people "you saw the sea split" if they did not. - A document "found" later, about events long ago. Then people would ask how such miracles could have been forgotten, and the book itself says it will never be forgotten. - A group conspiracy passed down to their children. People pass on beliefs because they think them true. No one passes on what he knows is a lie.

No other religion, among thousands, has ever claimed that God revealed Himself to an entire nation. The Rambam writes in his Letter to Yemen: "make this great spectacle of the revelation appeal to the imagination of your children... for this event is the pivot of our faith and the proof of its truth."

He mentioned other, more specific proofs that people discuss: no fish has been found with scales and no fins; the Torah names the only four animals with just one kosher sign. These can be debated. For him personally, one of the strongest is the return to the Land. Imagine Britain conquered and its people scattered across the world; after a thousand years, what British customs or laws would remain? Yet the Torah and the prophets said we would return, and in the last 70 years we have seen it begin.

Parts three and four

Every part of the Torah is equally holy, with the possible exception of the last eight verses. And the Oral Torah is inseparable from the written. The Torah never says that tefillin are square and black, or defines the "work" forbidden on Shabbat. Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch compared the written Torah to lecture notes: clear only to those who heard the lecture.

Why are there disputes in the Oral Law? The Rambam explains that before Hillel and Shammai there were almost none. Disagreements arose when students did not serve their teachers enough, and multiplied with each generation. The Sadducees, who rejected the Oral Law, left nothing behind.

He closed with Rabbi Sacks: "I wonder if any people has ever loved a book as we love the Torah. We stand when it passes as if it were a king, we dance with it as if it were a bride..." And with the Baal Shem Tov: the Jewish people are a living Sefer Torah, and every Jew is one of its letters. If God made the world, there must be a manual, and studying it is the closest we come to the divine mind.

Summary

  • The eighth principle has four parts: the same Torah, dictated by God, equally holy throughout, and the divine Oral Law with it.
  • "The same Torah" means the words; the Talmud admits doubt about some spellings, which change no meaning.
  • The Kuzari's argument: the Torah tells a whole nation "you yourselves saw", a claim no forger could make and no other religion has made.
  • The Rambam calls the revelation at Sinai "the pivot of our faith", to be taught vividly to our children.
  • The written Torah cannot be understood without the Oral Torah; disputes arose only as transmission weakened.
