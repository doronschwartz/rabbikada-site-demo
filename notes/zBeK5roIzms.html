Topic: Seven limitations on a father's power to declare his child a mamzer

Sources: The Rambam; the Rishonim; Tosafot Rid; the Gemara; Shulhan Arukh; the later authorities

Daily Halacha: Even HaEzer, Monday 15 June 2026

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The halacha

  • One: some say he is believed only while recognising another child as his firstborn.
  • Two: the Rambam understood it applies only to a fetus. A born child has a presumption of kashrut (hezkat kashrut) that the father's word cannot override.
  • Three: some Rishonim hold that once he says the child is not his, he no longer has the power to disqualify him. The Shulhan Arukh does not seem to follow this.
  • Four, a minority view of Tosafot Rid, with a proof from the Gemara: he is believed only if the mother does not contradict him, since she knows better. In divorce cases she always will.
  • Five, clear in the Gemara: once he has said "this is my child", for example when enrolling him in a school or nursery, he cannot retract without a convincing explanation (amatla).
  • Six: he must be certain (taanat barei), for example that he was not with his wife at the time. "I don't think it is mine" is not enough.
  • Seven, in the later authorities, though debatable: yakir is a law of testimony, so he is not believed where he has a self-interest (negiah), such as avoiding maintenance.
  • Almost all these points are debated, but their combination means the Batei Din in Israel have consistently rejected such claims. Only where none applies, with a certain claim and no self-interest, might the father perhaps be believed and the child become a mamzer.

Sources quoted

  • The Rambam: only a fetus; a born child has a presumption of kashrut.

  • The Gemara: he cannot retract without an amatla.

In one line

Combined limitations mean the Batei Din almost never accept a divorcing father's claim that his child is a mamzer.
