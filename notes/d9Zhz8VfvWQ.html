Part A — Melaben (Cleaning)

Definition

In the Mishkan, sheared wool had to be cleaned before it could be spun. Melaben is cleaning the fabric itself — removing something absorbed into the material. Removing something sitting on the surface (e.g. feathers all over your clothes) is not cleaning — permitted.

Three stages — each a separate Torah prohibition

  1. Soaking in water — begins releasing the dirt.
  2. Scrubbing — dislodges it.
  3. Squeezing out the water.

Even if two stages happened before Shabbat or by themselves, the third is still forbidden. So: don't run the tap onto a dirty shirt in the sink; don't put water on a stain; if rain fell on a stain, don't scrub it; don't wring out a wet garment.

Soaking a clean garment

Some Rishonim hold that soaking even a clean garment adds cleanliness. Halacha: soaking a clean garment is permitted.

  • A clean towel may be soaked to cool a headache.
  • A clean towel may be used to wipe up spilt water — but not a dirty (soiled) one.
  • Don't saturate it so much that lifting it inevitably squeezes water out — use another towel; a soaked towel is also a concern because one might come to wring it out.
  • Paper towels/tissues may be soaked completely and thrown away — but don't squeeze them while lifting.

Coloured spills

As discussed last week, a bleeding finger may be wrapped in a white towel (better to rinse under the tap first, but strictly permitted). For red juice or wine spills, prefer paper tissues to a reusable towel or handkerchief — disposables raise no colouring question. With no alternative, a towel may be used.

Removing a stain without water

Dried mud on clothes: is scraping it off Tochen (grinding)? One opinion says no — earth was already "ground" (ein tochen achar tochen, like crumbling a biscuit) — but we don't rely on that. Rather:

  • Removing what is on top of the fabric with a fingernail — permitted by all.
  • The stain remaining in the fabric — preferably ask a non-Jew to scrape it with a nail. If none is available and you need to look presentable in public, there is room to scrape it yourself.
  • Never with water — and you may not ask a non-Jew to use water (a Torah prohibition), except in extreme circumstances.
  • Dry mud on shoes may likewise be scraped off — wet mud is a different matter.

Dust on clothes

A hat, jacket or dress that fell on a dusty floor: for Sephardim, dust sits on the surface, so one may shake or brush it off — even with a clothes brush. Since one view (followed by Ashkenazim) considers it a Torah prohibition, preferably do it with a shinui, e.g. the left hand. Brushing a hat is fine.

Spreading out wet clothes

Chazal forbade spreading out wet clothes to dry — because of mar'it ayin (onlookers may think you washed them on Shabbat) — and mar'it ayin applies even in private (afilu bechadrei chadarim asur). So no spreading on a banister, airer or washing line, even indoors.

  • Clipping a garment with one peg so it just hangs down is not "spreading" — permitted (a peg is a kli shemelachto le'issur, usable for a permitted purpose). Two pegs spreading it out is a problem.
  • Hanging a wet coat in its usual place, e.g. on a coat hook or hanger — fine.
  • A jacket over the back of a chair, as normal — fine; spreading a dress over a chair — not.
  • Wet socks — you can't spread them out to dry; keep them on, or borrow dry ones.

Wet items on a radiator — a Torah prohibition

Placing something wet next to a heat source is forbidden by the Torah: Bishul (heating the water) and Melaben (heat-drying is part of laundering). A heated towel rail may keep a dry towel warm — but don't dry your hands on it and hang it back, and never put a wet tea towel on a radiator.

Q: A hat dropped in the dirt and blown about by the wind? — You may brush it off; that is not a "growing" (Zore'a) issue.

Part B — Kosher and Matir (Tying and Untying)

Background

In the Mishkan, the techelet dye came from the chilazon (whether we have it today is debated — hence some wear tzitzit with blue threads); catching it required nets, made by knotting cords. Spools of thread were also knotted end to end.

Two views of what a Torah-level knot is

All agree a Torah-level knot must be permanent (kayama). The debate is whether it must also be a professional/craftsman's knot (like the Mishnah's sailors' and camel-drivers' knots):

View

Torah prohibition

Rabbinic prohibition

Practical rule

Rif, Rambam, Shulchan Aruch — Sephardim

Permanent and professional

Either one alone

An ordinary knot meant to be untied within 7 days is permitted; a professional knot is forbidden even for an hour

Rashi, Rosh, Rema — Ashkenazim

Permanent, even if not professional

Any knot meant to last a while

A knot must be meant to be untied within 24 hours

Shoelaces

  • Sephardim may tie shoelaces intending to untie them within a week — e.g. on Shavuot night, staying up for more than 24 hours.
  • Those who tie shoes once and then slip them on and off for weeks must never tie that first knot on Shabbat.

Double knots

The Rema treats a double knot as a craftsman's knot (Ashkenazim avoid it even for an hour). The Chida (Rav Chaim Yosef David Azulai), who travelled widely, records the custom in all Sephardi lands: a double knot is not professional. So Sephardim may tie a double knot to be untied within seven days — shoelaces, a woman's headscarf, the bands of a Sefer Torah (the Spanish and Portuguese custom). Even a Yom Tov Sefer Torah, which may not be opened again for months, may be tied with a double knot — a special case with its own reasoning.

A bow is not a knot

A bow (aniva) slips undone with a pull — not a knot at all, and may be tied even for the long term. A tie (necktie) is also an aniva and may be tied on Shabbat even if kept tied for weeks. (Shoelaces are a single knot plus a bow.)

Knots that are normally permanent

Intending to untie within a week doesn't help for knots that are by nature permanent:

  • Tzitzit that have come undone — may not be re-tied, even "temporarily". Even tightening a loose tzitzit knot is making a knot — possibly a Torah prohibition.
  • A broken eruv cord — may not be re-tied by a Jew; here one may ask a non-Jew.

Dustbin bags

Permanence counts even if you get no benefit from it: a double knot on a bin bag will stay tied for good. Use a simple single knot (one twist of the two ends), which has no permanence at all. Q: two single knots, one on each pair of corners? — each is just a single knot: fine.

Beware the overhand knot: a knot tied within one strand locks on itself — it has the status of a double knot (only if untied within seven days).

Untying

The same rules apply in reverse: one may untie any knot that is not professional and was not made to last more than seven days.

Q: Taking the bins out on Shabbat? — Raises carrying and muktseh questions; possible in certain circumstances — to be discussed in the Muktseh shiur.

Summary

  • Melaben = cleaning the fabric itself. Soaking, scrubbing and wringing are each forbidden. Clean towels may be wetted; prefer paper for coloured spills.
  • Surface dirt may be removed; a dry stain preferably by a non-Jew with a fingernail — never with water. Dust may be shaken or brushed off (ideally with a shinui).
  • Don't spread wet clothes out to dry, even privately; hanging from one peg or in the normal place is fine. Nothing wet on a radiator or heated rail.
  • Knots (Sephardim): any non-professional knot — single or double — to be untied within 7 days; bows and neckties always fine. Never re-tie or tighten tzitzit; bin bags with a simple single knot.
