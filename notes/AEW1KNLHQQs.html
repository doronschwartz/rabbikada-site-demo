Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 6, Seifim 5-8 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Monday 29 July 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi said we are finishing the laws of the gerushah (the divorced woman) in Siman 6 and then starting the laws of the zonah. Last time we saw the kol, a public rumour. A rumour that comes out before the marriage (kol lifnei nisuin) is a concern, while a rumour after the marriage (kol acharei nisuin) is not, because the couple are already known to be living together as husband and wife. Today he began with Seif 5, then went through the Geonim in Seif 6 and the mavoi and shekhunah in Seif 7. The last part of the class began Seif 8, on the zonah.

Seif 5: two rumours that come out together

The Shulhan Arukh says that if a rumour comes out that a woman was both betrothed and divorced, the two rumours together, we are concerned about the rumour of gerushin for a Kohen. For an Israelite she may marry. The Rabbi set out three points.

1. Two rumours at once, or one after the other. If the rumours come out together, the rumour of divorce cancels the rumour of betrothal for everyone else, so she is permitted to an Israelite and forbidden only to a Kohen. The Shulhan Arukh does not say the rumours must come "at once" (miyad). That is the reading of the Rambam and Rashi: any time a rumour of betrothal is followed later by a rumour of divorce, the second can cancel the first. The Ravad disagrees. For him, a rumour of betrothal that has settled cannot be cancelled by a rumour of divorce a month later. She is then forbidden to everyone until she gets a get. The Beit Shemuel adds that even the Ravad still worries about the rumour of divorce for a Kohen.

Why not let one rumour cancel another? The Rabbi said we cannot easily measure how strong a rumour is. Rumours vary in how many people spread them and how firm they are. When the two come out together, it is the same rumour and the same crowd, so it works as one. When they come out apart, the second is a different kind of rumour.

2. The man the rumour named. If she was said to be betrothed to a particular man and the rumour of divorce came out at once, may she stay with him? The Beit Shemuel says no, because there was never a time for the first rumour to settle. It is as if it all happened before the marriage. The Beit Shemuel is unsure how far this goes, and the Rabbi said he was not sure either.

3. The amatla. The Shulhan Arukh adds that an amatla (a credible explanation that weakens the rumour) changes the law. The Rabbi gave the combinations:

The explanation (amatla)

Result

In both the betrothal and the divorce

No concern for anyone

In the betrothal only

She is permitted to everyone, including a Kohen, because the betrothal falls away and so there is nothing to divorce

In the divorce only

She stays forbidden to others as a possibly married woman. If that man dies, she may marry even a Kohen, because the rumour of divorce is too weak to bind her

In neither

The basic law above

He added that a rumour only counts if it is muhzak in Beit Din, meaning Beit Din examined it and found some substance. A rumour that was never investigated is not a concern.

A rumour about a halusah. The Rabbi then compared a rumour that she did halisah with a doubt that she did. We learned earlier that a doubtful halusah is forbidden to a Kohen at the outset, though if he married her he may stay. The Ravad treats a rumour as stronger than a doubt, so for him a halusah rumour is worse and one may not marry her even afterwards. The Beit Shemuel makes the obvious distinction that a rumour is weaker than a doubt. The Rema and the Beit Shemuel rule like the Rambam, so we are not concerned about a rumour of halisah.

Seif 6: the Geonim on a Kohen who marries a divorcee

The Rabbi said the next seif is a sharp warning from the Geonim. A Kohen who marries a divorced woman must be separated from her, and the community distances itself from him. The Geonim describe severe measures against such a Kohen, including excommunication, so that he does not go to a place where he is unknown and bless the people. The Rabbi said we do not follow the extreme penalties, but we need to know how seriously the Geonim saw it.

On the practical side, he said that in many congregations there are Kohanim married to divorced women. He mentioned the accepted custom that such a man is not called up as a Kohen and does not bless the congregation, though he may be called for another aliyah. He said he was not sure of the source and would check, and a student offered to look into it. The Shulhan Arukh does not say this, and the Rabbi said he would not claim more than the custom.

Seif 7: the same mavoi or the same shekhunah

A Kohen may not live with his divorced wife in the same mavoi (an alley). The Rabbi explained a point in Tosafot: the more serious the prohibition, the less distancing is needed, because there is less chance of falling into a sin. So Tosafot says a shekhunah (a neighbourhood) is a larger area than a mavoi, and if the woman remarried, so that she is a married woman, she may live in the same shekhunah but not the same mavoi. Other Rishonim hold that a shekhunah is larger and the distance is greater, so she must move even if she remarried. He said this does not belong here and moved on quickly.

Seif 8: who is a zonah?

This ended the laws of the gerushah and opened a long seif. The Torah says "a woman who is a zonah, a halalah or a gerushah". The Rabbi said the gerushah is simple. The question is how to define a zonah, and the Rambam has a unique approach.

The Mishnah and the Gemara. The Mishnah lists a convert (giyoret) and a woman who had relations through zenut. There are different views about what makes a zonah. Rabbi Akiva says a woman who is mufkeret (a prostitute). Rabbi Eliezer says even a single woman who has relations with a man outside marriage becomes a zonah. The Gemara rules that the Halacha does not follow Rabbi Eliezer. The Rishonim follow the Sages: a zonah is a giyoret or a woman who had relations with a forbidden man (nivalah biilat zenut). An ordinary single woman is not a zonah.

The Semag. The Semag says we should be careful about all the views, which would include a mufkeret. The Maharshal and the Beit Shemuel say to be strict. The Rabbi stressed that mufkeret does not mean literally a prostitute. It can mean a woman who sleeps around and would go with any man. So if a woman has done teshuvah and has a very loose past, a Kohen should not marry her, according to the Beit Shemuel. He said we would not stop such a marriage, but a Kohen who is careful has grounds to hold back.

The convert. Does a convert fall under "zonah" or is she a separate category? The Gemara gives four views on the age at which a child who converts becomes forbidden to a Kohen. The most lenient (Rabbi Shimon ben Yohai) says a girl younger than three is fine. The Halacha does not follow him, so any woman born non-Jewish who converts, even a baby, is forbidden to a Kohen. The daughter of two converts should not marry a Kohen at the outset either, though if they married they may stay together. He said this last point is less known, but it comes up.

The Rambam. The Rambam holds that there are only three categories of woman forbidden to a Kohen: the gerushah, the halalah and the zonah. Everything must fall under one of the three. A zonah, in his view, is:

  • a woman born non-Jewish, even if she converted
  • a woman who had relations with someone she may never marry, such as a mamzer or a close relative
  • a woman who had relations with a halal

A niddah does not make a zonah, even though relations with her are forbidden, because she may be married once she is pure. The thread is pegam (a blemish or contamination). The Rabbi noted that the Rambam found one thing hard in his commentary on the Mishnah: why a halal makes a woman a zonah. In the Mishneh Torah he concludes that it is a pegam in its own right.

Tosafot. The class ended as the Rabbi began Tosafot, which defines a zonah as a woman with whom a Jewish man can never have a permitted marriage. He said to continue next time.

Practical summary

  • Two rumours that come out together, one of betrothal and one of divorce, mean she is permitted to an Israelite and forbidden to a Kohen.
  • The Shulhan Arukh follows the Rambam and Rashi, so a later rumour of divorce can cancel an earlier rumour of betrothal. The Ravad disagrees.
  • An amatla in the betrothal frees her fully. An amatla only in the divorce leaves her forbidden to others while that man lives, though she may marry a Kohen after he dies.
  • Only a rumour that Beit Din has examined and found to have substance is a concern.
  • A zonah is not a single woman with a loose past, but a convert or a woman who had a forbidden relation. A very loose past is a reason for a careful Kohen to hold back.
  • A woman born non-Jewish who converts, even as a baby, is forbidden to a Kohen at the outset.
