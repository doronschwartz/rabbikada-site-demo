Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 301, seifim 21-25 and 29-30 (seifim 26-28 not studied) Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 27 June 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Seifim 21-22: A box on the head, and plasters

The Rabbi opened with a note on masks from last week: Purim in the coming year falls on Friday, so costumes and masks that are not clothing or adornment would have to come off when Shabbat begins.

Seif 21: one may not go out with a box on one's head to keep off the rain, since that is not a way of wearing anything. But one may wear even very thick, heavy clothes, such as the sackcloth farmers wore in the cold, since they have the name of a garment. Anything that is a garment for some people may be worn.

Seif 22: plasters. One may go out with a dressing on a wound if it actively heals, such as the onion or garlic peel used in their days, because healing gives it the status of a takhshit. A plain thread or protective cloth that does not heal is a burden, according to the Shulhan Arukh. Asked how anyone would know the difference, the Rabbi said it is not about what others see but about what the item is.

The Vilna Gaon and many others disagree and hold that protection alone is enough, and the Mishnah Berurah seems to rule leniently. So a plaster that contains something medicinal is permitted according to all, and a plain plaster may be worn, relying on this, certainly in an eruv and in places that are not a true Reshut HaRabbim.

Seif 23: Bells, labels and tags

Children may go out with bells woven into their clothes, since these are part of the garment and even adorn it. If not woven in, they may fall off and be carried. In the Gemara's time only wealthy children wore them; the Mishnah Berurah explains that even a poor child may, since something woven in will not be torn off.

The Rema: something attached to a garment is batel to it only if it is normal for such a thing to be there. You cannot sew a book onto your coat and call it part of the garment. Two questions always: will it come off, and is it the normal thing to have there?

The brand label on a suit sleeve. It is woven on, but is it normal to leave it? In England people remove it; in Israel many young men proudly leave it on. Rav Ovadia Yosef rules that one should remove it before Shabbat, but if one did not, one may walk with it. The Rabbi set out three levels: - A shaatnez-tested label, which people keep as a mark of pride: fine. - The sleeve label: better removed before Shabbat, but one may rely on wearing it. - A dry-cleaning tag, often pinned and never wanted: he could see no justification. It must be removed before Shabbat, and he questioned going out in it without an eruv.

Someone raised spare buttons sewn inside a shirt. A loose bag of buttons is plainly carrying. Spare buttons sewn in as most shirts have them are normal and woven on; the Rabbi felt these would be permitted.

Discovering a tag mid-walk. If you find you are carrying something in your pocket or on your clothes, walk straight home without stopping: carrying without putting down is only rabbinic, while stopping makes it worse. Pausing at a red light is not counted as stopping; stopping to chat is.

The Rema, from the Or Zarua (13th century, Vienna): where the authorities required Jews to wear a sign, it may be worn even if only partly attached. Mishnah Berurah 83: since it is worn all week by decree, it is a garment, and no one will remove it for fear of the authorities. The Rabbi noted this is perhaps the first reference in halacha to Jews being made to wear a badge, long before the Holocaust.

A handkerchief sewn to the garment may be worn, since it is batel. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul argued that today's expensive handkerchiefs are too important to be batel; Rav Ovadia Yosef asked where such a distinction comes from: relative to the suit, a handkerchief is secondary. The Ben Ish Hai holds that even pinning counts as attached. The Rabbi worked through a pensioner's travel pass pinned to a lapel in the same way, noting that it depends on whether wearing such a pass pinned on is normal today.

Noise. The Rema forbids bells that ring, but we do not hold that; we allow things that make noise on Shabbat, like a dishwasher set before Shabbat.

Seifim 24-25: Herbs, amulets and red threads

Seif 24: one may go out with medicinal herbs tied on, since healing makes them a takhshit. The Kaf HaHayim, following the Hida, extends this to ruda (rue), a herb used in Syria and Lebanon against ayin hara, which one may carry tied in a bag. The class discussed rue; one participant grows it at home for this reason.

The Rabbi said his own view, based on many sources, is that ayin hara in the sense of a look causing harm is psychological; the Rambam rejected it. He distinguished it from ayin raah, a negative, begrudging eye, which is real. Even so, he would answer someone who follows the Kaf HaHayim that he may go out with it, since psychologically it helps him.

Seif 25: amulets. One may go out with a kamea whose effect has been proven: either the amulet itself healed three people, or its writer's amulets for that illness worked three times. An unproven amulet may not be worn. The Rambam codified this even though he held amulets are nonsense, because the psychological benefit is itself real. - Modern amulets for success, sold in some circles: unproven, so he could not see how one may go out in them without an eruv. - The "Bring Them Home" necklace: at the end of the day it is a necklace, an adornment, and permitted. - A red thread: the Rabbi holds it is wrong. Based on the Rambam and the Ramhal, one who chooses mazal places himself under chance, while our relationship with Hashem is direct. In any case it is not an adornment, so it may not be worn without an eruv.

Someone asked whether hukkot hagoyim is the bigger concern. The Rabbi agreed these things raise real issues and pointed to the Rambam, Avodah Zarah 11:12, on writing verses as amulets. He acknowledged that people have long done such things, such as Tehillim under a pillow, and said he was conflicted on parts of it.

Braces. Fixed braces are part of you. Removable braces: one posek forbade them, since the benefit is cumulative over months, not immediate; everyone disagreed, since wearing them is part of the treatment. Permitted.

Seifim 29-30: A folded tallit

Seif 29: one who goes out with a tallit folded on his shoulders is liable from the Torah, since that is not wearing it. If it covers the body, even rolled up a little so as not to draw attention on the way to shul, it is fine. Seif 30: where it is normal to wear it that way, it is permitted. In many English shuls people wear a narrow tallit like a scarf. That is the wrong way to fulfil the misvah, but it has become a normal way of wearing it, so it may be worn.

Next week: gloves, a jacket on the shoulders, two garments, and hats. "Wearing a Borsalino hat, by the way, on Shabbat is halachically problematic. You should be aware."

Practical summary

  • A plaster may be worn outside an eruv; one with a healing element according to all.
  • Remove dry-cleaning tags before Shabbat; take off suit labels if you can, but you may wear them if you forgot.
  • Spare buttons sewn in and shaatnez labels are part of the garment.
  • If you discover you are carrying something, walk straight home without stopping.
  • Proven medical items and a normal necklace may be worn; unproven amulets and red threads may not, outside an eruv.
  • Wear a tallit over the body, not folded on the shoulders, unless that is the local way of wearing it.
