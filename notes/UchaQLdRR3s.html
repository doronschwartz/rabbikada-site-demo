Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 15, Seifim 10-15 (sisters, stepsisters, and the daughters, granddaughters, mothers and grandmothers of one's wife) Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 12 March 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi began with a review of Seifim 1-9. The arayot come in three kinds: blood relatives, those connected through a blood relative (the wife of your father), and combinations (a woman and her daughter). Hazal added sheniyot as a fence, either an extra generation or a parallel prohibition on the other side. If the prohibition is from the Torah there is no kiddushin and no get; if it is rabbinic, the kiddushin takes effect and a get is needed.

He also recapped the three categories already learned. Your mother is forbidden by the Torah and your grandmother by Hazal. According to the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh the prohibition continues endlessly only along an unbroken line of the same gender, while Rashi holds it always continues. Your father's wife is forbidden by the Torah in all cases, unless it was a one-off relationship. Of the aunts through marriage, only the wife of your father's paternal brother is forbidden by the Torah; the others are rabbinic.

Seif 10: sisters, and the daughter of a maidservant

A sister is forbidden by the Torah in every case, even if your father had her in a one-off relationship or a forbidden one (she is then also a mamzeret). The Gemara excepts the daughter of a maidservant (shifhah) or a gentile, because she is not traced to a Jewish father, so you may marry her.

The Shulhan Arukh adds a case: if a man had a child with his own shifhah, the child is treated as his sister and may not be married by his son. The Rema adds that this holds even if he says he did not intend to free her.

The source is a question to the Geonim about a shifhah belonging to the Rosh Galuta. The Geonim reasoned that a man with his own shifhah will not do a forbidden act, so he must have intended to free her, and the child is Jewish. The Rambam rejects this. We assume intent only in the two cases in the Gemara, a divorced wife and a failed kiddushin. Everywhere else we do not say that a man intended to make a relationship permitted.

The Rosh. The Rosh normally follows the Rambam, yet here he agrees with the Geonim. The Rabbi explained why. Freeing her does not require kiddushin. Once she is free and lives in his house, she is in the position of a pilegesh, which the Rosh permits. For the Rambam, freeing her only turns a shifhah into a Jewish woman, so a one-off relationship is still zenut, and nothing is gained.

Child of one's own shifhah

Rambam

Not assumed to be freed, so no prohibition

Geonim and Rosh

Assumed freed, so she is a sister and forbidden

Shulhan Arukh

Follows the Geonim and the Rosh, so forbidden

A question was asked about rumours (nitanan) that the man was already involved with her. The Rabbi said the case here is the simple one, where we have no evidence of earlier relations and the man comes saying it was a single occasion.

The Rema's added stringency, even for a regular shifhah or a gentile woman, is brought from the Or Zarua. The Rabbi found it hard to understand, since she would have to convert anyway before marriage. He mentioned only briefly the idea of zera Yisrael as one attempted proof and said it is weak.

Seif 11: the stepsister

The Gemara asks about the daughter of your father's wife from a previous marriage. The worry would be marit ayin, that people will think she is a real sister. The Bavli concludes it is permitted, because everyone knows about stepfamilies. The Yerushalmi forbids it. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Bavli, even if they grew up in the same house. In his words: "This is not an issue of sheniyot, this is an issue of marit ayin."

The Rabbi noted that the stepmother of your wife will be treated more strictly when we reach that Siman.

Seifim 12-15: your wife's daughters, and the great-grandchildren

The Torah forbids a woman together with her daughter or granddaughter. It is the only prohibition where the Torah reaches two generations. This applies even after she has died or been divorced, and works upward as well (your wife's mother and grandmother). Hazal added one more generation, so your own great-granddaughter and your wife's great-granddaughter are forbidden.

The Gemara is in doubt about the next generation, the great-great-granddaughter of your wife (and her great-great-grandmother). Is there a break (hefsek) or not? The Rishonim split:

Rabbenu Tam's question. Your son's wife is forbidden by the Torah, and so is the wife of any descendant down the generations. How can there be a doubt about a great-great-granddaughter, when the daughter-in-law of a great-great-grandson is forbidden? The Tosafot answer that you are in closer contact with a descendant who is an heir, so there is more concern. The Magid Mishneh says simply that Hazal's decrees do not always follow logic. This leaves the odd result that you may marry your great-great-granddaughter but not the wife of your great-great-grandson.

The Ramban's answer. The Gemara's doubt was never about your own descendants. Your own great-great-grandchildren are forbidden forever, and the question was only about your wife's. So the Ramban and the Rosh both forbid your own, for different reasons, while the Rambam permits it. The Shulhan Arukh rules that the prohibition continues without end, and mentions that the Rambam sees a hefsek.

Practical case within marriage. The Torah forbids the daughter of your wife only where you were married to the mother. If you had a one-off relationship with a woman, her daughter from another relationship is not forbidden by the Torah. Even so, the Rabbi explained, Hazal ruled that if rumours exist of a relationship, a man should not marry her daughter while she is alive. The concern is that once he marries the daughter, any later contact with the mother is a prohibition. So he waits until the mother dies. If he did not wait and is already married, he may stay with her.

The Rabbi pointed out that this rule is separate from the sheniyot and the arguments of the Rambam and the Rosh above. It is a different kind of concern.

The Shulhan Arukh's wording

At the end, the Rabbi raised a question about the Shulhan Arukh's way of ruling. In Seifim 12-15 he rules like the stringent view, as the stam, and then adds that the Rambam holds there is a break. This is not the usual formula of stam followed by yesh omerim. The Rabbi suggested that naming the Rambam gives that lenient view added weight. He said this is only something to think about, not a conclusion.

Looking ahead

Seifim 16 and 17 deal with a person's own aunts, the sisters of father and mother. After that the Siman turns to more general laws, which should go faster. The Rabbi hoped to finish the Siman the following week.

Practical summary

  • A sister is forbidden by the Torah even from a one-off or forbidden relationship, but not the daughter of a gentile or someone else's maidservant.
  • For a man's own shifhah, the Shulhan Arukh follows the Geonim and the Rosh: the child is his sister, although the Rambam disagrees.
  • A stepsister may be married, even if they grew up together, following the Bavli. This is different from the stepmother of one's wife.
  • The Torah forbids a wife's daughter and granddaughter, and her mother and grandmother. Hazal added another generation, and the Shulhan Arukh rules that it continues without end, noting the Rambam's lenient view.
  • If a man had only a one-off relationship with a woman, he should wait until she dies before marrying her daughter, but if he already married her he may stay.
