A new series

The Rabbi opened a new series on great Sepharadi hakhamim of the 20th century who are less well known than Rav Ovadia Yoseph or Rav Mordechai Eliyahu. "I don't want to start equating who's greater than the other," he said, but their lives and works deserve to be explored. The plan was four weeks, in order of age: Rav Uziel, Hakham Gagin of London, Rav Messas of Morocco and Rav Haim David HaLevi. The class had three parts: his life, his outlook, and his halachic rulings.

His life

Rav Bension Meir Hai Uziel was born in Jerusalem in 1880 and died in 1953. His father was a dayan and president of the Sepharadi community of Jerusalem, which is perhaps where he learned both his learning and his communal activism. He studied with Sepharadi and Ashkenazi hakhamim, and later hoped that all the communities, "whether you're from Bombay, whether you're from Saloniki, whether you're from Damascus or from Poland", might come together in one tradition. He was willing to compromise for it, the Rabbi said, but the idea never got off the ground.

  • At 20 he was teaching in a yeshiva.
  • In 1911, aged 31, he became Hakham Bashi of Yafo, the Ottoman-recognised rabbi. The post required someone born in the land who spoke Hebrew, Arabic and French. Rav Kook, then the Ashkenazi rabbi of Yafo, did not have these qualifications. The two were very different, Rav Kook more mystical and Rav Uziel more rational, but they became close friends and worked together.
  • During the First World War the Ottomans sent him to Damascus for a time because he interceded too actively for persecuted Jews.
  • From 1921 he served for three years in Salonika, then a great community.
  • From 1923 or 1924 he was Chief Rabbi of Tel Aviv for about 15 years.
  • From 1939 until his death he was Sepharadi Chief Rabbi, first of Palestine and from 1948 of the State of Israel.

Two days before he died he dictated a will: his aims had been to spread Torah, to love the Torah, Israel and its holiness, and to bring "genuine peace into the home of the Jew... and between Israel and his Father in heaven". "I don't think any of this is an exaggeration," the Rabbi said. "He literally lived his life like this."

His outlook

His halachic works are Mishpetei Uziel and Piskei Uziel; his thought is in Hegyonei Uziel and Mikhmanei Uziel, which are harder to find.

Against isolation. At a gathering of rabbis in Jerusalem in 1919 he warned against those who say, "Let us stand in a corner, looking at the events from a distance... we and our families will serve the Lord." Rabbis must go out among the people. Jews, and the State of Israel, have a duty to be ambassadors to humanity.

Israel as the beginning of redemption. At a time when many saw a state before Mashiah as a threat, he and Rav Kook saw it as the start of the redemption. His responsa are calm and structured, yet written with urgency: there is an opportunity that must not be wasted.

The middle path. Israel should be an example to the nations in its economy and its laws. He criticised both the religious who isolate themselves and the secular who want a state without religious identity, citing Mishlei: "Let your eyes look straight ahead... turn not to the right or the left." The Rabbi added: "It's easy in life to take an extreme, because it's easier to be extreme rather than to be nuanced."

No false humility. Rabbis must not use humility as an escape from leadership. That is lack of confidence, not humility.

The Sanhedrin. He worked for some time to restore the Sanhedrin, hoping a supreme court could guide all the communities, but did not gain the support of the rabbis and gave it up.

Fighting in 1948. When yeshiva students asked him for an exemption during the War of Independence, he answered, aged 68, that if he were not an old man he would be holding a gun to defend the Old City where he was born. Before we received the Torah we were promised the land; it is arrogant to sit back while others fight for you. The Rabbi said the context then, establishing the state, was different from defending it today, and he did not think Rav Uziel would necessarily say the same now. His point was the principle.

Worldly knowledge. He wrote that it is impossible to understand Torah deeply without broad knowledge of the sciences: mathematics, cosmology, philosophy, even psychology, which a dayan needs to judge who is telling the truth.

Autopsies: no distinction between bodies

In the 1930s medical schools needed autopsies for training. The Rabbi presented Rav Kook's view as forbidding the use of Jewish bodies, while allowing the bodies of non-Jews. Rav Uziel, in Piskei Uziel, rejected that distinction outright. The prohibition is because it degrades a human being, created in the image of God. "If it's forbidden, it's forbidden on Jews and non-Jews. If it's permissible, it's permissible on Jews and non-Jews." Do not even think of distinguishing.

His rulings

Conversion in Colombia. A small community in Colombia without a rabbi had converted the non-Jewish wife of a Jewish man, whose father was Jewish, before three ordinary observant men. She knew little, and a nearby rabbi asked whether he could marry them. Rav Uziel said yes, making four points: 1. Not knowing much, or not being likely to keep everything, does not disqualify a conversion. What is needed is acceptance of the misvot, not their observance. The Rabbi compared a Jewish woman who says, "I know I'm meant to cover my hair, I find it hard," with one who says the law is sexist: the first accepts the misvah, the second rejects it. 2. They were already civilly married, so she had no ulterior motive; her wish to convert was sincere. 3. She was zera Yisrael, of Jewish descent through her father, and it is a misvah to bring her back. The Rabbi called this "his own hiddush" and said batei din still use it. 4. Refusing her would leave a Jewish man in a forbidden relationship for life, which is also serious.

"Look at all the misvot he's going to do as a Jew," Rav Uziel wrote. Rav Kook took the opposite view, that such a conversion places a stumbling block before the convert and others.

Women in public life. In the 1920s Rav Kook opposed women voting or holding office. Rav Uziel wrote that there is no source to forbid it. To the claim that voting is immodest, he answered that by that logic women could never go into the street or do business. To the claim it would harm peace at home, he wrote that true family life, built on shared ideals, is not harmed by different votes. He also held that women may be elected, and that a woman accepted by both parties may judge monetary cases, perhaps better because of her compassion.

Agunot. Hakham Yaakov Moshe Toledano of Alexandria proposed a condition at the wedding: if the local beit din later ruled the marriage had failed, it would be void retroactively. It needed the support of other rabbis, did not receive it, and was shelved.

Halacha has limits. A case came from Morocco in which a woman named a man as father of her child and he denied it. The local rabbi obliged him to pay support, fearing abortion or abandonment. Rav Uziel rejected that reasoning: you cannot make someone pay because of the problems that may follow. He did, in the end, find for payment, on other grounds.

He also urged buying etrogim from the Land of Israel to support its farmers, and held that women may and should say the berakhah on time-bound misvot.

Summary

  • Rav Uziel combined a great posek, a thinker and a communal leader at the birth of the State of Israel.
  • He opposed isolation: rabbis and Israel must look outwards, to all of Israel and all humanity.
  • His rulings on autopsies, conversion of zera Yisrael and women in public life were ahead of their time.
  • His compassion still worked within the bounds of halacha.
