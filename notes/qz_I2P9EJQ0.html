Siman 451 – The Laws of Kashering Utensils (דיני הגעלת כלים)

Contains 27 Se'ifim — This class covers the foundational principles and Se'ifim 1–4, 6

Foundational Principles

Before diving into the Shulchan Aruch, several core principles must be understood. These underpin every halacha in this siman.

Principle 1: The Two Torah Sources for Hag'alat Kelim

Source 1 — Parshat Tzav (Vayikra 6:21) — The Korban Chatat

וכלי חרש אשר תבשל בו ישבר ואם בנחשת בשלה ומורק ושוטף במים

  • Earthenware vessel used to cook the chatat offering → must be broken (cannot be kashered).
  • Copper/metal vessel → can be purged (morak) and rinsed (shutaf) in water.

The Gemara (Avodah Zarah) derives: העידה תורה על כלי חרס שאין להם תקנה לצאת מידי דופיין — "The Torah testified that earthenware vessels have no remedy to remove their absorbed flavors."

Two lessons from this pasuk:

  1. Earthenware that absorbed forbidden flavor through cooking can never be purged — it's too absorbent.
  2. Metal utensils that absorbed forbidden flavor can be purged through water (hag'alah).

Source 2 — Parshat Matot (Bamidbar 31:22–23) — The War with Midian

After the war with Midian, Elazar HaKohen instructed Bnei Yisrael on how to kasher the captured utensils:

כל דבר אשר יבא באש תעבירו באש וטהר... וכל אשר לא יבא באש תעבירו במים

  • If the utensil was used with fire (i.e., forbidden food was cooked in it with fire) → pass it through fire (libun).
  • If it was not used with fire → pass it through water (hag'alah).

Rashi's principle: כבולעו כך פולטו — "The way it absorbed is the way it expels." This is the master rule of all kashering.

Note: The pasuk also requires tevilat kelim (immersion in a mikveh) for utensils acquired from a non-Jew — but that is a completely separate din from hag'alat kelim. You cannot kasher a utensil by taking it to the mikveh. These are two different processes.

Principle 2: Is There a Mitzvah to Kasher Utensils?

Opinion

Is Hag'alat Kelim a Mitzvah?

Reasoning

Tosafot

✅ Yes — just like tevilat kelim

Both are Torah-derived processes for making utensils usable

Rambam

❌ No — not listed in the 613 mitzvot

Hag'alah merely removes an obstacle (forbidden flavor). Tevilat kelim is a proactive transformation — changing the status of a utensil for Jewish use.

Practical consequence: No bracha is recited on hag'alat kelim — because even if it's a mitzvah, the consensus treats it as a preparatory process (hechsher mitzvah), not a standalone mitzvah.

For Sephardim: Where libun is required, it means libun chamur — full intensity. There's no halfway.

For Ashkenazim (Rama): Libun chamur is the default, but libun kal can be used when there's doubt whether the utensil needs libun or merely hag'alah.

Why Doesn't the Flavor Reabsorb During Hag'alah?

Since hag'alah merely removes flavors into the water (unlike libun which destroys them), why don't the flavors go back into the pot?

Two answers in the Rishonim:

  1. The boiling water that causes the flavor to exit cannot simultaneously cause it to reabsorb — the process only works in one direction at a time.
  2. The water is assumed to be 60 times the volume of the absorbed flavor — so the flavor becomes nullified (batel be-shishim) upon exiting the walls.

Practical consequence of this machloket: We advise rinsing the utensil in cold water immediately after hag'alah — to ensure the flavor doesn't reabsorb (satisfying the first reason).

Principle 4: Rov Tashmisho (רוב תשמישו) — Majority of Its Use

This is a critical principle that simplifies Pesach preparation enormously.

Rule: We follow the majority of how a utensil was used during the year — not the occasional exception.

Majority Use

Occasional Use

What's Required

With water/liquid (needs hag'alah)

Sometimes dry on fire (would need libun)

Hag'alah suffices

With cold food

Sometimes with hot food

Washing suffices — no kashering needed

Why so lenient? Because the entire requirement to kasher after 24 hours is only de-rabbanan (since after 24 hours, the flavor is noten ta'am li-fgam — degraded). Since it's a rabbinic decree, the Chachamim only applied it when the majority of use creates the concern.

Practical example: A pot used 100 times during the year — 97 times with cold chametz food, 3 times with hot chametz food. You only need to wash it thoroughly — no hag'alah needed, because rov tashmisho was cold.

Principle 5: Issura Bala vs. Hetera Bala (אסורא בלע / התירא בלע)

Core question: Was the absorbed food forbidden or permissible at the time of absorption?

The Gemara's undisputed rule: If the food was permissible when absorbed (hetera bala), then even dry absorption only needs hag'alah — not libun.

Example everyone agrees on: Sacrificial meat cooked on a spit — permissible when cooked, only becomes forbidden later (when the time limit passes). Even though it's dry/fire → hag'alah suffices because it was hetera bala.

The machloket: Is chametz hetera bala or issura bala?

Opinion

Chametz is...

Reasoning

Consequence

Rabbeinu Tam (Tosafot)

Hetera bala — absorbed permissibly

During the year when you cooked with chametz, it was completely permitted

You never need libun for Pesach. Hag'alah always suffices — even for dry/fire use.

The Rif

Issura bala — absorbed as if forbidden

Chametz is inherently something that becomes forbidden (unlike sacrifices, which are always permitted when fresh). Had it been Pesach, it would be forbidden.

Dry/fire use → needs libun

Shulchan Aruch: Follows the Rif — chametz is issura bala. Dry/fire use requires libun.

However — critical caveat (Mishnah Berurah 451:28): Rabbeinu Tam is still a significant opinion. Whenever there is another reason to be lenient (e.g., rov tashmisho, doubt about usage), we combine Rabbeinu Tam's opinion and treat chametz as hetera bala. This combination appears many times throughout this siman.

The Shulchan Aruch — Se'if by Se'if

Se'if 1 — Earthenware (כלי חרס) and China

Shulchan Aruch: Earthenware pots used with chametz during the year:

  1. Clean them well — ensure no visible chametz remains (mamashut / substance).
  2. Put them away for after Pesach — store in a discreet place where you won't accidentally use them.
  3. After Pesach — you may use them freely. The absorbed chametz in the walls is chametz she'avar alav ha-Pesach, but the Chachamim's penalty only applies to tangible chametz (mamash), not to flavor absorbed in walls.

Why is this not obvious? There was an opinion in the Gemara that the absorbed chametz flavor in the walls should also be treated as chametz she'avar alav ha-Pesach (and the pots could never be used again). Shmuel disagreed: the gezeirah only applies to tangible substance, not to absorbed flavor. We follow Shmuel.

What About Kli Sheni?

  • The Shulchan Aruch in Se'if 1 only mentions cooking and pouring (irui) as problematic — he does not mention kli sheni here.
  • In Se'if 5 (and 6), he does say kli sheni should ideally be kashered.
  • The Poskim resolve this: Since earthenware cannot be kashered anyway, and kli sheni is itself a chumra — you can use earthenware/china on Pesach that was only used as a kli sheni during the year (just wash it well).
  • Ashkenazim disagree and would not permit this.
  • Even many Sephardim would say don't do this in the first instance — but strictly speaking, there are grounds for leniency.

Practical example: China bowls used on the dinner table where food was poured into them (irui kli rishon → the bowl is kli sheni). Strictly speaking for Sephardim: wash well and usable on Pesach. In practice: most people have separate Pesach china.

Se'if 3 — Knives (סכינים)

Shulchan Aruch: Knives — kasher with hag'alah in a kli rishon. That's sufficient.

But wait — shouldn't knives need libun? If you used a knife on a barbecue (dry, with direct fire), that should be issura bala requiring libun!

Why hag'alah suffices:

The Mishnah Berurah explains this is a combination of two leniencies:

  1. Rabbeinu Tam — chametz is hetera bala, so you never need libun for Pesach anyway.
  2. Rov tashmisho — knives are rarely used dry on the fire. Their majority use is with water, with food, on the table, cutting, etc.

These two reasons combined allow hag'alah for knives.

Exception: A knife used exclusively or primarily for barbecue (dry, on fire) — that specific knife would need libun.

What about knives used only with cold food?

  • If a knife is only used to cut bread, cheese, vegetables — always cold — then me'ikar ha-din (strictly speaking) it doesn't need kashering at all. Just wash it thoroughly.
  • Rov tashmisho be-tzonen (majority use is cold) = washing suffices.

Removing Rust Before Hag'alah

Shulchan Aruch (continuation): Before doing hag'alah on knives (or any utensil), you must:

  • Remove all rust — the pasuk says אך את הזהב ("only the gold") — the word אך (ach) is a limitation: hag'alah must act on the metal itself, not on rust covering it.
  • If there are chips, grooves, or holes where rust cannot be cleaned → hag'alah doesn't work there; those spots need libun.
  • In practice: if you have very rusty utensils that can't be cleaned — you cannot kasher them.

Se'if 4 — Things Used on Naked Fire (Spits, Grills)

Shulchan Aruch: Utensils used directly on fire — such as spits (shefudim) and grates/grills (askalot) — require libun.

  • This follows the Rif — chametz is issura bala.
  • If it were like Rabbeinu Tam (hetera bala), libun would never be needed for Pesach.

Libun means: Sparks fly from it / it becomes red hot (~250°C+).

The Mishnah Berurah (451:28) adds the critical caveat:

ומכל מקום אין ללמוד מדין זה למקומות אחרים — "Don't extrapolate from this to all other cases."

When there are additional reasons to be lenient, we combine Rabbeinu Tam's opinion and permit hag'alah instead of libun. The default follows the Rif (libun for dry/fire), but it's not applied rigidly in every scenario.

Se'if 6 — Bowls, Cutlery, and Rov Tashmisho in Practice

Shulchan Aruch: כל כלי הולכים בו אחר רוב תשמישו — We follow the majority of a utensil's use.

Example — Bowls (ke'arot):

  • Bowls on the table: their majority use is receiving food poured from a kli rishon → that makes them irui kli rishon (the pouring is irui; the bowl itself becomes kli sheni).
  • Even though sometimes the bowl is placed directly on the fire → since the majority is irui, kasher by irui (pouring boiling water over it). No need for full hag'alah in a kli rishon.

The Rama's Stringencies (Ashkenazi Practice)

The Rama disagrees with the Shulchan Aruch's leniency on rov tashmisho in three escalating ways:

Rama's Position

What It Means

Everything as kli rishon

Don't rely on rov tashmisho — kasher everything with full hag'alah in a kli rishon, even if majority use was irui or kli sheni

Even if "perhaps" used as kli rishon

Even spoons and cutlery — if there's any possibility it was once stuck into a pot on the fire → kasher as kli rishon

Even cold-use vessels

Drinking cups and vessels used primarily with cold — if there's any chance it was once used with hot chametz → kasher with hag'alah

This is why Ashkenazim kasher virtually everything — cutlery, cups, bowls — even when Sephardim would say it's unnecessary.

Practical Example: The Kiddush Cup

  • Used throughout the year for cold grape juice/wine.
  • Me'ikar ha-din (Sephardi): no kashering needed — rov tashmisho is cold. Just wash it.
  • The Rama: perhaps during the year someone drank hot coffee in it, or it was washed in hot water with chametz dishes → kasher it, or use a new one for Pesach.
  • Strictly speaking: There is absolutely no need for a separate Pesach Kiddush cup.

Using Cold Food in Non-Kosher/Chametz Utensils

Rama (from Hilchot Kashrut, applied here): Any utensil that needs kashering — you cannot use it even with cold food on a regular basis.

Why? The absorbed flavors can only be expelled with heat — cold food doesn't extract them. So technically, cold use is fine. But the Chachamim forbade it as a regular practice because you might forget and use it with hot food.

However — one-off use (be-derech arai) is permitted:

Scenario

Permitted?

Eating cold cereal and milk in a non-kosher hotel bowl (possibly used with bacon)

✅ Yes — one-off, cold, the bowl is clean

Keeping chametz plates in regular use throughout Pesach for cold food

❌ No — regular/fixed use

Emergency on Pesach: no clean dish available, using a clean chametz plate for cold food once

✅ Yes — one-off emergency
