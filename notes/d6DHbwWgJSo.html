Topic: Lighting Shabbat candles away from home, as a guest or in a hotel, and the two obligations of the person and the place Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263, seifim 6-8, with the Mishnah Berurah and the Biur Halacha Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 8 July 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review

The previous class showed the importance of minhag through three examples: Shehiyanu at the candles on Yom Tov, the berakhah on Yom Kippur candles (the Shulhan Arukh says none, but the custom is to make it), and whether to light before or after saying the berakhah. This class turns to seifim 6 and 7, which go together, and seif 8. None of them is mentioned in the Gemara. They come from the Rishonim.

Seif 6: lighting in your own room

The Shulhan Arukh speaks of men who are away from home learning, "bachurim", here meaning people who are about to marry or are married but staying away for Shabbat. Such a man must light in his room and make a berakhah. A man who is at home with his wife does not need to light in his bedroom with a berakhah, because his wife lights on his behalf.

Shalom bayit applies to the whole house. Candles are not only for the enjoyment of the meal. Shalom bayit applies everywhere, and no place in the house that will be used on Shabbat may lack light. An attic you will not enter needs no light. A bedroom does, though it does not have to be a special lamp: light from the corridor, a lamp beside you, or street light coming in is enough. In earlier times, with only candles, it meant lighting in the bedroom as well as the dining room.

Shalom bayit means peace in your home, not only between husband and wife. A person home alone needs light so he does not trip and get angry with himself.

Seif 7: the guest

A guest who has no room of his own and no wife at home must give a small coin to the host and ask for a share in the host's candles. The Rabbi worked through the four cases:

  1. At home with your wife. You may light in the bedroom, but with no berakhah.
  2. Away, alone, in your own room. You light with a berakhah, even though your wife is lighting at home.
  3. Staying with a host family, your wife lighting at home for you. You do nothing.
  4. Staying with a host family, single (or no wife lighting for you), sleeping in the host's space without your own room. You buy a share with a small payment.

The logic: the person and the place

The Rabbi explained all of this with two obligations that the Rishonim already include in lighting: "hovat gavra" (the obligation on the person) and "hovat dira" (the obligation on the place). He said the Rishonim do not use these terms and they are more modern, but this is what the Biur Halacha says clearly.

  • The place must be lit, so that there is shalom bayit there.
  • The person must take part in the misvah.

At home, the wife's lighting fulfils both. Away alone, your wife covers the person but the place you sleep in needs light, so you light with a berakhah. As a single guest, the host's light covers the place, but nobody lights for you, so you pay and join. As a married guest, your wife covers the person and the host covers the place, so there is nothing to do.

The Biur Halacha adds that when you are with your wife as one unit, one berakhah covers the house, as when you check for Hames and one berakhah covers all the rooms. So when the woman lights her candle and says the berakhah, her husband can light elsewhere after her without a berakhah. The Rabbi added, as a stringency for those who think electric light counts as lighting, that he should switch off and then on with that intention after his wife has lit.

"You just have to think it through, each question. The principle works in all these cases."

The hotel

A hotel is the common modern case. The place does need light, and the person has his own obligation. Lighting in the dining room where you eat is one option, though you cannot make a berakhah there (this was to be learned in the next seif). Many hotels will not allow candles in the bedroom.

Electricity. The Rabbi said that, in a pressing situation, Sefaradim rely on electric light and make a berakhah on it: one switches the light off, says the berakhah and switches it on. A question on exactly this was put to Hakham Ovadia. The problem is that you will not leave the bedroom light on at night. The toilet light is not suitable, because the Gemara's rules about oil require that the light be one you would want to stay near. Where nothing else is possible, the best practical solution is to bring a timer, switch it on and say the berakhah.

LEDs. More and more homes use LEDs, which are not a flame and give off no heat. The Rabbi doubted that a berakhah can be made on them, and said it is getting harder to rely on this solution.

What is a family unit?

The Mishnah Berurah, following the Maharshal, says that an adult son who depends on his parents is part of their unit. This is the same as for Hanukkah, with a son in yeshiva whose parents pay his fees and give him an allowance. If he supports himself and pays for everything, he must light for himself. The same applies to Shabbat. The Rabbi said one should look at how the person actually lives, who pays for his food, and not at loans or technicalities.

A guest who paid to join the host's candles and is then moved to a separate room before Shabbat takes his money back. He then lights in that room with a berakhah, because now there is a hovat dira in that room.

Seif 8: several families eating together

The Shulhan Arukh records a dispute when several families eat together, for example in a hotel. Some say each family lights with a berakhah, since more light means more enjoyment. Others disagree. The Rabbi found the first view easy to hear when the light came from candles, but in a room already lit, "a hundred women lighting tiny tea lights" adds nothing. He also said it is worth asking where the practice of several women lighting in one house comes from.

Ashkenazim and Sefaradim. Ashkenazim in hotels, he noted, have all the women light and make the berakhah. For Sefaradim that is not possible, so the options are:

Situation

What a Sefaradi woman can do

Several women in one dining room

The first woman lights with a berakhah. Others do not light with a berakhah.

She wants to be part of another's berakhah

Ask one woman to have the others in mind (be motzi)

She wants to light herself

Light in a different room she is permitted to use, where she will actually be

A large hall with the other side of the room far away

Perhaps the far side counts as a separate place

For a synagogue Friday-night dinner, the Rabbi said that where there is already electric light nobody needs to light, and where the hall would be dark one person lights with a berakhah. A woman who lit at home cannot light again with a berakhah in the hall, though she should make sure there is light there.

Practical summary

  • A man at home with his wife may light in other rooms, but without a berakhah, because his wife's berakhah covers the house.
  • A man away from home who has his own room lights there with a berakhah, even if his wife lights at home.
  • A single guest who shares the host's space buys a share in the host's candles with a small payment.
  • In a hotel, a timer with a berakhah is the practical solution, since candles are usually not allowed in the room. The Rabbi doubted that LEDs work.
  • An adult child dependent on his parents is part of their unit, in the same way as for Hanukkah.
  • Where several women eat together, Ashkenazim all light with a berakhah, while Sefaradim have one woman light with a berakhah, or a woman lights in another room that she is using.
