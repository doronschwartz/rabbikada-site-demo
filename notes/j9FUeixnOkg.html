Pausing for the Yamim Noraim

After several months on the laws of the Sefer Torah and the Torah reading, the class paused to prepare for Rosh Hashanah, focusing on the shofar, because "the essence of Rosh Hashanah is shofar": the Torah calls the day Yom Teruah.

The Rabbi first set out where the laws of Rosh Hashanah sit in the Shulhan Arukh:

  • 581: the month of Elul, the custom of Selihot, and fasting on Erev Rosh Hashanah.
  • 582: mistakes in the Amidah, such as forgetting HaMelekh HaKadosh or HaMelekh HaMishpat, where one goes back.
  • 583: the simanim, the foods eaten on Rosh Hashanah night.
  • 584: the Torah reading on Rosh Hashanah.
  • 585: the blessing over the shofar, five paragraphs, the subject of this class.

The shofar is blown standing

The Rema notes the custom to blow from the tevah, the same place the Torah is read from. This is a good custom but not essential: if a building were extended and it made more sense for the baal tokeah to stand elsewhere, that would be fine.

More important is that the shofar must be blown and heard standing. Hazal learn this from a gezerah shavah with the Omer. Of Rosh Hashanah the Torah says "a day of teruah it shall be for you", and of the Omer it says "you shall count for yourselves". The Omer is counted "from when the sickle is first put to the kamah", the standing crop, and Hazal read kamah as komah, standing. So the Omer is counted standing, and so is the shofar. The Mishnah Berurah adds the general rule that where a misvah is done standing, its berakhah is also said standing.

What counts as standing? The test is simple: if whatever you are leaning on were taken away, would you still be standing? If yes, you are standing. If you would fall over, halachically you are sitting, and you have not fulfilled the requirement to stand.

The verse is only an asmakhta, a support for a rabbinic rule. The Rabbi described two understandings of asmakhta: either the Torah was deliberately written to leave room for the Hakhamim to attach their rulings to it, or it is simply a memory aid that the Torah itself did not intend. Either way, a person who is ill or elderly may sit, and someone who cannot get out of bed fulfils the misvah lying down.

How many blasts, and why

Before asking why we sit, the Rabbi explained the structure of the blowing.

The Torah level is nine. The Torah mentions teruah three times, and the verse veHaavarta shofar teruah teaches that each teruah is "announced" with a tekiah before and after it. Three times three is nine.

Why thirty? The Gemara is unsure what a teruah is: a groaning sound (genuhei ganah, what we call shevarim), a short wailing, trilling sound (yelulei yalil, what we call teruah), or a combination of both. To cover every opinion we blow:

Strictly it should be 27, since shevarim-teruah is one sound according to the opinion that requires it, but we count it as two notes, which gives thirty. The first set is twelve because it has four sounds in each round.

Sixty and a hundred. The Hakhamim added blowing during the Musaf prayer, so that "your remembrance should rise up for good". These blasts are part of the tefillah and have no meaning on their own. So when blowing for someone at home, there is no point blowing sixty: blow thirty, and if they are praying, they should blow again during their own Amidah. The extra forty that complete a hundred are a later custom from the time of the Arizal. Temanim blow only sixty.

Oriental Sefaradim blow during the silent Amidah of Musaf as well, while Ashkenazim and the Spanish and Portuguese blow only in the repetition.

So why do we sit?

The Rabbi explained that we rely on the fact that we will be standing later, during the blasts of Musaf. Since the congregation will anyway stand then, Hazal did not trouble them to stand for the first thirty. That is why they are called the tekiot meyushav, the "seated" blowings. According to the Mishnah Berurah, Ashkenazim stand for these as well.

This has a practical consequence. Someone who knows he must leave straight after the first thirty, such as a doctor on call or someone with a headache, must stand for them, because he will not hear the later blowing. The same applies when blowing in someone's home: unless the listener is ill or elderly, both the blower and the listener should stand.

Someone asked whether standing alone would be showing off in a shul where everyone sits. The Rabbi contrasted it with standing for Kaddish. Standing for Kaddish is an Ashkenazi custom of the Rema, not a requirement, and standing alone where the custom is to sit separates you from the community. Here, however, "this is the halacha", so the person should stand, ideally inconspicuously at the back and without leaning.

Pausing between the sets

The Rabbi raised a practice that has spread in some places: pausing for half a minute or so between the sets of the first thirty to recite a special viduy from the Arizal. He called it "a huge halachic issue". The blessing has already been said, and pausing to speak looks like an interruption (hefsek). Those who defend it argue that all thirty blasts are needed, and that teshuvah is part of the misvah of shofar, so it is not an interruption.

Still, Rav Ovadia Yosef was very much against the practice. "If you really want to do it, you do it in your head. You don't say it."

He also remarked that many Sefaradi practices in tefillah follow the Arizal rather than the Shulhan Arukh, such as the order of putting on tefillin, even though in halacha the Shulhan Arukh remains the basis.

The blower, the berakhah, and whom it covers

Who blows. The Rema says the most righteous person in the community should blow, and above all there should be no quarrelling over it. The blower must at least have in mind to fulfil the misvah for the whole community. Someone asked about a blower who deliberately excludes a person he has fallen out with. That person does not fulfil the misvah, the Rabbi said, though in his view the blower himself still does, since a thought cannot undo an action. He compared the Kohen who has animosity towards someone in the shul: he should not perform Birkat Kohanim at all, because it must be said "with love".

The berakhah. The blessing is lishmoa kol shofar, "to hear the sound of the shofar", not "to blow". The Mishnah proves the point: someone who blows into a pit fulfils the misvah only if he heard the shofar itself and not the echo. "The misvah is to hear."

Blowing again for others. One who has already fulfilled the misvah may still blow and say the berakhah for others. But the Mishnah Berurah says that if the listener knows the berakhah, it is better that he say it himself. The Rabbi applied the same principle to Kiddush on Friday night: if you already made Kiddush in shul, it is better for family members who can read to say it themselves, although it is not a berakhah levatalah if you say it for them. Shabbat morning Kiddush is different, because the berakhah there is a blessing on enjoyment, which can be said again.

Barukh Hu uVarukh Shemo. Some say it during the berakhot of the shofar and some do not. Better not to, but those whose established custom it is, following Moroccan poskim and the Spanish and Portuguese, should be left alone.

Holding the shofar. It is good to blow from the right side of the mouth and to point the shofar upwards, since the right represents rahamim, mercy.

The next question, what happens if the blower loses his breath partway through and someone else takes over, was left for the following week.

Practical summary

  • The shofar must be blown and heard standing. Leaning so heavily that you would fall if the support were removed does not count as standing. The ill and elderly may sit.
  • The Torah requires nine blasts; we blow thirty to cover the doubt about what a teruah is. The blasts during Musaf are part of the prayer; the extra forty are a later custom.
  • Sefaradim sit for the first thirty because they stand for the blasts during Musaf. Anyone who will not hear those later blasts must stand for the first thirty.
  • Rav Ovadia Yosef strongly opposed pausing to recite viduy aloud between the sets; if one wishes, think it without speaking.
  • The berakhah is "to hear the sound of the shofar". Someone who already fulfilled the misvah may blow and bless for others, but a listener who knows the berakhah should ideally say it himself.
  • Better not to say Barukh Hu uVarukh Shemo during the berakhah, but do not object where it is the established custom.
