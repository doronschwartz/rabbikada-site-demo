Topic: Why dikduk is essential: where the stress falls, the dagesh, the pronunciation of the letters, and the halachic and Talmudic sources Shiur: Part 1 of Dayan Daniel Kada's series on the rules of Lashon HaKodesh, given on Zoom, Tuesday 8 February 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A new series

The Rabbi opened a four-part series on dikduk, Hebrew grammar. This first class asks why it matters. The next weeks would cover the rules, and the fourth class an introduction to hazzanut and the maqamat with a guest hazzan.

He explained his own interest. He grew up in a Syrian home but went to Ashkenazi schools, and at his Bar Misvah argued with his father about how to read. Later, studying Biblical Hebrew, he fell in love with grammar, and he came to see how often Hebrew is read incorrectly. At school, the Rashis on grammar were skipped. "For me that was wrong," he said. Without correct reading a person cannot understand the text properly.

The word dikduk comes from dak, fine or thin: breaking words down into roots, prefixes and suffixes, and knowing where the stress falls and how each letter sounds.

Where the stress falls

English stresses the first syllable: Heathrow, Manchester, cupboard, table. Words stressed on the last syllable usually come from French, where the stress falls at the end. Hebrew is the opposite. About 95 percent of the time the stress is on the last syllable (milra), and when in doubt that is where it goes: Moshe, Daniel, VeAtah, Tesaveh, Yisrael, Terumah.

The Rabbi argued that Sepharadim have an advantage here. Ashkenazim lived among Yiddish and German speakers, whose stress falls early, and read Hebrew through the lens of their mother tongue, so the natural Ashkenazi reading puts the stress on the wrong syllable: SHAB-bos, KI Sisa, SED-ra. He stressed that he was not discussing vowels ("oy" or "o"); even in Ashkenazi pronunciation the stress should still be at the end. A participant asked about milel and milra: milel means "above", the stress is not on the last syllable; milra means "below", on the last.

Someone asked whether this was a problem in Rashi's time. The Rabbi thought it was a later problem, the product of Eastern European culture. A participant who grew up in a Sepharadi family and went to an Ashkenazi school added her own memories of how names were said there.

Stress changes the meaning

Does it matter whether one says MO-she or Mo-SHE? Very much. The Ibn Ezra says that before anything else a person must learn to read.

The Rabbi showed a source sheet. In Parashat Vayese, Rahel "was coming" with her sheep, and three verses later Rahel "had come". The word is spelled identically, באה, with the same vowels. The only difference is the taam: stressed on the last syllable (ba-AH) it is present, she is coming; stressed on the first (BA-ah) it is past, she came. Rashi points this out. Read both the same way and you have misread the verse. Similarly at the end of Bereshit, הבאה describes the souls "who came" to Egypt; reading the stress wrongly changes the meaning.

The dagesh changes the meaning

When Moshe comes down the mountain and Yehoshua hears a noise like battle, Moshe says it is not the sound of victory or defeat but "kol annot anokhi shomea". With a dagesh in the nun, annot comes from inui, affliction: Rashi explains it as the sound of blasphemy and insult that afflicts the soul. Without the dagesh, anot would mean answering. "99% of people reading this pasuk next week" will miss the dagesh and change the meaning.

The same root appears in the Selihot. "Anenu Elohei Avraham anenu" means "answer us". Add a dagesh, "annenu", and you are asking the God of Avraham to afflict us, twice. Another example: "Hodu" means give thanks; Hodu is also India, as in "from Hodu to Kush" in the Megillah. With the stress on the first syllable the word sounds like the country.

A halachic issue

The Gemara says a person's learning can be recognised by the way he speaks. But pronouncing the letters is also halacha. The Rambam in Hilchot Tefillah rules that someone who reads aleph as ayin and ayin as aleph may not serve as hazzan, and in Hilchot Birkat Kohanim that such a person may not perform Birkat Kohanim, like those who said "sibbolet" for "shibbolet". This comes from the Gemara in Megillah and is codified in the Shulhan Arukh.

Ashkenazim did not distinguish aleph from ayin. The Magen Avraham explains that the rule applies only where others do distinguish; if the whole community speaks this way, it is acceptable. The Taz says similarly that most people today cannot tell them apart, so the rule is not applied. The Rabbi suggested that today, when Ashkenazim and Sepharadim live side by side and everyone knows the difference, the old justification may no longer hold. He called this an argument of his own, not one found in the poskim.

A participant asked why the answer was not simply to educate, as other campaigns have done. The Rabbi agreed that education is right. Perhaps in Poland in the 1600s people did not realise any other reading existed. He mentioned Ashkenazi grammar works that do teach these rules, and noted there is no equivalent Sepharadi book.

Fatal consequences

The Gemara asks whether to hire a teacher who covers a lot but is not precise, or one who is precise but covers less. Rav Dimi says the precise one, because a mistake once learned stays. The proof: Yoav killed only the males of Edom. When David asked why, Yoav said the Torah says to wipe out "zakhar Amalek", the males. David replied that the word is "zekher", the memory, meaning everyone. Yoav went to his teacher, who confirmed he had taught "zakhar". Yoav drew his sword, quoting "cursed is the one who does the work of Hashem deceitfully". Some say he killed him, some say not; either way, one wrong vowel had grave consequences.

The Gemara in Eruvin adds that the people of Yehudah, who were careful with their language, kept their Torah, while the people of the Galil, who were not, did not.

The letters begad kefet

Six letters, bet, gimel, dalet, kaf, peh and tav, must sound different with and without a dagesh. Everyone distinguishes kaf and peh. For bet, most say "v" without a dagesh, but the Syrian, Iraqi and Spanish and Portuguese custom is "b" in both cases. The Rabbi's understanding is that without a dagesh it should be a softer "b", which he cannot produce himself, so he uses "v". He brought support from the Gemara on reading the Shema carefully: "esev besadekha" is only easy to slur together if the two letters sound almost alike.

Gimel without a dagesh is a guttural "gh", as in Baghdad; English words like daughter preserve a trace of it. The Israeli resh is really this gimel. "If you can't roll your resh, do an English r," he said, but not a different letter. Someone asked about Yemenites, who read gimel with a dagesh as "j" and without as "g": they too distinguish, and everyone agrees there must be a distinction.

A participant suggested making resources to teach children these sounds while they are young. The Rabbi said Sepharadim certainly should be doing this, and that it was a fair point to think about.

Summary

  • Hebrew is usually stressed on the last syllable; the culture around us shapes how we read.
  • Wrong stress or a missing dagesh can change the meaning of a verse or a prayer.
  • Pronouncing the letters correctly is a halachic matter for the hazzan and for Birkat Kohanim.
  • The Gemara shows that mistakes learned young are hard to undo, and that careful speech preserves Torah.
