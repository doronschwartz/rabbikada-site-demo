Topic: Pidyon shevuyim, redeeming captives: the limit on ransom, its exceptions, and the hostages taken on 7 October Sources: Mishnah and Gemara, Gittin 45a and 58a; Tosafot; the Rambam, Hilchot Matenot Aniyim 8:12; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah 252:4 with the Pithei Teshuvah Shiur: The first class of the Torat Am Yisrael series, given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Tiferet Eyal, in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 7 November 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A new series, one month on

Dayan Kada opened a new series dedicated to the safety of Am Yisrael, and especially the soldiers putting themselves on the line. The day before had marked the shloshim of the massacre, and the shiurim were dedicated to the memory of those murdered al Kiddush HaShem, to the hostages, that Hashem bring them "from darkness to light", and to the recovery of the injured. Later classes would deal with civilian casualties in war and with honey traps. Tonight's subject was the captives, then numbering about 240.

He made two introductory points. First, Hazal teach that one who studies the laws of an offering is as if he brought it. By learning this sugya from the Gemara to the contemporary poskim, we are, in a sense, engaged in the misvah of redeeming the hostages.

Second, pidyon shevuyim is one of the greatest misvot. A Sefer Torah may be sold for only three things: to support Torah study, to marry off an orphan girl, and to redeem captives. The Rambam writes that it includes many misvot: loving your neighbour, hesed, walking in Hashem's ways, and "do not stand idly by your neighbour's blood".

Not more than they are worth

The Mishnah in Gittin rules: "We do not redeem captives for more than their worth, because of tikkun haolam." The Gemara offers two explanations:

  • The burden on the community. Excessive ransoms drain communal funds needed for schools, mikvaot and synagogues.
  • Not encouraging the captors. If we pay huge sums, they will go and take more hostages.

Rashi points to a practical difference: a wealthy father or uncle who wants to pay privately. On the first reason he may; on the second, we stop him.

The Rif and the Rosh did not decide between the reasons. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh rule like the second: the concern is encouraging more kidnapping, so even a relative paying from his own pocket may not exceed the normal price. A captive may redeem himself for any sum, since it is his own life and money. And there is one exception: a talmid hakham, or a young student who shows exceptional promise, may be redeemed even at great cost.

Dayan Kada asked the class to note one thing carefully: the entire discussion so far is about money.

Rabbi Yishmael and the three answers of Tosafot

The exception comes from a story in Gittin 58a. A great Tanna came to a town and heard of a Jewish boy held captive there. He quoted the first half of a verse from Yeshayahu about Israel being handed over to plunderers, and the boy answered with the second half. Seeing his potential, the Tanna swore not to leave until the boy was redeemed at any price. The boy grew up to be Rabbi Yishmael ben Elisha.

Tosafot ask how this fits the Mishnah, and give three answers:

  1. A talmid hakham or potential scholar is different.
  2. A time of destruction. When the Jewish people are anyway at the mercy of their enemies, the concern of encouraging captors no longer applies. The Ramban gives this answer too.
  3. Danger to life. Where there is real danger, one may pay more.

Someone asked whether a leading scientist would count like a talmid hakham. He thought not: the exception rests on the merit of Torah that the person will spread among Am Yisrael.

Two historic cases

The Maharam of Lublin (sixteenth-century Poland) was asked about a young man jailed after an affair with a non-Jewish woman, for whom the authorities demanded a huge sum. How a person came to be captive, he ruled, is irrelevant. But the price was excessive and the man was not a talmid hakham, so the community could not pay.

The Pithei Teshuvah records the debate that followed. The Bet Hillel objected that Tosafot allow paying where life is in danger. The Knesset Yehezkel answered that this answer of Tosafot works only according to the financial reason; according to the reason we follow, danger to life is all the more reason not to pay, since more captives mean more lives at risk. "As you can see, this is a clear mahloket."

The Maharam of Rothenburg, the leading German Rishon of the thirteenth century, was imprisoned. His student the Rosh fled to Spain, where he became an authority for the Sefaradim. The Maharam's students raised a huge ransom, but he forbade them to pay it. He died in prison after seven years, having continued to write teshuvot from his cell. The Hida, in Shem HaGedolim, records that his body was released only after a wealthy man paid for it; the Maharam appeared to him in a dream and offered him wealth for his descendants or a place in Olam HaBa, and he chose Olam HaBa and was buried beside the Maharam in Worms.

Why did the Maharam refuse, if a talmid hakham may be ransomed? The Maharshal answers that normally captors cannot tell who is a scholar. The Maharam was seized precisely because of his stature, so paying for him would have made every leading rabbi a target. Perhaps, too, he chose to go beyond the letter of the law.

What about today?

If we stopped here, Dayan Kada said, there would be no justification for an excessive price for the 240 hostages. And Hamas do not ask for money; they ask for terrorists. Even if we equate the two, a reasonable exchange elsewhere in the world is one or a few for one. The Gilad Shalit exchange, over a thousand for one, would seem from these sources to have been wrong.

But Rav Asher Weiss, one of the leading poskim of today, argues that the classical sources do not compare:

  • Money, not torture. The Gemara and the teshuvot speak of captivity one could live through; the Maharam was writing teshuvot in prison. Here people are held by barbarians and face continuous suffering. "Hazal's whole concern was about money."
  • What is reasonable? The Radbaz in Egypt already asked how to value a person once there is no slave market.
  • A sovereign state. Every discussion from the Mishnah onward took place in exile. A sovereign state has other considerations.

The closest precedent is Entebbe. Rav Ovadia Yosef wrote a teshuvah, in the Hoshen Mishpat section of his responsa, when the Israeli government asked the hakhamim about an exchange. While they were deliberating, the Prime Minister came to tell them that the rescue had succeeded, and the teshuvah ends with praise to Hashem for the salvation. One of his central arguments: if soldiers do not know that the country will always come for them, their morale falls. We judge by the situation in front of us now, even if there is a risk later.

Dayan Kada concluded that there are strong grounds to say there is very little cap today: the issue is torture and not money, we have a sovereign state, and the soldiers must know they will be saved, especially as many of the hostages are soldiers. "Now there are 240 people at stake."

Questions from the class

  • A Jew jailed for a crime? In a democratic prison in England or America he is not a captive in the halachic sense; that is a consequence of his actions.
  • Released terrorists can kill, and money cannot. A strong point, he agreed. But the duty now is to save those in front of us, and to strengthen defences, surveillance and vetting for the future.
  • Who decides? In practice the government. The halachic arguments give grounds in both directions, so it becomes a strategic decision.
  • Is this war a milhemet misvah? That, he said, is a central question for a later class, and it may change the picture.

Practical summary

  • Redeeming captives is among the greatest misvot; a Sefer Torah may be sold for it.
  • The Mishnah forbids paying more than a captive's worth. The Rambam and Shulhan Arukh rule that the reason is not to encourage kidnapping, so it applies even to private payment; a talmid hakham is an exception, and a person may ransom himself at any price.
  • Whether danger to life permits a higher price is disputed among the Aharonim.
  • Rav Asher Weiss argues that the classical cap concerned money, and that torture, sovereignty and the need to back soldiers change the picture.
  • Dayan Kada's view: there are strong grounds that there is little cap on freeing the hostages now, while the government must weigh the wider strategy.
