These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A night like day

After a class the previous week on bedikat hames and hekhsherim, this class went through the Seder, explaining both what we do and why. It is the only night of the year when we say Hallel, and twice: in the prayers and at the Seder. Night is usually a time of concealment, but this night's light is so strong that "the night shines like the day". Everything below applies equally to women unless stated otherwise.

The plate. Why three massot? The simple reason: two whole massot as on every Yom Tov, plus one to break. The shank bone recalls the Pesah offering ("an outstretched arm"); the egg recalls the festival offering. One reason given for an egg, a sign of mourning, is that Tisha BeAv always falls on the same weekday as the first night of Pesah.

When to start

That year the first Seder was on Friday night, with nightfall at about 8.50pm and midnight just after 1am. The Ben Ish Hai writes that Shalom Aleikhem and Eshet Hayil are still said, and these can be said before nightfall. Only two misvot of the night are from the Torah: eating massah and telling the story. These must be at night, and the rabbinic misvot should be too.

  • First night. Get everything ready, everyone in their seat with their cup and drink, and begin Kadesh at nightfall. If people cannot last, there is room to start Kiddush from sunset (about 8pm), since the four cups are rabbinic. Arvit may be prayed early. In shul the compromise was to pray from sunset: "the complaints in shul come before the complaints at home."
  • Second night, Mosei Shabbat. One may not prepare on Shabbat for Yom Tov. Ashkenazim (the Mishnah Berurah) wait until nightfall to set the table. Sephardim may begin from sunset, since a rabbinic prohibition is set aside at twilight for a misvah. Kiddush itself waits until nightfall, since it includes Havdalah.

Kadesh: the four cups

  • Wine or grape juice? The view that pasteurised wine or grape juice is invalid for Kiddush is the Rambam's; the Shulhan Arukh's main view is that one fulfils the misvah. If you use grape juice every Friday night, there is no reason to switch at the Seder. If you are strict all year, be strict tonight too. Someone who cannot drink wine or grape juice at all should not endanger himself.
  • Red is preferred. For someone who gets migraines from red wine, the Rabbi suggested white wine with a few drops of red. The Mishnah Berurah objects on grounds of dyeing on Yom Tov, but we hold there is no dyeing with liquids.
  • How much? On Friday night most of a reviit (about 44 ml) by one person suffices. At the Seder everyone must drink, and a full reviit (86 ml), because besides Kiddush there is the misvah of freedom: "you can't create a shaliah to experience freedom for you." Drinking most of a large cup is the best way; 44 ml is enough after the fact. Drink in one or two gulps, not sips.
  • Leaning. Sephardim are far stricter. The Shulhan Arukh rules that one who did not lean for the massah or the cups must go back, and in order: cup one, Urhas, karpas, Maggid. Lean to the left, a movement of the whole body. Ashkenazim rely after the fact on the Raavyah, who held leaning is not needed today.
  • Women and leaning. The Shulhan Arukh says a woman leans only if she is a "woman of standing" (hashuvah), since only such women leaned in those days. "It's not a chauvinistic kind of thing." The Rema writes that our women are all considered important, yet Ashkenazi women do not lean, relying on the Raavyah. In practice Sephardi men and women lean equally: "nowadays we consider all of our women to be hashuvot, Barukh HaShem."
  • Rinsing the cup. By the Kabbalah one rinses between cups; by halacha one may simply top it up. He was told that Rav Ovadia Yosef did not rinse.
  • Children are not obliged to drink the four cups. If money is tight, spend it on treats that will excite them rather than on wine.

Urhas, karpas and Yahas

Sephardim wash before eating any wet food dipped in liquid all year round, so Urhas is not "to make the children ask"; that is the Ashkenazi reason. Karpas is celery or parsley; Ashkenazim in Europe used potatoes. We do not lean, since it recalls the tears of slavery, and eat less than a kazayit so the berakhah extends to the maror.

The middle massah is broken because the Haggadah is said over broken bread. The Rabbi does not like the custom of children stealing it and holding it to ransom: "You teach children to steal things?" It comes from the Rambam's word for snatching it away, which meant putting it aside.

Maggid: the freedom to ask

Every night we remember the Exodus. What is unique tonight? Pesah commemorates not only physical freedom but intellectual freedom. When Moshe asked Pharaoh for a three-day holiday, Pharaoh increased the work: if they have time to think, they are not working hard enough. In a dictatorship people may not ask questions, and he gave the example of Russia at the time. The Arizal reads Pesah as peh sah, the mouth that speaks.

So even someone alone, as many were at the start of Covid, still asks the questions. "We're asking questions to demonstrate herut." The question is not only a means to an answer.

  • Maggid is not a prayer. In prayer, standing before HaShem counts even without understanding. In Maggid, "if you don't understand what you're saying, you wasted your time." What must be said is the questions, the answer, Rabban Gamliel's three things, and the Hallel. The rest is "a springboard for discussion". Better to read in English than Hebrew you do not understand, which is why in many homes it was read in Arabic. Women should say at least these core parts and join in the discussion.
  • The whole night. Telling the story has no fixed limit. Where does the Shulhan Arukh say to stay up all Shavuot night? It does not, yet we do. On Pesah, falling asleep over a book about the Exodus is fine, but after Maggid "now I discuss the sport? Has veshalom."

Massah, maror and Korekh

  • Only the head of the house eats two kezeitim at Mosi Massah, from the whole and the broken massah. Everyone else, especially the women, needs one, then one at Korekh and one at the afikoman: three in total. "I say this every year, and people are surprised. This is halacha," as Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul and Rav Ovadia Yosef write.
  • Submerge the maror in haroset, then shake it off so its bitterness remains. At Korekh there is no need to shake it off.
  • From the berakhah on massah until everyone has finished Korekh, no unrelated talk, since the berakhot on massah and maror cover Korekh too. "Pass me the haroset" and "is this a kazayit?" are fine.

From the questions. A china tea saucer cannot be kashered, but it was at most a second vessel, which does not absorb. And the Shulhan Arukh follows its main use, so wash it and use it. Glass, even used in the oven, need only be washed. Followers of the Ben Ish Hai are stricter.

The wicked son's question

Why are we so harsh with the wicked son? "Lakhem, to you", and not to him; but the wise son also says "you". The Rabbi preferred an answer from the Yerushalmi brought by the Ritva: the problem is the word avodah, service. The opening of Shemot uses it five times in two verses for the slavery. Then HaShem tells Moshe the sign: "you will serve (taavdun) God on this mountain." To the wicked son nothing has changed: one master was Pharaoh, now the master is God. Look at all the shopping, cleaning and cooking it took to get here.

A medical student in his sixth year of hard work who asks "what is all this for?" is waving a red flag. Torah is hard work, but so is much in life that we value. "When you only see the avodah," that is the problem.

Practical summary

  • Begin Kiddush at nightfall; if necessary from sunset. On Mosei Shabbat, Sephardim may prepare the table from sunset.
  • Each person drinks a reviit, leaning to the left; Sephardi women lean too. Grape juice is fine if used all year.
  • Children need not drink four cups; money is better spent on what keeps them engaged.
  • Maggid must be understood; read in a language you know, and keep discussing the Exodus through the night.
  • Three kezeitim of massah for everyone, two at Mosi Massah only for the head of the house; no unrelated talk until after Korekh.
