Topic: Eating the massah at the Seder: which massah the kezayit comes from, who needs two, chewing, amounts and timing, leaning, and not speaking until after Korekh Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 475 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom in the first lockdown, Wednesday 1 April 2020

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where the recording begins

This was one of a series of lockdown classes on the Seder, a week before Pesah, when many people were preparing to make their first Seder at home. On Monday night the Rabbi had covered Kadesh and the misvah of telling the story of the Exodus. The recording begins part-way through Mosi Massah, just after the bottom massah is put down, and ends as he begins Maror.

To recap the two berakhot: - For HaMosi, hold all three massot, so that the berakhah is said over two whole massot. - Then put down the bottom one and say Al Akhilat Massah over the top two: the whole one and the broken one.

Two misvot from the Torah

Only two misvot on Seder night are from the Torah: telling the story of the Exodus, which he had covered on Monday, and eating massah, as the verse says, "In the evening you shall eat massot." The maror, karpas and the rest are rabbinic.

One kezayit fulfils the misvah from the Torah. The question is which massah it should come from: - Some say the top massah, because we do not pass over a misvah: the first one we reach is the massah of the misvah. - Others say the middle, broken massah, because that is the "bread of affliction", ha lahma anya, the broken bread.

So the halacha in the Shulhan Arukh is to eat two kezeitim, one from the top massah and one from the middle massah, "although, strictly speaking, one kezayit is sufficient".

Only the one leading the Seder needs two

The Rabbi stressed that this applies to the person conducting the Seder. At a normal Seder, with five or ten people round the table, the head of the household eats his kezeitim from the top and middle massot. He then gives everyone else a small piece of each and makes up the amount from the box, since those two massot could never provide a kezayit for everyone.

In that case there is no need for everyone else to have two. The second kezayit is only because of the doubt about which massah the misvah comes from. Someone eating massah from the box, not from the leader's massot, has no such doubt, so one kezayit is enough. "That's clear. There's no need to have two kezeitim."

This year, he noted, many couples would be making the Seder alone. Then it makes sense for both husband and wife to take a kezayit from each massah.

Asked whether the others should still be given a piece of the middle massah, he said yes: it is symbolic, and "it's nice".

When chewing is hard

The hard, brittle massah is "an Ashkenazi problem, not a Sefaradi problem". Even for Ashkenazim, he said, the Rema shows that soft massah was once normal; hard massah came with industrial baking, months in advance. "The soft massah is the real thing", but it is hard to get and expensive, so most people will eat hard massah.

For the elderly, people without teeth or anyone who cannot chew: - You may break up or crush the massah and swallow it, though try to chew it a little, since we want the taste of massah. - Even if you only swallow it, you have fulfilled the misvah: one who swallows massah has fulfilled the obligation. - If even that is too hard, you may soften it in water. That is not the first choice, but it is far better than not eating massah at all.

Someone asked whether massah may be crushed on Yom Tov. Yes, and even on Shabbat: the prohibition of grinding does not apply to something already made, just as bread may be broken up on Shabbat.

The massah must taste of massah

At least the first kezayit must be eaten plain, because we need the taste of massah. "I know people are excited about the haroset. Don't start smearing your haroset on the massah before you get there."

Those who cannot eat wheat can buy oat or spelt massot, but these are only for people who need them; the first choice is wheat. He had tried oat massot: very expensive, not very tasty, and "three or four in a box", barely enough for one person on Seder night.

How much, how fast, and leaning

  • How much: a kezayit is about 27 grams. There are different opinions on volume and weight, so aim for a substantial amount; measuring about 30 grams beforehand is sufficient.
  • Measuring on Yom Tov: normally one may not measure on Shabbat or Yom Tov, but for a misvah it is allowed, so if you did not measure before the festival, you may do so then.
  • How fast: eat the kezayit in one go, not a bite now and another later, within about four minutes. If that is hard, for example for the elderly, one has until seven or even nine minutes, "but certainly not more than that".
  • Leaning: the massah must be eaten leaning, and as he explained on Monday, leaning means leaning on something, a chair or a cushion, not tilting into the air. Leaning is part of the misvah: if you did not lean, you must eat again.

Someone asked about a leader who leaned for one of his two kezeitim but not the other. He has fulfilled the misvah and does not repeat it. Only if he did not lean at all must he eat again.

No talking until after Korekh

Some people say the berakhah, eat the massah and start chatting. Others sit humming and pointing because they think they cannot speak while the maror and haroset go round. The Rabbi explained the halacha.

The berakhah Al Akhilat Massah covers not only this massah but also the massah eaten in Korekh, the sandwich. So the Shulhan Arukh writes that one should not speak from the berakhah until after eating the massah, the maror and the Korekh. After those three, conversation can begin with the meal.

But anything needed for the misvah may be said. Asking someone to pass the maror, the haroset or what you need for Korekh is permitted; you do not need to signal with your hands. Only unrelated talk should wait.

Maror

The recording ends as he began Maror, the lettuce. The Torah mentions maror only together with the Pesah offering: "with massot and bitter herbs they shall eat it". Massah has a separate verse of its own, so it remains a Torah misvah today, but maror is a Torah misvah only when there is a Pesah offering. Today it is rabbinic, though still "a very important misvah", eaten to remember the offering or the bitterness our ancestors went through.

Practical summary

  • The person leading the Seder eats two kezeitim, one from the top massah and one from the middle massah, because of a doubt about which one the misvah comes from. Everyone else eating from the box needs only one, with a symbolic piece of the leader's massot.
  • A kezayit is about 27 to 30 grams, eaten in one go within about four minutes (up to seven or nine if necessary), leaning on something.
  • The first kezayit is eaten plain, without haroset. Someone who cannot chew may crush the massah, or if necessary soften it in water; swallowing still fulfils the misvah.
  • Wheat massah is the first choice; oat or spelt is for those who cannot eat wheat.
  • From Al Akhilat Massah until after Korekh, speak only about what is needed for the massah, maror and Korekh.
