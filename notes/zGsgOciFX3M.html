Pausing Shabbat for Rosh Hashanah

The Thursday class had just finished Mukseh in Hilchot Shabbat. With three classes left before Rosh Hashanah, the Rabbi paused Shabbat until after Sukkot to learn the laws of Rosh Hashanah and the shofar, which the group had never studied together. These run from Siman 581 to 603. Most of the day follows the ordinary laws of Yom Tov; the topic that needs depth is the shofar. The first chapters are mostly custom, so the class moved quickly.

Siman 581: Selihot in Elul

The Shulhan Arukh says it is customary to rise early for Selihot from Rosh Hodesh Elul until Yom Kippur. The whole chapter is minhag. The Gemara never mentions Selihot, but the custom was already widespread in the time of the Geonim. The Rambam records saying them only in the Ten Days of Teshuvah. The core was always the Thirteen Attributes, with a few passages from the Geonim; later generations added more. The Rabbi compared this to Shaharit, whose core from the Gemara (Barkhu, the blessings of the Shema, the Shema, the Amidah and Tahanun) takes fifteen or twenty minutes with kavanah. His warning: "the more you add, the less it's done properly." If you have only half an hour, spend it on the core. Why forty days? Moshe Rabbenu went up for forty days to ask forgiveness, and the last letters of "Ani ledodi vedodi li" are four yuds, forty.

The four days recall a korban, which was kept and checked for blemishes for four days before it was offered. When Rosh Hashanah falls on Monday or Tuesday, as this year, Ashkenazim begin on the Sunday of the week before, so as to start at the beginning of a week.

When to say Selihot

The ideal time is from midnight until dawn, an et rason, a time of favour. Nightfall to midnight is a time of din, judgment. Today many say Selihot later in the day, and that is fine; earlier is better. Before midnight is not forbidden halakhically, but the Hida, citing the Arizal, writes strongly against it. Rav Gaguine records in Keter Shem Tov that the Spanish and Portuguese in England used to say the next morning's Selihot straight after Arvit, because the mornings were too cold and dark. This week a small community in England asked him about a weekly Selihot at 10 or 10.30 at night, since people would come neither in the morning nor at midnight. His answer: if the alternative is no Selihot at all, do it then, though the Kabbalists dislike it; if a better time is possible, use it. Some poskim also hold that for spiritual matters, unlike legal times such as the Shema, midnight follows Jerusalem, about two hours earlier than London.

The Hazan, fasting and Erev Rosh Hashanah

For the Yamim Noraim one looks for the most God-fearing person to lead, preferably at least thirty, like the Levites who began singing at thirty, and married, like the Kohen Gadol on Yom Kippur. Yet "every Jew is kasher": even a mamzer may be Hazan. Above all, the community must want him. Someone asked whether a Hazan or shofar blower could leave out one person he dislikes. Legally, the Rabbi explained, including others works through agency, so if he does not intend to include that person, that person has not fulfilled the misvah. Morally it is completely wrong, and he warned that whoever plays with this puts the whole congregation at risk because of his ego. Other customs in the chapter: - Many used to fast on Erev Rosh Hashanah, and the Rema mentions some who fasted ten days. These fasts end at sunset, have no Torah reading, and are set aside for a brit milah. - Tahanun is not said on Erev Rosh Hashanah, but viduy is said in Selihot, which belong to before dawn. When said after dawn, the Spanish and Portuguese leave out the viduy; the Kaf HaHayim, and generally the Eastern Sefaradim today, say it in full. - We cut our hair and wash our clothes, coming confident before Hashem. The Rema adds the mikveh and visiting the cemetery, where we ask our loved ones to pray for us. We never ask the dead for anything ourselves, which is forbidden.

Siman 582: HaMelekh HaKadosh, and knowing your posek

In the Ten Days we say HaMelekh HaKadosh and HaMelekh HaMishpat, stressing Hashem's kingship. Saying HaEl HaKadosh sends you back to the start of the Amidah, since the first three blessings are one unit. Saying the weekday ending of Hashivah Shofetenu sends you back to that blessing, or to the start if you have finished. Almost every Rishon applies this to both. One opinion, a student of Rabbenu Yonah, says HaMelekh HaMishpat needs no repetition, since the word Melekh was said. The Shulhan Arukh rejects it, and Rav Ovadia Yosef, normally strict about doubtful blessings, rules that one opinion against so many is no real doubt: you go back. The Ben Ish Hai rules that, because of that opinion, you do not. A tip: before each Amidah, intend to say the special words. If you later doubt, you may assume your intention held. A correction within two or three seconds, before starting the next blessing, is fine. Then "something astonishing". On Friday night of the Ten Days the Magen Avot blessing also says HaMelekh HaKadosh. That blessing was added so latecomers would not walk home alone, so Rav Ovadia rules that if it was forgotten we do not go back. Here the Ben Ish Hai rules that we do, because according to Kabbalah the blessing is an obligation. Likewise with the Shabbat addition to Birkat HaMazon at Seudah Shelishit: the Shulhan Arukh says not to repeat it, the Ben Ish Hai says to, since for the Arizal the third meal is as important as the others. The lesson: understand a posek's framework before learning his rulings. Briefly: on Shabbat we say "Zikhron Teruah"; we omit the phrases about festivals of joy, since Rosh Hashanah is not a pilgrim festival; and some Moroccan communities still say the whole Amidah aloud so that people can follow. He smiled at the many rules for the New Year greeting in the Mishnah Berurah: male or female, singular or plural, before or after midday.

Siman 583: the simanim, and apple and honey

Ask what the symbol of Rosh Hashanah is and most people say apple and honey. The Shulhan Arukh, from the Gemara, lists other foods for the first night: black-eyed beans ("may our merits increase"), leek ("may our enemies be cut off"), spinach ("may they be removed"), dates ("may they come to an end") and gourd ("may the decree be torn up and our merits read out"). The Gemara calls this "simana milta", a sign. It is forbidden to treat it as superstition, as if the food decides the year; it is psychology, beginning the year on the right foot. He cited an explanation of three enemies: the physical enemy, the prosecutor, and the enemy within made by our sins. Both communities do the simanim on both nights. The Rema adds an apple dipped in honey, and pomegranates. These vegetables did not grow in Germany or Poland, so they used what they had; he dated the first mention to the Maharam of Rothenburg, about 750 years ago. More customs: a sheep's head, to be a head and not a tail; Ashkenazim avoid nuts, since egoz has the gematria of het, sin; the Hida records communities that avoided fish, since dag sounds like deagah, worry. Tashlikh is a figurative casting away of sins; the Spanish and Portuguese do not say it, and he urged not to make it bigger than it needs to be. Not sleeping on Rosh Hashanah has a source in the Yerushalmi, but being awake means nothing if the time is wasted, and it is better to pray at sunrise and rest after midday than to sleep in.

Practical summary

  • Siman 581 is custom: Selihot from Rosh Hodesh Elul for Sefaradim, from the Sunday before (at least four days) for Ashkenazim.
  • The best time for Selihot is midnight to dawn. Late evening is discouraged, but better than none.
  • Forgot HaMelekh HaKadosh: go back to the start of the Amidah. HaMelekh HaMishpat: Rav Ovadia says go back; the Ben Ish Hai says not to.
  • Before each Amidah of the Ten Days, have in mind to say the special words.
  • Eat the simanim as a sign that sets our mind on a good year, never as a charm.
