These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Are women obligated in Havdalah?

Last week covered the order of Havdalah, the cup, using beer, and sitting for Havdalah. The Rabbi left the last seif of 296 for tonight.

Siman 296, seif 8: women are obligated in Havdalah just like men, but one opinion disagrees. The Rema: therefore women should not make Havdalah for themselves but hear it from men.

Why are women obligated in a positive time-bound misvah? The rule of Shabbat is "Zakhor veShamor bedibbur ehad." Since women are bound by all the prohibitions of Shabbat (Shamor), they are bound by all its positive misvot (Zakhor): Kiddush, the meals, honouring Shabbat. Havdalah is part of that package.

The dissenting view, the Orhot Hayim, argues that Havdalah comes after Shabbat and is not part of Shabbat. A class member asked whether it is really time-bound if it can be said for hours, or even until Tuesday. The Rabbi explained that any misvah with a time frame, however long, counts as time-bound, like tallit and tefillin all day or massah all night.

How to read the Shulhan Arukh. When the Shulhan Arukh states one view plainly and then brings "some say", the plain view is the ruling. So for Sefaradim women are obligated and may make Havdalah. Still, because of the dissenting view, it is better for them to hear it from a husband or adult children.

Women, accepting a misvah, and blessings

How does an Ashkenazi woman living alone make Havdalah, if the Rema says she should not? The Mishnah Berurah applies an idea Ashkenazim use widely, from Rabbenu Tam: a woman who takes on a misvah she is exempt from makes it an obligation for herself, and may then say the blessing. This is why many Ashkenazi women bless on lulav, sukkah and shofar.

On this the Rabbi explained when "bli neder" belongs. It is for a good deed you take on, like visiting someone who is ill, where accepting it would make it a vow. It is pointless for ordinary plans like a drive to Manchester.

The Shulhan Arukh rejects Rabbenu Tam's idea and rules that a woman may not bless on a misvah she is not obligated in. The Rema accepts it, and the Spanish and Portuguese, Indian, Iraqi and Afghan communities had the custom of women blessing on these misvot, though not Syria. Rav Ovadia Yosef ruled strictly that it is a blessing in vain and the custom should be stopped. Other poskim say a family with the custom may continue; without a known custom, as with a convert or a baal teshuvah, do not bless.

Hearing Havdalah in shul when you will make it at home

The Rabbi warned that it is harder for a man who has already fulfilled Havdalah to make it again for women at home than for a woman to make it herself. So a man going home only to women should hear Havdalah in shul with the intention not to fulfil the misvah.

Asked whether he may smell the spices and hold his hands to the light in shul, the Rabbi worked through the three kinds of blessings: enjoyment, misvah, and praise.

  • Wine and spices are blessings of enjoyment. At home is a new setting, so he may drink and smell in shul and bless again at home.
  • "HaMavdil" is a blessing on a misvah, which cannot be repeated, so he should intend not to fulfil it in shul.
  • The fire is also best treated as a misvah blessing. If it were enjoyment, we would bless every time we saw a fire. So he should not fulfil it in shul either.

The Biur Halacha adds a strong argument. Havdalah is part of Shabbat, but the fire, remembering Adam lighting fire on the first Saturday night, has nothing to do with Shabbat. It is a positive time-bound misvah, so women are exempt. A woman making Havdalah alone should therefore say the whole Havdalah but leave out Borei Meorei HaEsh, which is not compulsory anyway. The Rabbi said there are strong grounds for this. He added that it is not usually publicised because it is complicated, and each person must judge whether to explain it at home.

He stressed that the fire is not lit to show that we may now light fires: "that's not the reason we do a fire on Saturday night." It is to remember Adam. And a woman may certainly drink from the Havdalah cup; the Mishnah Berurah laments women alone who do not make Havdalah.

Spices: which blessing, and which spices

Siman 297, seif 1: say the blessing on spices if you have them; there is no need to search. They are for comfort, since we are sad at losing the extra soul of Shabbat. Spices in the house or garden count as having them; a trip to the shop is not needed.

The Mishnah Berurah records that Ashkenazim always say "Borei Minei Besamim" on Saturday night, whatever they have, because people confuse the blessings. Sefaradim reject this and say the correct blessing:

  • Asei Besamim for spices from a tree, such as cinnamon. Rav Ovadia Yosef rules this also for cloves.
  • Isvei Besamim for herbs, such as mint or lavender.
  • Minei Besamim for mixtures or when unsure.

Asked about using cinnamon and mint together: have only one kind, since two blessings would interrupt Havdalah.

Seifim 2-3: no blessing on fragrance meant to remove a bad smell, such as herbs in a toilet or around the deceased, or on spices used at idolatrous gatherings. If one blessed on these, take proper spices and bless again. Spices put into non-Jewish wine to flavour it may be smelled, but are not used for Havdalah, since that would not be fitting.

Seif 4: the Tur mentions blessing on the hadas from Sukkot. The Rema says not to, since a dry hadas has no smell. "Obviously we don't do this."

When you cannot smell

Seif 5: someone who cannot smell, for example with a bad cold, should not bless on spices. The Shulhan Arukh allows him to bless for children or someone who does not know how. The Rabbi asked how one can bless on someone else's enjoyment without enjoying it oneself. Many poskim disagree, and the halacha does not follow it.

  • With a cold, someone else says the spice blessing. The one making Havdalah pauses and stays silent, not answering amen, so as not to interrupt.
  • The Mishnah Berurah criticises a custom where each person says his own spice blessing while Havdalah continues. The Rabbi said it "should be abolished."
  • If the spices reach you late and you have not spoken, smell them without a blessing. If you spoke in between, better not to smell.

The fire: Borei Meorei HaEsh

Siman 298, seif 1: the Rabbi noted the siman numbers are written in an unusual order, to avoid the letters of a word meaning "murder." Bless on fire if you have it; do not search. "Meorei" is plural: the many colours of the flame. If the candle will only be available hours later, make Havdalah now and bless on fire later.

After Yom Kippur some say one should search for fire. There the fire is not about Adam; it shows the contrast with Yom Kippur, when fire was forbidden. So it must be a flame that burned throughout Yom Kippur. When Yom Kippur falls on Shabbat, one may be more lenient and bless on an ordinary flame if there is no such flame.

The Rema: if there is no wine or beer, still say the blessings on spices and fire separately.

Seif 2: "misvah min hamuvhar" is an avukah, several wicks together, hence the plaited Havdalah candle. But one wick is completely valid. "People, you're crazy trying to find a candle with two things." If you have no Havdalah candle, light a match.

Practical summary

  • Women are obligated in Havdalah. A Sefaradi woman alone makes it herself; with a man present, better to hear it from him.
  • A man making Havdalah at home only for women should intend not to fulfil HaMavdil and the fire in shul.
  • Sefaradim say the correct blessing on the spice in hand; use only one kind.
  • If you cannot smell, let someone else say the spice blessing.
  • A single-wick candle or a match is enough for the fire.
