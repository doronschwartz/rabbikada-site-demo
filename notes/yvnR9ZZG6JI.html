Topic: Olam HaBa: what it is, who reaches it, Gehinam, Gan Eden and the resurrection Sources: Berakhot 17a; Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1; the Ramhal, Derech Hashem 2:2 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 11 March 2024 (Rosh Hodesh Adar II), the third class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Recap: why we must earn the good

Dayan Kada said the topic is truly fundamental: Jews believe in a world to come, but few know what it is. Olam HaBa literally means "the world that is coming".

He summarised the previous class in ninety seconds. Hashem is good and wants to give perfect goodness, which is found only in Him. He cannot create another God, so He allows us to cleave to Him, devekut, like being allowed to spend unlimited time with a monarch you admire. Why not simply give it to us? Because goodness that is earned is not like goodness given as a gift, and to earn it we must be able to fail. That is why this world exists, and why free will is an axiom of our belief.

Asked what happens after death, someone suggested the World to Come could be reached tonight, through learning. He explained that this confuses it with the days of Mashiah, a separate subject. Olam HaZeh is this world; Olam HaBa is the world that follows death.

The righteous sit with crowns on their heads

The first source was the Gemara in Berakhot 17a. Rav used to say: in the World to Come there is no eating or drinking, no having children, no business, no jealousy, hatred or competition. Rather, "the righteous sit with their crowns upon their heads and enjoy the radiance of the Shekhinah."

Enjoying Hashem's presence is compared to eating and drinking: the pleasure of a good meal, magnified many times. Given the choice between a lovely dinner and ten minutes with the Queen, he said, he at least would choose the Queen.

Why does the Gemara first say what it is not? Because for physical beings it is easier to negate what we know than to describe something purely spiritual.

The crown is the intellect. The crown on their heads is their understanding of what Hashem is. Those who spent a year learning about royalty before the Queen's banquet would be closer to her. This life is the opportunity to develop that understanding. Torah study is part of it, but some people know all the laws and never stop to think about Hashem. When do we make time to think about Him? Someone answered: when we pray. Exactly, he said. The purpose of prayer is meditation, introspection about our relationship with Hashem.

A world of consequences

Someone asked whether the World to Come is static. The simple answer, he said, is that there is no progression there. Think of a stadium: whatever seat you have earned, front row or the last stand at the back, is your seat for the whole performance.

When someone objected that it seems strange to have a mind and be unable to learn, he said that this world is the world of toil and choice, and the World to Come is the world of consequences. Teshuvah belongs here. Once a person has died and seen the light, it is too late to change. "Free will, free choice is only during your 120 years in this life."

He repeated that he does not like the words reward and punishment. Becoming fit at the gym is not a reward but a consequence. So too a person who has led a debased life and has no understanding of Hashem is not being punished by having no seat at the royal banquet; it would simply make no sense for him to be there.

"All Israel have a share", with exceptions

People often quote "All Israel have a share in the World to Come." What they do not know is that the Mishnah in Sanhedrin continues "and these are the ones who have no share". Most people are sinners in the ordinary sense, with some good and some bad, and their place matches what they have done. But some things are so severe that a person does not enter the stadium at all.

Someone asked whether this applies only to Jews. Here the Mishnah does mean Jews, he said, and that difference would be the subject of a later class. Briefly: Jews have the framework of 613 misvot, which makes it easier to reach that level. A non-Jew who keeps the seven Noahide laws also has a share in the World to Come.

Gehinam and Kaddish

The Ramhal (Derech Hashem 2:2) explains that most people are a mix of good and bad, and justice requires every action to be repaid, so the repayment is divided. The main repayment, cleaving to Hashem or being cut off, follows the majority of a person's deeds, in the World to Come. The bad deeds of the righteous are repaid here.

Suffering in this world, for people who are essentially good, is a process of refinement, like getting rid of bad habits before sitting at the royal table. Some need more refinement than this world provides, and that is Gehinam. Gehinam is a deeper, more intense refinement, a transition before the World to Come, lasting at most twelve months.

That is why Kaddish is said for at most twelve months, and by custom for eleven months and three weeks, so as not to imply that the parent needed the full term. Using the same image of an apple as before: a few bruises can be cut away, but an apple bruised all over is thrown out. "If you have arrived in Gehinam, there's something to be happy about," because it means you have a future. The worst consequence is to cease to exist, which, he suggested, is why people fear death and work so hard to leave a name behind.

Asked about his sources, he said the twelve months is stated in the Talmud, and everything he teaches on this comes from the Torah and the Talmud.

What does Kaddish do? Kaddish never mentions death. It sanctifies Hashem's name. When a child cares about Hashem's presence in the world, it reflects on the parent who raised him, and that elevates the parent's soul. Paying someone to say Kaddish is good, but nothing like a child saying it. Kaddish is in Aramaic because that was the vernacular, and people were meant to understand it. For mourners who cannot read the words, he said, he and his colleagues had published a responsum suggesting that the rabbi say the Kaddish once and the mourner say it in English, which is far more meaningful. Any misvah done in the parent's memory also elevates the soul.

Why the Torah does not spell it out

Someone asked whether the World to Come appears in the Humash. Not explicitly, though the Talmud finds many hints. He gave two reasons. First, an answer he attributed to Rabbi Sacks: too much focus on the next world can empty this world of meaning, and Judaism insists that this world is rich and important. Second, the Torah speaks to everyone, and it is easier to motivate people with rain and livelihood, as in the Shema.

The last source said that the perfection of a person lies in developing the intellect and refining the character, while material desire and bad character traits pull the other way, and a person is placed between them to choose. Every misvah, he said, aims at one of these two. Kashrut teaches restraint, and a berakhah makes us think before we eat. Shabbat makes us stop for a day and think about creation. Without good character, how can a person sit at the royal banquet?

Why are we created imperfect? So that we can choose perfection and make it truly ours, like an honour earned rather than handed out.

Gan Eden and the resurrection

At the end, someone asked about the resurrection. Since the body was part of the journey, he explained, it must share in the consequences. Tehiyat HaMetim means a person recreated with a body that is subordinate to the soul, without today's cravings. Today body and soul are in constant conflict, as when the body wants to stay in bed on Shabbat morning and the soul wants to go to shul. Then they will live in harmony.

The World to Come begins, he said, after six thousand years, in a renewed world, and we are now in the year 5784. Someone who dies now and has a share rests in Gan Eden, the world of souls, where he experiences a taste of what is to come while waiting. "The world to come is the world to come in terms of time, not in terms of location."

Summary

  • Olam HaBa is the soul's enjoyment of Hashem's presence; the "crowns" are the understanding of Hashem a person has developed.
  • This world is for choice and teshuvah; the World to Come is the world of consequences, with no further change.
  • Some sins are so severe that a person has no share at all.
  • Gehinam is a refinement of at most twelve months, and Kaddish and misvot by children elevate the soul.
  • Our task is to develop our intellect and character through the misvot, and the resurrection will reunite body and soul in harmony.
