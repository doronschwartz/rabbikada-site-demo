These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

"Do not make them a crown"

Last week covered humility and lowness of spirit, Olam HaBa as identity, and the Rambam's reading of wisdom, honour and wealth. This week the Rabbi returned to the piece the Rambam almost did not write.

The Mishnah. Rabbi Sadok says: do not make the words of Torah a crown to glorify yourself, nor a spade to dig with. Hillel used to say: whoever makes use of the crown passes away. From here: whoever benefits from words of Torah removes his life from the world.

The Rambam begins: I decided not to comment, because the Mishnah is clear and because most of the great rabbis, perhaps all of them, will be unhappy with what I say. But I decided to explain it anyway, without regard for those before me or those of today.

The Rambam then says people threw the plain meaning behind them. They imposed levies on communities and taught the public that supporting the hakhamim was an obligation. There is nothing to rely on for this. We never find the sages of the Talmud asking for gifts or collecting for "their great and glorious yeshivot", which the Rabbi read as sarcasm. The Rambam also had a famous distaste for the Exilarch. The Rabbi compared that office to a chief rabbi funded by taxes on the community. "So rabbinic politics is nothing new."

The Rambam's proofs

  • Hillel was a woodcutter while he studied. With students so great, he could have given it up at any time had he wanted support.
  • Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa, in whose merit the world was sustained, lived from week to week on a measure of carobs and asked no one.
  • Karna, a judge in Eres Yisrael, drew water for a living. He told litigants: find someone to draw water in my place, or pay me what I lose, and I will judge for you.
  • Rav Yosef carried wood and said "great is work, for it warms its master", which the Rambam reads as work sustaining a person (Rashi: its sweat keeps you healthy).
  • Rabbi Tarfon, caught eating fallen grapes and taken for a thief, was bundled into a sack by the owner to be thrown in the river. He cried out his name and was released. He regretted it all his life: he was wealthy and could have paid, but he had saved himself through the honour of his Torah.
  • Yonatan ben Amram asked Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi for food in a famine without saying he was a scholar, so as not to benefit from his Torah.

The Rambam answers the proofs brought against him. Those who took support because they were old or physically unable to work are a different case. Of course they may take charity. The Gemara's choice between living like Elisha, who accepted hospitality, and like Shmuel, who took nothing, is about accepting a kindness freely offered, not asking for money, still less imposing taxes.

What, then, may we do for the hakhamim? Invest their money for them, buy from them first, and offer them goods at a fair price, as one prefers to do business with a friend. And Hazal exempt them from certain communal taxes.

The key line, he said, is why the Rambam calls it a hillul HaShem: it turns Torah into one more profession, and then the payer feels he can control the rabbi. "For clarity and disclosure, I do take a rabbinic salary," he added, "and we will explain that."

Objections and replies

"The Rambam himself permits it." People cite the end of Hilchot Shemitah VeYovel: not only the tribe of Levi but anyone whose spirit moves him to stand before HaShem, to serve Him and know Him, becomes holy of holies and HaShem is his portion. The Rabbi pointed out that the key words are that Levi was set apart "to serve HaShem and to teach His ways to the many". The Levi had a job: he served the people, and they gave him tithes. Long-term kolel study with no intention of teaching, becoming a rav or dayan, or serving the community, he said, is "a non-starter". He had no doubt the Rambam would not allow it.

The Tashbes, Rabbi Shimon ben Semah Duran, rejects the Rambam's proofs one by one: Hillel worked only before he became a leader, Rabbi Hanina chose asceticism, Rav Yosef's "warms" means health. Rav Yosef Kafih answers these objections in turn.

The Kesef Mishneh (the Beit Yosef) adds two points. The Rambam's anger is aimed at someone who chooses no trade at all and lives on charity. And even if the Rambam is right, the hakhamim have relied on "it is a time to act for HaShem": as the Oral Torah was written down to save it from being forgotten, rabbis may take salaries so that Torah is not forgotten.

The Shulhan Arukh and the Rema. The ruling first quotes the Rambam's prohibition, with an exception for the sick. Then come the leniencies: a rav who needs it may take a salary so that he need not work, though not one who is well off; and some allow even students to receive stipends. It concludes that earning one's own living is better. Everyone agrees that someone wealthy enough must not take a rabbinic salary, including, the Rabbi said, someone in kolel who owns property and lives comfortably.

The rabbinate today

In fairness, the Rabbi said, the rabbinate is no longer what it was. In Europe the rav spoke publicly only two or three times a year, on Shabbat Shuvah and Shabbat HaGadol, and otherwise sat and learned and answered questions. Today rabbis run programmes, take funerals, counsel and advise. The job has become like other jobs, perhaps because salaries came first. Still, it is not the ideal. If a person is given a gift, fine, but certainly not if he can manage without. Karna is the precedent for being paid for time you could otherwise earn in.

He told of the late rabbi of Ohel David, Rabbi Gabbay, who was independently wealthy and gave his whole salary to charity. That way he could say what he wanted. Today, he said, many rabbis cannot speak freely, "and that's what happens when you commercialise Torah". A kolel that trains rabbis, dayanim and teachers deserves support. Long-term study with no intention of going out to teach, he said, is not something we support. When a rosh kolel argued that men of 45 cannot now find work, the Rabbi answered from the Gemara: "Flay a carcass in the marketplace" rather than say a job is beneath you. We need hakhamim who study and should support them, "done in the correct way".

Questions. Someone who knew Rabbi Gabbay confirmed the story. How, someone asked, can we tell which kolel to support? Ask what it does and who studies there.

No bribes, and one hour of teshuvah

Mishnah 22. HaShem does not take a bribe. Obviously not money, the Rambam says. It means you cannot offset a thousand sins against a million misvot. Every misvah is rewarded and every sin has its consequence; that is why Moshe was punished for his one sin. The Rabbi applied this to the scales of Rosh Hashanah, then less than three months away. They do not count misvot against sins. They weigh the importance we give to the misvot, which shows who we are. Hence the Gemara that one who regrets his misvot loses them: the deed cannot be undone, but its place in your identity can.

Mishnah 17. One hour of teshuvah and good deeds in this world is better than all the life of the world to come, yet one hour of the world to come is better than all of this world. The Rambam explains: Olam HaBa is incomparably greater, but only here can you change who you are. The Ramhal explains that there is progress there, "from strength to strength", but its pace is fixed by the identity you arrive with. So use this world for wisdom and virtue.

Summary

  • The Rambam reads "do not make them a spade" as forbidding scholars to live off their Torah, and brings proofs from Hillel, Karna, Rabbi Tarfon and others.
  • The Tashbes and the Kesef Mishneh defend the practice of rabbinic salaries; the Shulhan Arukh permits it in steps but prefers self-support.
  • The Rabbi rejected long-term kolel study with no aim of serving the community, while supporting institutions that train rabbis and teachers.
  • No bribes: misvot and sins do not cancel out; the scales of Rosh Hashanah weigh importance.
  • Only in this world can a person change his identity, so use the time well.
