Topic: Leaving during the reading, the opinions on speaking and studying while the Torah is read, the Haftarah, and when one must stand for the Sefer Torah Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 146 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 12 January 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Back from the winter break

The class resumed the laws of Keriat HaTorah after the winter break and the classes on Hanukkah. The previous classes had covered the Haftarah and the meturgeman. Siman 146 has four paragraphs: the first is about leaving, the rest about speaking.

Leaving between aliyot

It is forbidden to leave while the Sefer Torah is open, even for someone who has already heard the reading and only came in to pick up a book, and even if there is a minyan without him. Between aliyot, bein gavra legavra, "between man and man," it is allowed when there is a need and ten remain.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that this must be occasional. Someone who walks out in every break for tea and a chat "looks like he is throwing off the yoke." Once in a while, for a need, is fine.

The Beur Halacha says one may leave as soon as the reader has finished, even before the closing berakhah. But the start is stricter: once the Sefer Torah has been lifted and shown to the congregation for reading, it counts as begun, so one should not leave then.

Studying or speaking during the reading: four opinions

The Rabbi first asked the class what they thought. Mundane talk, clearly not. Studying by yourself? Asking the rabbi a question? Then he read 146:2, which brings four opinions about the time once the reader has begun:

  1. Strictest: it is forbidden to speak, even words of Torah, even between aliyot, even if you have already heard the reading. By this view even Mi Sheberakh blessings would be out.
  2. Some allow studying quietly by yourself, even during the reading.
  3. Some allow speaking words of Torah with someone else if there are ten others paying attention to the reading.
  4. Some allow this only for someone whose Torah is his profession. Someone who does not study all week should not choose this moment.

Two things everyone agrees on:

  • You may read along quietly with the Baal Koreh. That is part of listening.
  • No opinion allows mundane chatter while the Torah is being read. "That is 100% asur."

Rav Ovadia Yoseph rules like the second opinion: one may study quietly, even reciting words, so one could even say Tehillim. "But the correct thing, if possible, is that you pay attention and listen." The Shulhan Arukh itself says one should try to focus on the reading.

Between aliyot, only the first opinion forbids speaking. Since the halacha follows the majority, this is why there are Mi Sheberakh blessings and some talking in the breaks. Even then, keep it to relevant things, "what time is the Kiddush," not "how was your week."

Someone asked how one could study in a room where a minyan is reading. If you clearly show at the start that you are not part of the reading, for example by moving to a corner before the Sefer Torah is taken out, you may continue studying with your partner quietly, though still not mundane talk.

The exceptions

Everyone agrees one may read Shenayim Mikra VeEhad Targum during the reading. And everyone agrees none of the leniencies apply to Parashat Zakhor, and, according to the Shulhan Arukh, Parashat Parah, which he holds is a Torah obligation: there everyone must listen to every word.

The Hafes Hayim's rebuke

The Rabbi read the Beur Halacha aloud as "a bit of musar," reminding the class that the author of the Mishnah Berurah also wrote the Hafes Hayim. The whole paragraph, he writes, is an open rebuke to those who talk idle chatter during the reading. If some forbid even Torah study, how much more so idle talk, turning one's ear from the word of Hashem. His prayer too becomes an abomination, "meisir ozno mishmoa Torah gam tefillato toevah." He warns that when a prominent person sitting at the front does this, it can be a hillul Hashem.

If someone speaks to you during the reading, the Rabbi said, answer politely: "I'm sorry, I'll answer afterwards." This is not like Shema, where there are rules for responding. "And it doesn't mean we should go around shushing everyone." But for ourselves and our families, we should know that it is forbidden.

Someone asked what to do in shuls where people talk despite signs. The Rabbi said that when he reads and someone keeps talking, he simply stops reading. Everyone turns to see why, and it goes quiet.

The Haftarah (146:3)

The prohibition on speaking during the Torah reading comes from the Gemara. One of the Rishonim extended it to the Haftarah, and the Shulhan Arukh rules that way. It is less severe, but one should not talk during the Haftarah either. There are two aspects: the misvah to hear, and the respect owed to what is being read to you. If a passage is repeated many times, take a book and read, but do not talk.

Someone mentioned the custom in many Ashkenazi shuls for everyone to read the Haftarah to himself. The Rabbi said this comes from the Rema, as a custom rather than an obligation, and suggested it may have arisen because people were not paying attention.

Standing during the reading (146:4)

One does not need to stand while the Torah is read. The Mishnah Berurah records that the Maharam of Rothenburg stood, as a personal practice, "to feel as if he was receiving it at Har Sinai," while agreeing that the halacha allows sitting.

The Rabbi drew a practical lesson. If your custom is to stand but you are in a shul where everyone sits, do not stand. "Don't do hiddur at the expense of standing out." Even the Maharam held that sitting is permitted.

On standing for the Ten Commandments, he said the Rambam opposes it, so as not to give one part of the Torah more honour than another. Where the custom is to stand, the common solution is to call up the rabbi for that aliyah, so the congregation stands for him and stays standing.

The Baal Koreh does stand, as anyone reading for the community does.

When must you stand for the Sefer Torah?

Then the Rabbi tested the class. Do you stand when the Ark is open? When the scroll is open on the Tevah? When it is held up for a prayer?

The Mishnah Berurah and Shaar HaSiyun answer clearly: one stands only when the Sefer Torah is being carried. When it is in its place, on the Tevah or held by someone standing still, even for the Hashkavot or the prayer for the Royal Family, there is no obligation to stand. The same applies when the Ark is open.

When it is moving, you stand until it reaches its place or until you can no longer see it. In a large shul, once it is out of sight you may sit. Even when it is moved a short distance on the Tevah from one place to another, everyone stands. He suggested that the singing in the Spanish and Portuguese synagogue, when the scroll is lifted to be moved, serves to remind people to stand.

He told of a rabbi's induction where the Ark had been left open, and a distinguished guest refused to speak until it was closed. Out of respect they closed it, though halachically it was not necessary. He also mentioned that in the Spanish and Portuguese community the Ark stays open for much of Yom Kippur. People are not required to stand all that time.

Practical summary

  • Do not leave while the Sefer Torah is open; between aliyot leave only occasionally, for a need, when ten remain.
  • Mundane talk during the reading is forbidden by every opinion. Reading along quietly is always fine, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph allows quiet personal study, but the ideal is to listen.
  • Between aliyot, talking about relevant matters is permitted by the majority, which is the basis for Mi Sheberakh blessings.
  • Parashat Zakhor, and Parashat Parah according to the Shulhan Arukh, require full attention from everyone.
  • Do not talk during the Haftarah.
  • Stand for the Sefer Torah when it is being carried, not when it is resting on the Tevah or the Ark is open; follow the congregation rather than standing out.
