Who is a mamzer?

The Rabbi opened by calling this one of the most painful issues a Beit Din deals with. "These things still happen today, and they are very tragic cases when they come." He set out three questions: what a mamzer is, what restrictions apply, and whether anything can be done.

Many English translations render mamzer as "bastard", a child born out of wedlock. That is not the halachic definition. If a Jewish man and a Jewish woman who are not married have a child, what they did is wrong, but the child is a regular Jew who may marry anyone.

A mamzer is a child born from a relationship that carries karet or the death penalty: a Jewish man, married or not, with a married Jewish woman who is not his wife, or an incestuous relationship. Several cases do not produce a mamzer:

On the niddah exception he remarked that without it, marriage today would be very complicated. Asked how paternity was proven before modern science, he explained the principle of rov beilot ahar habaal: we assume a married woman's child is her husband's, unless, for example, he was away for a year.

What restrictions apply?

Very few. A mamzer is a full Jew. He is called to the Torah, can serve as hazan according to most poskim, and according to most can be a sofer and a kosher witness. The Gemara even says a mamzer who is a talmid hakham takes precedence over an ignorant Kohen Gadol.

The one great restriction is in Parashat Ki Tese: "A mamzer shall not enter the congregation of Hashem, even the tenth generation." A mamzer may not marry a regular Jewish woman, and a mamzeret may not marry a regular Jewish man. It is a negative prohibition, like a Kohen marrying a divorcee, so if it happens the marriage is valid and needs a get. The tragedy is that the children of such a marriage are always mamzerim, whichever parent is the mamzer, generation after generation. A mamzer may marry a mamzeret, or a convert.

He did not go into the theology. His best explanation at present: "Yes, it's not fair. But fairness is one value," and the Torah also protects the family unit with very severe sanctions on those who break it.

How mamzerim are created today

Incest is rare, and adulterous couples usually take care not to have a child. Mamzerim who were never identified are, the Gemara says, mixed in and not our concern. The common case, the Rabbi explained, is this:

A couple who are not religious marry in an orthodox synagogue because their parents want it. Ten years later they divorce civilly without a get. She remarries a Jewish man outside the synagogue and has children. She is still married to her first husband, so those children are mamzerim. Ironically, had her second partner not been Jewish, the children would be regular Jews.

Even more tragic: a couple married in an orthodox setting later join the Reform community and divorce through a Reform Beit Din. A Reform get is not a get. She remarries a Jewish man, and years later a child who has become religious comes to the Beit Din to marry and discovers he is a mamzer. To the Reform movement's credit, the Rabbi said, in the UK he understood they send couples married in an orthodox setting to an orthodox Beit Din for their get.

Asked about the reverse case, a Reform marriage with no get, he said the child would not be a mamzer: the Reform marriage is nothing, and although whether a civil marriage counts as kiddushin is debated, once a child is born one may rely on the lenient view.

Solution one: forbidden, but it would work

A male mamzer could have children with a non-Jewish woman. The children would be non-Jewish, with no taint of mamzerut, and if the mother and children later converted, they would be regular converts. But it works only for a man, since a mamzeret's children with a non-Jew are Jewish and remain mamzerim. It needs the woman's full agreement, since no Beit Din converts children without the mother. Above all, it is forbidden: a mamzer is a full Jew, and relations with a non-Jew are forbidden to him. No rabbi can advise it. If it happened, a Beit Din would look at the circumstances.

Solution two: the shifhah kenaanit

In times of slavery, a non-Jewish slave, an eved kenaani or shifhah kenaanit, took on the misvot a Jewish woman keeps and had a special half-Jewish status. The Gemara rules that a mamzer may marry a shifhah kenaanit, and their children follow the mother, so they are not mamzerim. When the master frees them, they become full Jews. The Gemara tells of a master who freed his slave to complete a minyan.

So a male mamzer could, in principle, make the woman he loves his halachic shifhah, have children, and then free them all. Again it only works for men. The bigger question is whether it works today, when slavery is illegal.

After the Holocaust, this was hotly debated. Rav Weiss, then Av Beit Din of Manchester, wrote to Rav Mordechai Breisch, the author of Helkat Yaakov, in Zurich, about ten cases of women who survived, believed their husbands dead, remarried and had children, only for the first husbands to return. The women were not to blame, but the children were mamzerim. Rav Breisch suggested the slave route, arguing that "the law of the land" applies only to monetary matters. Rav Weiss rejected it: is it real slavery when both see each other as husband and wife and she could walk away at any moment? And it would undermine conversion, since anyone wanting to bring a non-Jewish partner into Judaism could bypass the Beit Din this way.

The Rabbi said no Beit Din officially takes this position today, though he would not be surprised if it happens quietly in difficult cases, because "when you're faced with such a person, you want to turn the world around to be able to help them."

Solution three: annulling the parents' marriage

The only solution that works for both men and women is to show that the original marriage never took effect, almost always by finding that one of its witnesses was not kosher at the time. He described a case in Israel around 2018 in which a Beit Din, by majority, declared a marriage void because one witness had not been religious at the wedding, so a woman whose husband refused to give a get no longer needed one.

Someone asked why we do not simply invalidate every marriage this way. He answered that a marriage with no valid witnesses is no marriage at all; the principle cannot be stretched to every wedding.

Practical summary

  • A mamzer is a child of a married Jewish woman and a Jewish man who is not her husband, or of incest; a child born out of wedlock is not.
  • A mamzer is a full Jew in every respect except that he or she may not marry a regular Jew, and the status passes to the children.
  • Today mamzerim most often result from remarriage after an orthodox wedding without a valid get, including after a Reform get.
  • Having children with a non-Jew and converting them would work for a man but is forbidden; the slave route is debated and not officially followed.
  • The third possibility, which works for both men and women where the facts allow, is to show that the parents' original marriage was invalid because a witness was not kosher.
