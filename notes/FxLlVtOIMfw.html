Series Introduction

  • New series for 2025 on Hilchot Pesach, following previous learning of Hilchot Shabbat, Sukkot, and Chanukah.
  • Hilchot Pesach comprises 61 simanim in the Shulchan Aruch — significantly more complex and extensive than Chanukah (~15 simanim).
  • This year (5785/2025): Erev Pesach falls on Shabbat (Pesach begins Motzei Shabbat), adding unique complexities addressed in dedicated simanim later.
  • Focus of this series: The preparatory halachot from the beginning — Bedikat Chametz, Biur Chametz, Hagalat Kelim, baking of Matzot — rather than the Seder itself.
  • Primary source: Shulchan Aruch with Sephardi psak, supplemented by Rama, Mishnah Berurah, and Acharonim.

Siman 429 – Laws of the Month of Nisan (שאין נופלין על פניהם בכל חודש ניסן)

Contains 2 Se'ifim

Se'if 1 – Learning Hilchot Pesach 30 Days Before

Shulchan Aruch: שואלין בהלכות הפסח קודם לפסח שלושים יום — We ask and discuss the halachot of Pesach beginning 30 days before the Chag.

Source — Two Gemarot and an Apparent Contradiction

  1. Gemara (Pesachim 6a): שואלין ודורשין בענין החג שלושים יום קודם — "We inquire and expound regarding the Chag 30 days before." Most Rishonim understand this as an obligation to teach, study, and discuss the halachot of Pesach from 30 days prior.
  2. Gemara (Megillah): Moshe Rabbeinu instituted that Bnei Yisrael should study the laws of each Chag on the Chag itself — Hilchot Pesach on Pesach, Hilchot Sukkot on Sukkot.

The Contradiction — Rabbenu Nisim's Resolution

  • Question: Is the obligation 30 days before, or on the Chag itself?
  • Rabbenu Nisim's Chiddush: The "30 days before" is a din in Talmud Torah — it means that when a rabbi has many questions to answer, from 30 days before Pesach he must prioritise Pesach-related questions over others. However, the actual obligation to teach and study the halachot is only on the Chag itself, per Moshe's institution.

Majority View — Obligation from 30 Days Before

  • Most Rishonim reject Rabbenu Nisim and hold there is a genuine obligation to learn Hilchot Pesach from 30 days before.
  • They resolve the contradiction in two ways:
  1. Moshe's institution was the original; the 30-day rule was a later development expanding it.
  2. Beit Yosef: 30 days before = learning about preparations for the Chag. Moshe's institution = learning the halachot of the Chag day itself (Yom Tov). Two different aspects.

Practical Halacha

  • The Shulchan Aruch rules in accordance with the majority: we begin studying Hilchot Pesach 30 days before.
  • Every individual has an obligation to review and learn Hilchot Pesach annually — set aside time each day from 30 days before.
  • The 30 days before Pesach begins on Purim — this is why there is a minhag to share a Pesach-related halacha or Torah idea already on Purim itself.
  • Rambam lists these as two distinct dinim: (a) learning on the Chag itself, and (b) preparing 30 days before.

Mishnah Berurah — Focus on Practical Halachot

  • The Mishnah Berurah criticises the practice found in some communities (especially very Charedi ones) where the rabbi gives a highly complex, theoretical pilpul on Shabbat HaGadol that is not practically relevant.
  • He insists the focus must be on relevant, practical halachot of the Chag. This has always been the Derech of the Sephardim as well.

Se'if 2 (Rama) – Kimcha DePischa: Charity Before Pesach

Rama: The minhag is to purchase wheat to distribute to poor people in advance of Pesach. Anyone who has been living in the community for 12 months must contribute to the communal fund for the poor.

Key Points

  • Rama uses the word "minhag" (custom), but the Acharonim point out this appears to be a genuine obligation — it is recorded in the Talmud Yerushalmi as obligatory.
  • Resolution: The obligation is to give tzedakah for Pesach. The minhag aspect is the specific practice of going and buying actual food/wheat rather than just giving money.
  • Why specifically Pesach? Pesach is recognised as the most expensive of the Chagim — we don't find a parallel halacha before Sukkot or Shavuot.

The Name "Kimcha DePischa" (קמחא דפסחא)

  • Literally: "the flour of Pesach."
  • Development of the practice:
  • Originally: buy wheat for the poor (Rama)
  • Then: give flour
  • Current practice: give matzot themselves (Mishnah Berurah)
  • Any form of tzedakah given for Pesach fulfils this obligation.

Who Is Obligated?

  • Someone who has lived in the community for 12 months.
  • Someone who has moved to the community with intent to live there permanently — even if less than 12 months.
  • A person visiting just for Pesach — lesser obligation, though people should still support them.

Priorities in Giving

  • There is an obligation to focus first on the poor within your own town/community.
  • Giving to organisations and Torah institutions worldwide is commendable, but it needs to start at home, especially before Pesach.

Se'if 2 (continued) – Practices During the Month of Nisan

No Tachanun the Entire Month

Reasons for the festive character of Nisan:

  1. First 12 days: In the time of the Mishkan, the inauguration (Chanukat HaMishkan) took place — each Nasi brought his sacrifice daily, making each day a Yom Tov.
  2. 8 days of Pesach follow.
  3. Since the majority of the month (20 out of 30 days) is already festive, we extend it to the entire month.
  4. Midrash: The inauguration of the future Beit HaMikdash will take place at the end of Nisan, after Pesach.

Specific Practices Omitted

  • No Tachanun the entire month of Nisan.
  • No Tzidkatecha at Shabbat Mincha — because Tzidkatecha is a form of Tzidduk HaDin (acceptance of divine judgement / remembrance of the dead) for three Tzaddikim who died on Shabbat (Moshe, David, and possibly Shlomo). Inappropriate during the festive month.
  • No public eulogies — in olden days, eulogies were intensely emotional events with crying and chest-beating; this is forbidden during Nisan.
  • No public fasts announced during Nisan.

Stone Settings (Matzeivot)

  • It is forbidden to schedule a stone setting (unveiling) during the month of Nisan. This is not widely known.
  • A funeral must take place (with limited eulogy), but a stone setting — which involves eulogies and sadness — should not be fixed for a date during Nisan.

Fasting on a Wedding Day in Nisan

  • Some allow it as a private fast — this is the general Ashkenazi practice.
  • Sephardi position: The minhag of the Sephardim is that it is actually wrong (not correct) for a Chatan to fast on his wedding day — the wedding day is a personal Yom Tov, making fasting inappropriate.
  • Exception: If the Chatan is getting married at night, he may fast during the day and eat before the Chuppah.
  • If getting married during the day — Sephardim do not fast.

No Tzidduk HaDin at Funerals

  • The Rama states: we refrain from Tzidduk HaDin at funerals during the entire month of Nisan.

Mizmor LeTodah on Erev Pesach

  • We do not say Mizmor LeTodah on Erev Pesach — because the Todah offering included bread (chametz), and since chametz could not be offered on Erev Pesach, we omit the psalm.
  • Note: This is a minhag.

Isru Chag (איסרו חג)

Rama: It is customary to eat well on the day after each of the three festivals. This is called Isru Chag.

  • Source: Gemara in Sukkot, expounding the pasuk from Hallel: אִסְרוּ חַג בַּעֲבֹתִים עַד קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ.
  • Simple meaning: "Bind the festival offering with ropes to the horns of the altar."
  • Gemara's drash: אִסְרוּ חַג — "tie yourself to the festival" / stay attached to its spirit. בַּעֲבֹתִים — with "fat" animals (עבות = thick/fat), i.e., eat well. עַד קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ — as if one offered a sacrifice.
  • Concept: After the spiritual high of the Chag, we take something mundane (a meal) and infuse it with the light of the Chag, carrying it into daily life.
  • One should not fast on Isru Chag — not after Pesach, Shavuot, or Sukkot.

Siman 430 – Shabbat HaGadol (שבת הגדול)

Contains 1 Se'if

Se'if 1 – Why It Is Called Shabbat HaGadol

Shulchan Aruch: The Shabbat preceding Pesach is called Shabbat HaGadol because of the miracle (נס) that occurred on it.

The Miracle

  • When Bnei Yisrael left Mitzrayim on 15 Nisan (a Thursday), the 10th of Nisan was a Shabbat.
  • The Torah commands (Parashat Bo): בֶּעָשֹׂר לַחֹדֶשׁ הַזֶּה — on the 10th of Nisan, each family was to take a sheep and prepare it for the Korban Pesach.
  • The sheep was the deity of the Egyptians. The Jews took these sheep and tied them to their doorposts openly.
  • The great miracle: Despite the Egyptians' fury, they could not harm the Jews. The dread of Hashem was already upon them.
  • The Chachamim chose to commemorate this miracle on the Shabbat (not on the 10th of the month), even though Shabbat doesn't always fall on the 10th.

Alternative Reason

  • Some Acharonim say it is called "HaGadol" because the rabbi gives the longest (gadol) derashah of the year on this Shabbat.

The Greeting of Shabbat HaGadol

  • The Chida writes that the minhag of the Medakdikim (meticulous ones) of Yerushalayim is to greet each other with "Shabbat HaGadol Shalom" rather than the usual "Shabbat Shalom U'Mevorach."
  • This is the only special Shabbat (besides Shabbat Shuvah) where the greeting changes — despite other named Shabbatot existing (Shabbat Chazon, Shabbat Nachamu, etc.).

When Erev Pesach Falls on Shabbat (This Year — 5785)

  • Shabbat HaGadol itself is Erev Pesach — that Shabbat carries the title "Shabbat HaGadol."
  • However, the great derashah is given on the preceding Shabbat — because on Erev Pesach/Shabbat itself, it would be too late to teach people how to kasher utensils, etc.
  • Everything else — including the Ashkenazi minhag of reading the Haggadah (see below) — remains on Erev Pesach/Shabbat HaGadol itself.

Rama – Reading Part of the Haggadah on Shabbat HaGadol

  • Ashkenazi minhag: Read part of the Haggadah on Shabbat HaGadol — from "Avadim Hayinu" until "Lechaper Al Kol Avonoteinu."
  • Reason: To commemorate the miracle of the 10th of Nisan.
  • Vilna Gaon's reservation: He did not follow this minhag, since the Haggadah itself states the mitzvah of discussing the Exodus is specifically on Pesach night, not before.
  • Result: Not all Ashkenazi communities follow this minhag, though many do.

Siman 431 – The Time of Bedikat Chametz (בדיקת חמץ)

Multiple Se'ifim discussed

Se'if 1 – When and How to Check

Shulchan Aruch: At the beginning of the night of the 14th (of Nisan), one checks for chametz by the light of a candle, in all the cracks and crevices of the house, in all places where it is customary (derech) to bring chametz.

The Timing

  • The mitzvah is to perform Bedikat Chametz as early as possible at the beginning of the night.
  • This is more than the general principle of zerizin makdimin (the diligent perform mitzvot early). There is a specific din that the Chachamim instituted Bedikat Chametz to be at the very beginning of the night.

This Year (5785): The 14th falls on Friday night (Shabbat), so Bedikat Chametz cannot be performed then. It is therefore brought forward to Thursday night.

Why Bedikat Chametz Exists — Torah vs. Rabbinic Obligation

Min HaTorah (Biblical level):

  • A person can fulfil the obligation by simply nullifying (bitul) ownership of all chametz — sincerely declaring in one's heart that he relinquishes all property rights over any chametz he owns.
  • The Torah prohibits: בַּל יֵרָאֶה וּבַל יִמָּצֵא — chametz should not "be seen" or "be found" that belongs to you.
  • Once ownership is relinquished, even chametz physically present in your home is not a Torah violation.
  • Seeing chametz that isn't yours (e.g., walking past a bakery on Pesach) — no prohibition at all.

The Chachamim instituted Bedikat Chametz for two reasons:

  1. Sincerity concern: People may not genuinely relinquish ownership. The test: if your chametz were stolen over Pesach, would you truly feel no loss? If you'd claim insurance, the bitul wasn't sincere.
  2. Temptation concern: Even with sincere bitul, if chametz is physically present, a person may forget it's Pesach and come to eat, drink, or benefit from it.

Therefore: The Chachamim required physical searching and removal of chametz — not merely verbal nullification.

Note on Mechirat Chametz (selling chametz): The concept of selling chametz is a much later development. These halachot assume no sale — the obligation is to physically remove and destroy.

Why Specifically at Night?

Two reasons:

  1. Practical: Nighttime is when people are home from work and available.
  2. Functional: The contrast of darkness with candlelight allows one to see into corners and crevices more effectively than daylight alone.

Using a Torch / Phone Flashlight

  • The Poskim accept that a torch or phone flashlight works as "or ha'ner" (the light of the candle) for Bedikat Chametz. It has the same — if not better — illumination.

Switching Off the Lights?

  • Syrian minhag: Switch off electric lights and search by candlelight only.
  • Justification: The contrast of darkness with the candle flame may help reveal chametz in corners (per Mishnah Berurah's reasoning for nighttime).
  • Practical ruling: This is a nice minhag but not obligatory. If searching is more effective with lights on, that is perfectly fine. The Rav personally keeps lights on.

What Needs to Be Checked?

The rule: Only places where it is derech (customary) to bring chametz need to be checked.

  • In Talmudic and Rishonic times, there was no concept of weeks-long Pesach cleaning. The entire search was done on the night of the 14th, taking several hours.
  • Modern reality: Bigger homes, more appliances, more complexity — so people clean in advance.

If you cleaned in advance:

  • Areas checked before Pesach do not need rechecking on the night of the 14th, provided you are certain no chametz has entered since.
  • "Certain" means: you know that no one has gone into that room/area since it was checked.

Exception — Young Children:

  • The Gemara compares a child to a mouse — they carry chametz from place to place.
  • Any area within a child's reach must be rechecked on the night of Bedikat Chametz, even if previously cleaned.
  • This includes: ground level, low drawers, anywhere a child could drop food while walking around.

No need to check:

  • Chandeliers, toilets, tops of bedroom cupboards, attics — anywhere chametz is never brought or stored.
  • If there is a strict household policy of no food in certain areas (e.g., upstairs bedrooms), those areas don't require checking.
  • Exception: With young children, you must check everywhere they can reach — even toilets.

Restrictions Before Bedikat Chametz — Not Beginning Activities

General principle (found in many areas of halacha): When the time for a mitzvah arrives, one may not begin activities that could cause one to forget.

Shulchan Aruch:

  • From the time of Bedikat Chametz (nightfall, or half an hour before for eating), one may not begin a new meal, activity, or Torah study.
  • If one began before the time arrived — one may continue (began b'heter).

Mishnah Berurah — Stricter View:

  • There is a special din in Bedikat Chametz beyond the regular rule.
  • Since the Chachamim's takanah was specifically for the beginning of the night, even having something small to eat is problematic once the time arrives.
  • One should begin Bedikat Chametz immediately.

Rama — Even Stricter:

  • Even if one began eating or learning before the time — one must stop when the time for Bedikah arrives.
  • This is unique to Bedikat Chametz — in all other areas of halacha, if you began before the time b'heter, you may continue.
  • This underscores the special emphasis on beginning Bedikat Chametz right at the start of the night.

Arvit vs. Bedikat Chametz — Which Comes First?

Halacha (Sephardi psak): Arvit first, then Bedikat Chametz.

Question raised: With Chanukah, we learned that candle-lighting comes before Arvit because the mitzvah is specifically at the beginning of the night. Why not the same here?

Two answers:

  1. The "beginning of the night" aspect of Bedikat Chametz is debatable — the Shulchan Aruch mentions it but also says one need not stop a prior activity (unlike Chanukah where you do stop).
  2. Bedikat Chametz takes hours, so there is a real concern that if done first, one will forget or be too tired for Arvit. Chanukah candle-lighting takes one minute — no such concern.

Beginning from Sunset (Shkiyah)

  • In England (and other northern locations), nightfall (tzet hakochavim) can be late — around 8:15–8:30 PM in mid-April.
  • There is room to begin Bedikat Chametz from shkiyah (sunset), provided the search continues into actual nightfall.
  • This leniency is especially relevant for families with young children who want to participate.

Siman 432 – The Bracha on Bedikat Chametz (ברכת בדיקת חמץ)

Multiple Se'ifim discussed

Se'if 1 – The Wording of the Bracha: "Al Biur Chametz"

Shulchan Aruch: Before beginning Bedikat Chametz, one recites the bracha: "…אשר קדשנו במצוותיו וציוונו על בִּעוּר חמץ" — "…Who has sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us concerning the destruction of chametz."

Why "Biur" and Not "Bedikat"?

  • The question: We are about to search for chametz (bedikah), not destroy it (biur). Why say "al biur chametz"?
  • Answer: Bedikat Chametz is only a means to an end. The bedikah has no intrinsic purpose — it exists solely to enable the biur (destruction) the next morning.
  • If one searches the entire house meticulously but never destroys the chametz found — the whole exercise was pointless.
  • The Chachamim instituted the search so that one can then properly destroy.
  • Therefore, the bracha is formulated on the end purpose (biur), not the intermediate step (bedikah).

The Word "Al" (על) vs. "Le-" (ל)

  • We say "al biur" (על ביעור) — "concerning" the destruction — not "le-va'er" (לבער) — "to destroy."
  • "Le-" would imply: "I am about to do this right now."
  • "Al" implies: "concerning this matter" — it is going to happen (tomorrow), and the bedikah tonight is part of that process.

The Meaning of "Biur"

  • Sephardi understanding: Biur means destruction / getting rid of — by any means (burning, crumbling, flushing, etc.).
  • Ashkenazi understanding: Biur means specifically burning — which is why Ashkenazim are particular to burn their chametz.

Unique Aspect — Bracha Now, Fulfilment Later

  • Question raised in class: This seems unprecedented — the bracha is said at night, but the biur doesn't happen until ~12 hours later the next morning.
  • Resolution: The bracha is technically going on the bedikah itself — that is the mitzvah being performed now. We simply use the word "biur" because the bedikah is a means toward the biur. There is no hefsek (interruption), because the mitzvah act (searching) begins immediately after the bracha.

A Person Without a Home

  • Discussion: Does a homeless person perform Bedikat Chametz? On his pockets?
  • The takanah of the Chachamim was for homes and dwellings — not pockets.
  • While we do check pockets as well (agav / incidentally), there was no formal institution for checking pockets.
  • This remains a matter of debate among the Poskim.

Se'if 2 – Forgetting the Bracha

Shulchan Aruch: If one forgot to say the bracha before beginning, one may say it at any point during the search, as long as one has not yet finished — because searching the entire home is one continuous mitzvah.

  • Example: If you've searched half the house and then remember — say the bracha and continue searching the remaining half.

Se'if 3 – Speaking During Bedikat Chametz

Shulchan Aruch:

  • From the bracha until beginning to search: One must not speak at all (no hefsek between bracha and start of the mitzvah).
  • After beginning the search: One should ideally not speak until completing the entire search, in order to maintain focus and attention.
  • In practice: Once at least one room/area has been searched, the bracha has "landed" on something. If necessary, one may speak — but it is better not to.

What Speech Is Permitted?

  • Anything related to the search: "Has this cupboard been checked?" / "What is this ingredient?" / "Check behind there" — all permitted; it is part of the bedikah.
  • Unrelated speech (phone calls, casual conversation): Should be avoided. If urgent, one may take a phone call — but try to minimise.
