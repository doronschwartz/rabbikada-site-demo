Series Introduction

  • New series for 2025 on Hilchot Pesah, following previous learning of Hilchot Shabbat, Sukkot, and Hanukkah.
  • Hilchot Pesah comprises 61 simanim in the Shulhan Arukh — significantly more complex and extensive than Hanukkah (~15 simanim).
  • This year (5785/2025): Erev Pesah falls on Shabbat (Pesah begins Mosei Shabbat), adding unique complexities addressed in dedicated simanim later.
  • Focus of this series: The preparatory halachot from the beginning — Bedikat Hames, Biur Hames, Hagalat Kelim, baking of Massot — rather than the Seder itself.
  • Primary source: Shulhan Arukh with Sephardi psak, supplemented by Rama, Mishnah Berurah, and Aharonim.

Siman 429 – Laws of the Month of Nisan (שאין נופלין על פניהם בכל חודש ניסן)

Contains 2 Se'ifim

Se'if 1 – Learning Hilchot Pesah 30 Days Before

Shulhan Arukh: שואלין בהלכות הפסח קודם לפסח שלושים יום — We ask and discuss the halachot of Pesah beginning 30 days before the Hag.

Source — Two Gemarot and an Apparent Contradiction

  1. Gemara (Pesachim 6a): שואלין ודורשין בענין החג שלושים יום קודם — "We inquire and expound regarding the Hag 30 days before." Most Rishonim understand this as an obligation to teach, study, and discuss the halachot of Pesah from 30 days prior.
  2. Gemara (Megillah): Moshe Rabbeinu instituted that Bnei Yisrael should study the laws of each Hag on the Hag itself — Hilchot Pesah on Pesah, Hilchot Sukkot on Sukkot.

The Contradiction — Rabbenu Nisim's Resolution

  • Question: Is the obligation 30 days before, or on the Hag itself?
  • Rabbenu Nisim's Chiddush: The "30 days before" is a din in Talmud Torah — it means that when a rabbi has many questions to answer, from 30 days before Pesah he must prioritise Pesah-related questions over others. However, the actual obligation to teach and study the halachot is only on the Hag itself, per Moshe's institution.

Majority View — Obligation from 30 Days Before

  • Most Rishonim reject Rabbenu Nisim and hold there is a genuine obligation to learn Hilchot Pesah from 30 days before.
  • They resolve the contradiction in two ways:
  1. Moshe's institution was the original; the 30-day rule was a later development expanding it.
  2. Beit Yoseph: 30 days before = learning about preparations for the Hag. Moshe's institution = learning the halachot of the Hag day itself (Yom Tov). Two different aspects.

Practical Halacha

  • The Shulhan Arukh rules in accordance with the majority: we begin studying Hilchot Pesah 30 days before.
  • Every individual has an obligation to review and learn Hilchot Pesah annually — set aside time each day from 30 days before.
  • The 30 days before Pesah begins on Purim — this is why there is a minhag to share a Pesah-related halacha or Torah idea already on Purim itself.
  • Rambam lists these as two distinct dinim: (a) learning on the Hag itself, and (b) preparing 30 days before.

Mishnah Berurah — Focus on Practical Halachot

  • The Mishnah Berurah criticises the practice found in some communities (especially very Charedi ones) where the rabbi gives a highly complex, theoretical pilpul on Shabbat HaGadol that is not practically relevant.
  • He insists the focus must be on relevant, practical halachot of the Hag. This has always been the Derech of the Sephardim as well.

Se'if 2 (Rama) – Kimcha DePischa: Charity Before Pesah

Rama: The minhag is to purchase wheat to distribute to poor people in advance of Pesah. Anyone who has been living in the community for 12 months must contribute to the communal fund for the poor.

Key Points

  • Rama uses the word "minhag" (custom), but the Aharonim point out this appears to be a genuine obligation — it is recorded in the Talmud Yerushalmi as obligatory.
  • Resolution: The obligation is to give tzedakah for Pesah. The minhag aspect is the specific practice of going and buying actual food/wheat rather than just giving money.
  • Why specifically Pesah? Pesah is recognised as the most expensive of the Chagim — we don't find a parallel halacha before Sukkot or Shavuot.

The Name "Kimcha DePischa" (קמחא דפסחא)

  • Literally: "the flour of Pesah."
  • Development of the practice:
  • Originally: buy wheat for the poor (Rama)
  • Then: give flour
  • Current practice: give matzot themselves (Mishnah Berurah)
  • Any form of tzedakah given for Pesah fulfils this obligation.

Who Is Obligated?

  • Someone who has lived in the community for 12 months.
  • Someone who has moved to the community with intent to live there permanently — even if less than 12 months.
  • A person visiting just for Pesah — lesser obligation, though people should still support them.

Priorities in Giving

  • There is an obligation to focus first on the poor within your own town/community.
  • Giving to organisations and Torah institutions worldwide is commendable, but it needs to start at home, especially before Pesah.

Se'if 2 (continued) – Practices During the Month of Nisan

No Tachanun the Entire Month

Reasons for the festive character of Nisan:

  1. First 12 days: In the time of the Mishkan, the inauguration (Chanukat HaMishkan) took place — each Nasi brought his sacrifice daily, making each day a Yom Tov.
  2. 8 days of Pesah follow.
  3. Since the majority of the month (20 out of 30 days) is already festive, we extend it to the entire month.
  4. Midrash: The inauguration of the future Beit HaMikdash will take place at the end of Nisan, after Pesah.

Specific Practices Omitted

  • No Tachanun the entire month of Nisan.
  • No Tzidkatecha at Shabbat Minhah — because Tzidkatecha is a form of Tzidduk HaDin (acceptance of divine judgement / remembrance of the dead) for three Tzaddikim who died on Shabbat (Moshe, David, and possibly Shlomo). Inappropriate during the festive month.
  • No public eulogies — in olden days, eulogies were intensely emotional events with crying and chest-beating; this is forbidden during Nisan.
  • No public fasts announced during Nisan.

Stone Settings (Matzeivot)

  • It is forbidden to schedule a stone setting (unveiling) during the month of Nisan. This is not widely known.
  • A funeral must take place (with limited eulogy), but a stone setting — which involves eulogies and sadness — should not be fixed for a date during Nisan.

Fasting on a Wedding Day in Nisan

  • Some allow it as a private fast — this is the general Ashkenazi practice.
  • Sephardi position: The minhag of the Sephardim is that it is actually wrong (not correct) for a Chatan to fast on his wedding day — the wedding day is a personal Yom Tov, making fasting inappropriate.
  • Exception: If the Chatan is getting married at night, he may fast during the day and eat before the Chuppah.
  • If getting married during the day — Sephardim do not fast.

No Tzidduk HaDin at Funerals

  • The Rama states: we refrain from Tzidduk HaDin at funerals during the entire month of Nisan.

Mizmor LeTodah on Erev Pesah

  • We do not say Mizmor LeTodah on Erev Pesah — because the Todah offering included bread (hames), and since hames could not be offered on Erev Pesah, we omit the psalm.
  • Note: This is a minhag.

Isru Hag (איסרו חג)

Rama: It is customary to eat well on the day after each of the three festivals. This is called Isru Hag.

  • Source: Gemara in Sukkot, expounding the pasuk from Hallel: אִסְרוּ חַג בַּעֲבֹתִים עַד קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ.
  • Simple meaning: "Bind the festival offering with ropes to the horns of the altar."
  • Gemara's drash: אִסְרוּ חַג — "tie yourself to the festival" / stay attached to its spirit. בַּעֲבֹתִים — with "fat" animals (עבות = thick/fat), i.e., eat well. עַד קַרְנוֹת הַמִּזְבֵּחַ — as if one offered a sacrifice.
  • Concept: After the spiritual high of the Hag, we take something mundane (a meal) and infuse it with the light of the Hag, carrying it into daily life.
  • One should not fast on Isru Hag — not after Pesah, Shavuot, or Sukkot.

Siman 430 – Shabbat HaGadol (שבת הגדול)

Contains 1 Se'if

Se'if 1 – Why It Is Called Shabbat HaGadol

Shulhan Arukh: The Shabbat preceding Pesah is called Shabbat HaGadol because of the miracle (נס) that occurred on it.

The Miracle

  • When Bnei Yisrael left Mitzrayim on 15 Nisan (a Thursday), the 10th of Nisan was a Shabbat.
  • The Torah commands (Parashat Bo): בֶּעָשֹׂר לַחֹדֶשׁ הַזֶּה — on the 10th of Nisan, each family was to take a sheep and prepare it for the Korban Pesah.
  • The sheep was the deity of the Egyptians. The Jews took these sheep and tied them to their doorposts openly.
  • The great miracle: Despite the Egyptians' fury, they could not harm the Jews. The dread of Hashem was already upon them.
  • The Hachamim chose to commemorate this miracle on the Shabbat (not on the 10th of the month), even though Shabbat doesn't always fall on the 10th.

Alternative Reason

  • Some Aharonim say it is called "HaGadol" because the rabbi gives the longest (gadol) derashah of the year on this Shabbat.

The Greeting of Shabbat HaGadol

  • The Chida writes that the minhag of the Medakdikim (meticulous ones) of Yerushalayim is to greet each other with "Shabbat HaGadol Shalom" rather than the usual "Shabbat Shalom U'Mevorach."
  • This is the only special Shabbat (besides Shabbat Shuvah) where the greeting changes — despite other named Shabbatot existing (Shabbat Hazon, Shabbat Nachamu, etc.).

When Erev Pesah Falls on Shabbat (This Year — 5785)

  • Shabbat HaGadol itself is Erev Pesah — that Shabbat carries the title "Shabbat HaGadol."
  • However, the great derashah is given on the preceding Shabbat — because on Erev Pesah/Shabbat itself, it would be too late to teach people how to kasher utensils, etc.
  • Everything else — including the Ashkenazi minhag of reading the Haggadah (see below) — remains on Erev Pesah/Shabbat HaGadol itself.

Rama – Reading Part of the Haggadah on Shabbat HaGadol

  • Ashkenazi minhag: Read part of the Haggadah on Shabbat HaGadol — from "Avadim Hayinu" until "Lechaper Al Kol Avonoteinu."
  • Reason: To commemorate the miracle of the 10th of Nisan.
  • Vilna Gaon's reservation: He did not follow this minhag, since the Haggadah itself states the misvah of discussing the Exodus is specifically on Pesah night, not before.
  • Result: Not all Ashkenazi communities follow this minhag, though many do.

Siman 431 – The Time of Bedikat Hames (בדיקת חמץ)

Multiple Se'ifim discussed

Se'if 1 – When and How to Check

Shulhan Arukh: At the beginning of the night of the 14th (of Nisan), one checks for hames by the light of a candle, in all the cracks and crevices of the house, in all places where it is customary (derech) to bring hames.

The Timing

  • The misvah is to perform Bedikat Hames as early as possible at the beginning of the night.
  • This is more than the general principle of zerizin makdimin (the diligent perform misvot early). There is a specific din that the Hachamim instituted Bedikat Hames to be at the very beginning of the night.

This Year (5785): The 14th falls on Friday night (Shabbat), so Bedikat Hames cannot be performed then. It is therefore brought forward to Thursday night.

Why Bedikat Hames Exists — Torah vs. Rabbinic Obligation

Min HaTorah (Biblical level):

  • A person can fulfil the obligation by simply nullifying (bitul) ownership of all hames — sincerely declaring in one's heart that he relinquishes all property rights over any hames he owns.
  • The Torah prohibits: בַּל יֵרָאֶה וּבַל יִמָּצֵא — hames should not "be seen" or "be found" that belongs to you.
  • Once ownership is relinquished, even hames physically present in your home is not a Torah violation.
  • Seeing hames that isn't yours (e.g., walking past a bakery on Pesah) — no prohibition at all.

The Hachamim instituted Bedikat Hames for two reasons:

  1. Sincerity concern: People may not genuinely relinquish ownership. The test: if your hames were stolen over Pesah, would you truly feel no loss? If you'd claim insurance, the bitul wasn't sincere.
  2. Temptation concern: Even with sincere bitul, if hames is physically present, a person may forget it's Pesah and come to eat, drink, or benefit from it.

Therefore: The Hachamim required physical searching and removal of hames — not merely verbal nullification.

Note on Mekhirat Hames (selling hames): The concept of selling hames is a much later development. These halachot assume no sale — the obligation is to physically remove and destroy.

Why Specifically at Night?

Two reasons:

  1. Practical: Nighttime is when people are home from work and available.
  2. Functional: The contrast of darkness with candlelight allows one to see into corners and crevices more effectively than daylight alone.

Using a Torch / Phone Flashlight

  • The Poskim accept that a torch or phone flashlight works as "or ha'ner" (the light of the candle) for Bedikat Hames. It has the same — if not better — illumination.

Switching Off the Lights?

  • Syrian minhag: Switch off electric lights and search by candlelight only.
  • Justification: The contrast of darkness with the candle flame may help reveal hames in corners (per Mishnah Berurah's reasoning for nighttime).
  • Practical ruling: This is a nice minhag but not obligatory. If searching is more effective with lights on, that is perfectly fine. The Rav personally keeps lights on.

What Needs to Be Checked?

The rule: Only places where it is derech (customary) to bring hames need to be checked.

  • In Talmudic and Rishonic times, there was no concept of weeks-long Pesah cleaning. The entire search was done on the night of the 14th, taking several hours.
  • Modern reality: Bigger homes, more appliances, more complexity — so people clean in advance.

If you cleaned in advance:

  • Areas checked before Pesah do not need rechecking on the night of the 14th, provided you are certain no hames has entered since.
  • "Certain" means: you know that no one has gone into that room/area since it was checked.

Exception — Young Children:

  • The Gemara compares a child to a mouse — they carry hames from place to place.
  • Any area within a child's reach must be rechecked on the night of Bedikat Hames, even if previously cleaned.
  • This includes: ground level, low drawers, anywhere a child could drop food while walking around.

No need to check:

  • Chandeliers, toilets, tops of bedroom cupboards, attics — anywhere hames is never brought or stored.
  • If there is a strict household policy of no food in certain areas (e.g., upstairs bedrooms), those areas don't require checking.
  • Exception: With young children, you must check everywhere they can reach — even toilets.

Restrictions Before Bedikat Hames — Not Beginning Activities

General principle (found in many areas of halacha): When the time for a misvah arrives, one may not begin activities that could cause one to forget.

Shulhan Arukh:

  • From the time of Bedikat Hames (nightfall, or half an hour before for eating), one may not begin a new meal, activity, or Torah study.
  • If one began before the time arrived — one may continue (began b'heter).

Mishnah Berurah — Stricter View:

  • There is a special din in Bedikat Hames beyond the regular rule.
  • Since the Hachamim's takanah was specifically for the beginning of the night, even having something small to eat is problematic once the time arrives.
  • One should begin Bedikat Hames immediately.

Rama — Even Stricter:

  • Even if one began eating or learning before the time — one must stop when the time for Bedikah arrives.
  • This is unique to Bedikat Hames — in all other areas of halacha, if you began before the time b'heter, you may continue.
  • This underscores the special emphasis on beginning Bedikat Hames right at the start of the night.

Arvit vs. Bedikat Hames — Which Comes First?

Halacha (Sephardi psak): Arvit first, then Bedikat Hames.

Question raised: With Hanukkah, we learned that candle-lighting comes before Arvit because the misvah is specifically at the beginning of the night. Why not the same here?

Two answers:

  1. The "beginning of the night" aspect of Bedikat Hames is debatable — the Shulhan Arukh mentions it but also says one need not stop a prior activity (unlike Hanukkah where you do stop).
  2. Bedikat Hames takes hours, so there is a real concern that if done first, one will forget or be too tired for Arvit. Hanukkah candle-lighting takes one minute — no such concern.

Beginning from Sunset (Shkiyah)

  • In England (and other northern locations), nightfall (tzet hakochavim) can be late — around 8:15–8:30 PM in mid-April.
  • There is room to begin Bedikat Hames from shkiyah (sunset), provided the search continues into actual nightfall.
  • This leniency is especially relevant for families with young children who want to participate.

Siman 432 – The Berakhah on Bedikat Hames (ברכת בדיקת חמץ)

Multiple Se'ifim discussed

Se'if 1 – The Wording of the Berakhah: "Al Biur Hames"

Shulhan Arukh: Before beginning Bedikat Hames, one recites the berakhah: "…אשר קדשנו במצוותיו וציוונו על בִּעוּר חמץ" — "…Who has sanctified us with His commandments and commanded us concerning the destruction of hames."

Why "Biur" and Not "Bedikat"?

  • The question: We are about to search for hames (bedikah), not destroy it (biur). Why say "al biur hames"?
  • Answer: Bedikat Hames is only a means to an end. The bedikah has no intrinsic purpose — it exists solely to enable the biur (destruction) the next morning.
  • If one searches the entire house meticulously but never destroys the hames found — the whole exercise was pointless.
  • The Hachamim instituted the search so that one can then properly destroy.
  • Therefore, the berakhah is formulated on the end purpose (biur), not the intermediate step (bedikah).

The Word "Al" (על) vs. "Le-" (ל)

  • We say "al biur" (על ביעור) — "concerning" the destruction — not "le-va'er" (לבער) — "to destroy."
  • "Le-" would imply: "I am about to do this right now."
  • "Al" implies: "concerning this matter" — it is going to happen (tomorrow), and the bedikah tonight is part of that process.

The Meaning of "Biur"

  • Sephardi understanding: Biur means destruction / getting rid of — by any means (burning, crumbling, flushing, etc.).
  • Ashkenazi understanding: Biur means specifically burning — which is why Ashkenazim are particular to burn their hames.

Unique Aspect — Berakhah Now, Fulfilment Later

  • Question raised in class: This seems unprecedented — the berakhah is said at night, but the biur doesn't happen until ~12 hours later the next morning.
  • Resolution: The berakhah is technically going on the bedikah itself — that is the misvah being performed now. We simply use the word "biur" because the bedikah is a means toward the biur. There is no hefsek (interruption), because the misvah act (searching) begins immediately after the berakhah.

A Person Without a Home

  • Discussion: Does a homeless person perform Bedikat Hames? On his pockets?
  • The takanah of the Hachamim was for homes and dwellings — not pockets.
  • While we do check pockets as well (agav / incidentally), there was no formal institution for checking pockets.
  • This remains a matter of debate among the Poskim.

Se'if 2 – Forgetting the Berakhah

Shulhan Arukh: If one forgot to say the berakhah before beginning, one may say it at any point during the search, as long as one has not yet finished — because searching the entire home is one continuous misvah.

  • Example: If you've searched half the house and then remember — say the berakhah and continue searching the remaining half.

Se'if 3 – Speaking During Bedikat Hames

Shulhan Arukh:

  • From the berakhah until beginning to search: One must not speak at all (no hefsek between berakhah and start of the misvah).
  • After beginning the search: One should ideally not speak until completing the entire search, in order to maintain focus and attention.
  • In practice: Once at least one room/area has been searched, the berakhah has "landed" on something. If necessary, one may speak — but it is better not to.

What Speech Is Permitted?

  • Anything related to the search: "Has this cupboard been checked?" / "What is this ingredient?" / "Check behind there" — all permitted; it is part of the bedikah.
  • Unrelated speech (phone calls, casual conversation): Should be avoided. If urgent, one may take a phone call — but try to minimise.
