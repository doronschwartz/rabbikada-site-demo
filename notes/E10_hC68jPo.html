Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 289, seifim 1-2 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 8 February 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Making the bed and laying the table

This class was given on Zoom. Siman 289 is the order of the Shabbat morning meal, in two seifim.

Seif 1: the table is laid and the cloth spread as on Friday night, with two loaves, Kiddush is made, the meal must be in the same place as Kiddush, and nothing may be eaten before it. Drinking water before Shaharit is allowed, since there is no obligation of Kiddush yet.

The Rabbi asked whether one may make one's bed on Shabbat morning, since it might seem to be preparing for the night. The Mishnah Berurah says it is forbidden unless one plans to sleep in the afternoon, or the bed is in a room where leaving it undone is degrading. The Rabbi explained that people who make their bed every morning do it because an orderly room is proper, not to prepare for sleeping. He told the story attributed to Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach of a father who saw his son's tidy room in yeshiva and needed no further proof that the boy was learning well. So for most of us who make our beds every morning, it is allowed on Shabbat morning. He added that the "mitah" in the Shulhan Arukh most likely refers to the couches people reclined on to eat, which should be set out in honour of the meal.

"Al ken berakh": a lesson about criticising minhagim

The Mishnah Berurah lists customs of verses said before the morning Kiddush, all fine. Then he writes that "some of the masses" begin from "Al ken berakh HaShem et yom HaShabbat vayekaddeshehu", which is half a verse, and that this is wrong because one may not split a verse.

The Rabbi made several points:

  • The Mishnah Berurah's problem is not saying Hashem's name in half a verse. Both Sefaradim and Ashkenazim may say Hashem's name when quoting part of a verse. Sefaradim say the full name even then, as Rav Ovadia Yoseph always did.
  • His problem is presenting half a verse as if it were a complete statement.
  • Saying "Al ken berakh" is the custom of all Sefaradim, Middle Eastern and Spanish and Portuguese. The Ben Ish Hai, who did not know the Mishnah Berurah, has a teshuvah that one may quote from an atnah, the main pause in the verse, since that is a complete phrase. "Al ken berakh" begins after an atnah, so the Sefaradi custom is "absolutely fine."

The lesson: "when we criticize other minhagim we should always be aware of the full understanding." Customs that have gone on for hundreds of years usually have a reason.

What Kiddush actually is. The essential morning Kiddush is just Borei Peri HaGefen. Everything before it is a beautiful custom. If you have no siddur or do not know the verses, just say Borei Peri HaGefen.

The Gemara calls it "Kiddusha Rabba", the great Kiddush. The Rabbi explained this is a euphemism: Friday night's Kiddush is from the Torah and the morning one is rabbinic, so the Sages gave it a grand name to stop people taking it lightly.

When do women become obligated?

Before praying, one may not do Kiddush, so the weekday rules apply: water, tea or coffee are allowed. Once one has prayed, the obligation of Kiddush begins and one may not even drink water until Kiddush. Men and women are equal in this.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that someone allowed for medical reasons to eat before praying must make Kiddush first. In his view, the obligation begins as soon as one is allowed to eat. Women may eat before praying, so on this view a woman would need to make Kiddush before her first drink in the morning.

Many disagree, among them Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul: Kiddush begins only once one has finished praying. A woman who prays on Shabbat morning may eat and drink before praying; after she prays, she may not until Kiddush. The Rabbi said this is the halacha for us.

The difficulty is a woman who does not pray on Shabbat morning. Rav Ben Sion and the Kaf HaHayim hold she must make Kiddush before eating or drinking. The Rabbi prefers the approach of Rav Moshe Feinstein: there is a halachic obligation for husband and wife to eat the Shabbat meals together, and Kiddush belongs with the meal. A married woman therefore has no obligation of Kiddush while her husband is still in shul and cannot make it.

His conclusions:

  • A married woman, even if she does not pray: she may eat and drink freely until the time shul normally finishes. From then she should not eat or drink until Kiddush.
  • A woman who prays regularly: no obligation until she has prayed.
  • A single woman living alone who does not pray: she needs to make Kiddush before drinking a cup of water. Some argue that until she says the morning berakhot she may still drink, and she should say them early.
  • A girl at home with her parents: asked about a 14-year-old, he said she can rely on her father: she may eat until the time he finishes shul, even if he is late home.

Asked about Seudah Shelishit eaten in shul in winter, he explained the requirement is to eat the main Shabbat meal with one's wife. But if there is a large Kiddush in shul and no lunch at home, a husband who eats his main meal there should ask his wife's permission.

Kiddush on whisky and the "beverage of the town"

Seif 2: where there is no wine, Kiddush may be made on the beverage of the town, such as beer. If there is neither, one eats without Kiddush.

Many people make the morning Kiddush on whisky or vodka in a small shot glass, even with grape juice on the table. The Mishnah Berurah records an old European custom: wine was very expensive, so it was treated as unavailable and kept for Friday night. A second leniency, from the Taz, is that a strong drink needs only a small amount. The Rabbi's view:

  • Sefaradim never had this custom. Sefaradim should not make the morning Kiddush on whisky or vodka when grape juice is available.
  • Even for Ashkenazim he considers it a very weak custom, "extremely dubious" with a tiny cup that does not hold a reviit.

What counts as the beverage of the town? Sefaradim are strict: "Hamar Medina" means a local drink that is alcoholic. In England, beer qualifies, and arguably whisky; he was less sure about vodka. Rav Moshe Feinstein wrote that Coca-Cola in America is the beverage of the country, but Sefaradim do not accept this. Tea and Coca-Cola are not acceptable for Sefaradim, nor alcohol-free beer.

If there is neither wine nor the local beverage, Kiddush is made on bread: wash, say HaMosi, with Kiddush in mind. If there is no bread either, you eat and drink without Kiddush, since one may not fast, and make Kiddush when you can.

The reason for the difference: on Friday night Kiddush on bread is recognisable because it has its own blessing; on Shabbat morning the only blessing is on the drink, so a drink is preferred.

Practical summary

  • If you make your bed every morning, you may make it on Shabbat morning.
  • The essential Shabbat morning Kiddush is Borei Peri HaGefen; the verses before it are custom.
  • After praying, do not eat or drink until Kiddush. A married woman who does not pray may eat until the time shul normally ends.
  • Do not make Kiddush on whisky or vodka when wine or grape juice is available.
  • Without wine, a Sefaradi may use beer in England, but not tea, Coca-Cola or alcohol-free beer.
