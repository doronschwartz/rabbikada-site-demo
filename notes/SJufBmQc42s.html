Topic: Yissakhar and Zevulun partnerships: their sources, how they work in halacha, and what "reward" for a misvah means Sources: Bereshit 49:13 and Devarim 33:18 with Rashi; Zevahim 1:2; Sotah 21a; the Rema, Yoreh Deah 246:1; Rav Moshe Feinstein; Rav Hai Gaon Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Thursday 9 January 2025, the week of Parashat Vayehi

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where the idea comes from

Search online and you will find many organisations offering Yissakhar and Zevulun contracts. The Rabbi set out to ask how such an arrangement actually works. Is it a partnership? Is it fifty-fifty? Is one side more important? And beyond the halacha, what does it mean to share money and Torah?

That week's parashah contains Yaakov's blessing to Zevulun, that he will dwell by the seashore. Rashi explains that Zevulun went out to trade and supported Yissakhar, who sat and learned. Rashi says the same on Moshe's blessing, "Rejoice, Zevulun, in your going out, and Yissakhar in your tents." Zevulun is mentioned first because his support made Yissakhar's Torah possible.

The Gemara has examples too. A Mishnah in Zevahim quotes a Tanna called Shimon ahi Azaryah, "the brother of Azaryah". Rashi explains that Azaryah supported Shimon so that he could learn, and Shimon is named after his brother because of that support.

What cannot be sold

One point, the Rabbi stressed, is accepted by everyone. The Gemara in Sotah tells that Hillel, who was poor, had a wealthy brother called Shevna who offered to pay for a share in Hillel's Torah after the fact, and was refused. Once you have done a misvah, you cannot sell its reward. "However much Torah you've learned, if someone comes to you and says, at the end of your life, I want a share in the Torah you've learned... forget about it, that's impossible."

The only question is an agreement made in advance: I will learn, you will support me, and we will share.

Rav Moshe Feinstein: a real partnership

Rav Moshe Feinstein, in his responsa on Yoreh Deah, understands Yissakhar and Zevulun literally as a real business partnership, and draws several new conclusions from it:

  • It must be fifty-fifty. Zevulun gives half his income and receives half of Yissakhar's reward. The Rabbi remarked that he knew of no such arrangement anywhere in which the supporter really gives half his yearly income.
  • It does not come from maaser money. Since it is a business arrangement, the supporter still owes his tithes separately.
  • Breaking it is like theft. If either side fails to keep his part, the supporter by not paying or the learner by not learning, it is like a partner who takes his share without doing his work.

Someone asked whether such a partnership frees Zevulun from learning himself. The Rabbi said no: everyone must still fix times for Torah, morning and evening.

Rav Hai Gaon: you cannot buy someone's reward

At the other extreme is a responsum of Rav Hai Gaon, from over a thousand years ago, brought at the start of Hilchot Talmud Torah. He was asked whether a person can pay someone to learn for him and receive the reward. He answered that anyone who believes this is a fool and deserves to be punished for thinking it. Just as a person who fasts cannot hand the reward of his fast to someone else, the reward of Torah belongs to the person who learned it.

How then does he explain Yissakhar and Zevulun? Simply. It is not a partnership. Whoever enables someone else to do a misvah receives reward for enabling it, just as enabling a sin is itself wrong. Yissakhar keeps all his reward, and Zevulun is rewarded for supporting Torah.

What is the reward of a misvah?

The Rabbi suggested that the dispute depends on how we understand sekhar, reward. We sometimes picture misvot as deposits in a bank account in Olam HaBa. In the Rambam's thought there is nothing like that. "Sekhar misvah misvah": the reward of a misvah is the misvah.

He gave an analogy. If a child behaves well and you give him a present, that is a reward: there is no inherent link between the present and the deed. But if someone spends months exercising and loses weight, nobody calls the weight loss a reward. It is the consequence. A misvah changes the person who does it. Learning Torah makes you someone who understands God's ways, and the closeness to God that results is the reward. It is fully experienced in Olam HaBa, but it is built now, in the identity we create.

On that view, a fifty-fifty trade in Torah makes no sense. Imagine a great mathematician whose patron says: I will support you for twenty years, and you give me half your mathematics. The understanding is in the mathematician's mind and cannot be handed over. A donor to a university may receive an honorary doctorate, but he does not receive the doctor's knowledge. He deserves recognition as the one who made hundreds of doctorates possible. Someone in the class added that if you pay an inventor, you may own the patent but not his mental effort, and the Rabbi agreed: "Two completely different things."

Middle positions

The Rema, at the start of Hilchot Talmud Torah, writes that a person may make a condition with his fellow that one will learn and the other support him, and they will share the reward, but he cannot sell Torah he has already learned. The language of a condition, the Rabbi said, leans towards a real agreement, closer to Rav Moshe.

The Sis Eliezer has a long responsum trying to make peace between the views. He distinguishes two kinds of reward: sekhar gemuli, the reward for the deed itself, which can be shared, and sekhar seguli, the effect of the misvah on the person, which cannot. The Rabbi said he understood the idea but found it hard to read into Rav Hai Gaon's words. The Sis Eliezer also brings stories suggesting that refusing to let others share in one's misvah can show an ayin ra, a mean eye, and that sharing is a chance to do a misvah not for the sake of reward.

Does Yissakhar lose half his reward? The Or HaHayim says no. Zevulun receives a share, but Yissakhar keeps all of his, like a candle that lights another without losing any of its own light. The Pele Yoes, on the other hand, advises staying away from these partnerships, because why give up a share of eternal reward for money? Only if a person would otherwise be unable to learn at all should he accept one.

Who comes first?

Rashi gives Zevulun precedence, since he is mentioned first. Other passages, such as one in Berakhot, seem to put the learner first and the supporters second. The Rabbi's resolution was that Zevulun comes first because he lays the foundation, "if there is no flour, there is no Torah". Once the foundation is laid and the learning begins, Yissakhar, the one who is learning, takes precedence.

Summary

  • Yissakhar and Zevulun comes from Rashi on the blessings of Yaakov and Moshe, with examples in the Gemara of one brother supporting another.
  • Everyone agrees that Torah already learned cannot be sold; the question is an agreement made in advance.
  • Rav Moshe Feinstein treats it as a real fifty-fifty partnership, outside maaser, binding on both sides; Rav Hai Gaon says reward cannot be transferred, and the supporter is rewarded only as an enabler.
  • On the Rambam's view that the reward of a misvah is the change it makes in us, Torah cannot be traded, though supporting it is itself greatly rewarded.
  • Whatever the arrangement, it cannot apply to the past, and it must be taken seriously by both sides.
