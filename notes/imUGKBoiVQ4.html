Topic: The conversion of a child on the authority of the Beit Din, parental consent, and the child's right to protest at bar or bat misvah Source: Gemara Ketubot 11a; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268 Shiur: Sixth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, Wednesday 30 June 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

From pregnant mothers to children

The previous class dealt with converting a spouse, civil marriages, a woman who converts while pregnant, and a brit milah on Shabbat. Tonight moves on to children, which, the Rabbi said, is very common.

The Gemara in Ketubot

The Mishnah (Ketubot 11a) says that a girl who converted before the age of three receives the full ketubah of two hundred. On this Rav Huna says: a ger katan, a child convert (a girl under twelve, a boy under thirteen), is immersed al daat beit din, on the authority of the Beit Din.

The Gemara asks why this needs saying. It is obvious: conversion is a zekhut, a benefit, and zakhin leadam shelo befanav, one may confer a benefit on a person in his absence, just as I may accept a gift on behalf of a friend who does not know of it. The answer: one might have thought that a non-Jew prefers the freedom of a life without restrictions, so that conversion is not purely a benefit. Rav Huna teaches that this is said only of an adult who has already "tasted the taste of the forbidden." For a child, it is a benefit.

The Gemara's attempt to prove this from the Mishnah is rejected: the Mishnah may speak of a child converting with her father. Rav Huna's novelty is that it works even for a child alone.

A side point from the Rashba. Why does the Gemara say only "we immerse him" and not "we circumcise him"? The Rashba gives three answers. Tevilah applies to boys and girls alike; tevilah is the final step; and, most interestingly, milah is a continuing state. A boy circumcised as a child still bears the milah when he reaches thirteen and is content with it, so it hardly needs the Beit Din's authority. Tevilah is over in a moment, so it needs zakhin.

How can zakhin work for a child?

Zakhin is understood as a form of shelihut, agency: we assume the person would have appointed us. But a katan cannot appoint an agent, and neither can a non-Jew. So how does it work?

  • Tosafot in Ketubot answer that the conversion of a child works only rabbinically. They then ask how such a boy could marry a Jewish girl, and answer that the Hakhamim have the power to allow it. The Rabbi noted that this is a big novelty, since generally the Hakhamim can only tell us to refrain from something, not permit what the Torah forbids.
  • Tosafot in Sanhedrin reject this. Here the Beit Din is not acquiring anything on the child's behalf. The child himself acquires, in his own body, by becoming a convert and entering under the wings of the Shekhinah. The Beit Din only facilitates. This, the Rabbi said, gets the best of both worlds: it is not real zekhiyah, which avoids the problem of a katan, but it has enough of zekhiyah to explain how a child can convert at all.
  • Rashi writes that such a child may now touch wine. "You have nothing better to tell me than that he can touch wine?" asked the Rabbi. It suggests Rashi too sees it as a rabbinic conversion, and the Bah draws this out. Later authorities say that according to Rashi he could not marry a Jewish woman, and questions of yibum and halisah would follow.
  • The Ritva brings the rabbinic view and strongly rejects it, deriving from the Rambam that it is a complete conversion.

Why three? Rav Moshe Feinstein (Igrot Moshe, Yoreh Deah 1:158) first notes that both milah and tevilah need three when a child is converted this way, as is done today. Then he asks: if this is zekhiyah, one person should suffice. He goes back and forth and concludes that one would indeed suffice if we could see into the person's depths and circumstances. Since no single human can judge whether this really is a benefit, three are needed to reach that decision together.

Must a parent bring the child?

The simple reading of the Gemara is that Rav Huna's rule works even for a child who comes alone. The Tur quotes the Bahag, whose words imply that it works only with the father. The Beit Yosef explains that the Bahag means a parent, father or mother, but not a child on his own: you cannot take a child off the street. The Bah and others say the Bahag's words are not meant so precisely. The Shulhan Arukh rules that the child may be converted whether brought by the father, the mother, or coming by himself.

Asked why the Shulhan Arukh seems to rank a mother's consent with a child coming alone, the Rabbi said this follows the Gemara's structure: the Shulhan Arukh cannot be learned without the Gemara behind it.

The Beit Yosef also brings the Mordekhai: the child must ask for it. We do not go out and convert children against their will. The Ran disagrees, but the Shakh shows from the wording that the Shulhan Arukh follows the Mordekhai. "You can't just take a child off the street and say, this is good for you."

Is it a benefit if the child will not grow up observant?

A classic case: a Jewish father has a child with a non-Jewish mother who does not want to convert. He promises to make Kiddush with the child on Friday nights. Should the Beit Din convert the child?

  • Rav Kook held that conversion is normally a benefit, but not if the child will be raised in a non-religious home. Then it is a liability.
  • Rav Moshe, in the same teshuvah, acknowledges that most Jews are not Orthodox and doubts that it is a benefit, yet he writes that simply being a Jew is itself a zekhut. "That's a huge statement," said the Rabbi.

He offered a way to understand Rav Moshe. According to the Rambam and the Ramhal, the ultimate punishment, karet, means losing one's place in the World to Come, not endless torment. Gehinom is a rectification of at most twelve months. On that view, being Jewish is an opportunity that cannot make things worse, like sending a child to a far better school: he may waste it, but he is not punished for being there. Someone pointed out that this sits uneasily with Rav Moshe's own reason for three judges, and the Rabbi agreed that it seems to be a contradiction.

The practice today, in Israel and in England, is that no Beit Din would convert a child unless both parents are or will be Jewish by the end of the process, and the parents commit to basic observance.

Teenagers and other hard cases

Once a child passes bar or bat misvah, the Beit Din can no longer act on his behalf; he would have to convert as an adult. Technically that works, but the Rabbi questioned whether a fourteen-year-old should make an irreversible life decision. The London Beth Din has a policy of not accepting anyone under eighteen or still at university. His own Beit Din has no fixed rule, but he said it makes sense. In such a case the young person can learn regularly with someone and be reassessed at eighteen. The lesson: use the years before bar misvah.

One more point: a child who is a shoteh in the halachic sense, unable to respond in any way, cannot be converted even when the rest of the family converts. The Beit Din must assess this carefully.

Protest at maturity

Rav Yosef says that converted children may protest when they grow up and leave. The Gemara asks: then why give a girl converted under three a ketubah of two hundred, when she might later protest? It answers that once she has reached maturity for one moment (shaah, "a moment," not an hour) without protesting, she can no longer protest.

The Rosh and the Ran ask how this can work. The Ran's first answer: a protest made in childhood that continues into adulthood counts. The main answer, given by both: protest is possible only after reaching maturity, and only until the person does any act that expresses Jewish identity, such as modeh ani, washing the hands in the morning, or saying Shema at bedtime. After that it is too late.

He closed with a question for next week: a boy may perform shehitah before bar misvah. If at thirteen he protests, he was never Jewish, and the meat people ate for years was nevelah.

Practical summary

  • A child may be converted al daat beit din, because conversion is a benefit, and both milah and tevilah need three.
  • Whether such a conversion is rabbinic or from the Torah is disputed; Tosafot in Sanhedrin and the Ritva treat it as complete.
  • The Shulhan Arukh allows it with the father, the mother, or the child alone, but following the Mordekhai the child must want it.
  • In practice batei din today require both parents to be or become Jewish and observant.
  • After bar or bat misvah the child can protest, but only until he or she first acts as a Jew.
