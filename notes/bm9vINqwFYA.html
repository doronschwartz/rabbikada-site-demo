Topic: Why one asks Mah Nishtanah even when alone: the freedom to ask questions

Sources: The Gemara; the Torah; the Arizal

Daily Halacha: Pesah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • Maggid opens with Ha Lahma Anya as an introduction, but its heart begins with Mah Nishtanah: really one question, why is this night different, with four reasons, answered by Avadim Hayinu.
  • The Gemara says that even one sitting the Seder alone asks himself the questions. Why ask oneself, with no one to answer?
  • Questions recur with Egypt: three times the Torah speaks of a child asking about it.
  • We mark freedom from physical slavery by reclining and drinking four cups. But there is also mental freedom: a dictatorship denies free speech and the right to ask.
  • When Moshe asked for a three-day break, Pharaoh said that if they had time to think of a holiday, the work should be made harder: he sought to enslave us mentally as well.
  • So asking is not only a means to an answer but an end in itself, demonstrating intellectual freedom as reclining demonstrates physical freedom.
  • The Arizal reads Pesah as "peh sah", the mouth speaks. Genuine, appropriate questions, unlike the Rasha's cynical one, bring us closer to HaKadosh Barukh Hu, which is why the night is full of things to arouse curiosity.

Sources quoted

In one line

We ask Mah Nishtanah even alone because asking questions itself expresses the mental freedom we gained in leaving Egypt.
