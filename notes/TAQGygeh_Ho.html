Topic: The structure of Maggid: what each section of the Haggadah does and why it is there Source: The Pesah Haggadah, with the laws of sippur yesiat Misrayim Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal and on Zoom, Monday 3 April 2023, two days before Pesah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Preparing the mind, not only the house

We spend weeks preparing the house, the food and the wine for Pesah, the Rabbi said, but do we set aside time to learn what we will actually be doing? The heart of the night is the Haggadah. The previous class covered the laws of the Seder; this one went through Maggid section by section, explaining the function of each paragraph. "If you understand what we're going to talk about tonight, it will really change your experience of the Seder."

Why is there no berakhah on the Haggadah?

Telling the story is one of the two Torah misvot unique to this night, the other being massah, yet there is no berakhah over it. One reason given: it is a misvah with no fixed limit, like charity. There is a minimum, but the more you do the greater the misvah. The Shulhan Arukh says the telling should go on all night, until sleep overtakes you.

Ha Lahma Anya: the introduction

Ha Lahma Anya is not yet the Haggadah; it is an introduction, said over the broken massah from Yahas. We break the massah now, rather than at Mosi Massah, because lehem oni is "the bread over which many things are answered": the story must be told over it. The Rabbi's custom is to lift the broken massah, since that is the bread being read over. The paragraph says: this is the bread we are about to talk over; anyone who wants to join, come now; this year here and enslaved in exile, next year in the Land of Israel and free.

Why Aramaic? The rest of Maggid is in Hebrew because it quotes the Hakhamim, the Mishnah and the beraitot. This paragraph, an invitation and an announcement, was said in the language everyone understood. The Ben Ish Hai adds a Kabbalistic reason: since we say "all who are hungry, come and eat", it is in Aramaic so that the harmful forces, which do not understand Aramaic, are not invited too.

Why invite so late? Partly as a last chance. And it underlines our exile: in Temple times you could not join a Pesah group at night, since each group had to be fixed before the offering was slaughtered.

Then the plate is removed and the second cup poured, both to provoke questions, "because this is where the dialogue begins".

Part 1: the question, the answer, a story and a statement

Mah Nishtanah is one question, not four: why is this night different? It has four parts, four ways in which the night is different. The misvah unique to this night is to tell the story through question and answer, which is why the Rambam says that even someone alone asks himself. Asking questions is intellectual freedom: under tyranny you cannot express yourself.

Avadim Hayinu is the answer. In the Syrian custom the leader announces in Arabic, "this is the answer", before it. "If you had one minute on Pesah night", these two paragraphs, the question and the answer, are the essential Haggadah.

The story of the five rabbis illustrates the answer. Avadim Hayinu ends: however wise we are, we must talk about the Exodus. Really? Here is a story: five great rabbis spent the whole night discussing it until their students came to say it was time for Shema. Some note that none of them had a direct link to the slavery: Rabbi Akiva descended from converts, three were Kohanim and Rabbi Yehoshua was a Levi, and the tribe of Levi did not work in Egypt. If they spoke about it all night, how much more must we.

Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah's statement shows that there is a misvah to recall the Exodus every night, from the word "all" in "all the days of your life". If every night, how much more on Pesah night.

Part 2: the four children

Now we learn how to tell it: differently for each child. It opens with Barukh HaMakom. The Rabbi saw a beautiful idea in this: every child is a blessing, whether wise or wicked, and must be treated as your child.

  • The wise child: teach him all the laws, to the very end of Masekhet Pesahim, which ends with the afikoman.
  • The wicked child speaks of "this avodah of yours". The Rabbi's favourite explanation turns on the word avodah. The opening of Shemot uses it five times for the slavery. The wicked child sees all of Judaism as servitude: we served a master then and serve a Master now, so what are we celebrating? He does not see that service of Hashem is a different kind of avodah. The Rabbi added that he dislikes calling prayer "the service", as if we bow to a figure on a throne, when prayer is a time to meditate, connect and ask.
  • The simple child gets simple questions and simple answers.
  • The child who cannot ask is often not slow but apathetic, made to sit at the table. He must be drawn in. The Lubavitcher Rebbe used to ask: where is the fifth child, who did not come at all? You must go and find him.

The next paragraph (yakhol meRosh Hodesh) is a side note on the verse quoted for the fourth child, learning that the telling is on the night of the fifteenth.

Part 3: spiritual slavery and freedom

The Gemara records a dispute between Rav and Shemuel: does "begin with disgrace and end with praise" mean physical slavery or spiritual? We do both. Avadim Hayinu covered the physical; Mitehilah ovdei avodah zarah covers the spiritual. Our ancestors worshipped idols, and Hashem brought us close to His service. The verses quoted simply prove the point.

Part 4: the covenant that stands for us

Barukh Shomer Havtahato goes back to the promise made to Avraham at the covenant between the pieces. Hashem "calculated the end": the 400 years were counted from the birth of Yishak, so Israel was in Egypt 210 years, and the actual slavery lasted 116.

Then Vehi sheAmdah. Many children learn that the letters of vehi stand for the six orders of the Mishnah, the five books of the Torah, the Ten Commandments and the One God. "Lovely idea, but the problem is it's not what it says." Hi, "she", is the covenant (brit is feminine) we just praised Hashem for keeping. That covenant has stood for us in every generation, and the first example is Lavan, who wanted to destroy Yaakov and his family.

Part 5: four verses, dissected

Next, four verses from the declaration of the first fruits are quoted in full, and each phrase is explained with a proof text. They follow the story in order:

  1. History: "An Aramean sought to destroy my father, and he went down to Egypt": forced to go, only to sojourn, few in number, becoming a great nation.
  2. The bondage: the Egyptians did us harm, afflicted us and imposed hard labour.
  3. Its effect on us: we cried out, and Hashem saw our affliction, our toil and our oppression, the mental stress.
  4. Redemption: Hashem took us out of Egypt, Himself and not through an angel, "with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, with great awe, with signs and with wonders". The paragraph about "I will pass through Egypt" is a supporting verse showing that Hashem acted directly. "Wonders" leads straight into the ten plagues and Rabbi Yehudah's mnemonic for them.

Part 6: counting the plagues and Dayenu

Rabbi Yose HaGelili, Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva multiply the plagues: ten, forty or fifty in Egypt, and five times as many at the sea. What does it mean? How many elements do you count in each plague? Blood meant thirst, paying for water, unwashed clothes. The Rabbi suggested this as a Seder discussion: find five hardships in each plague. "This is the misvah. The discussion."

Dayenu follows: each of the fifteen steps alone would have been enough, and how much more must we be grateful for all of them together.

Part 7: Pesah, Massah and Maror, and seeing yourself

Rabban Gamliel says that whoever has not explained these three has not fulfilled his obligation: the Pesah offering because Hashem passed over our houses, massah because there was no time for the dough to rise, and maror because they embittered our lives. The leader must explain them.

Bekhol dor vador caps it all. The Rambam's text, followed by Sefaradim, says one must show oneself (leharot) as if one left Egypt: demonstrate it, act it out, and hopefully feel it. Other texts read lirot, to see oneself, an inward feeling. "Stick to your custom."

From here, Hallel and the berakhah over the second cup are no longer the telling but prayer, which is why many communities change to a different tune.

Practical summary

  • Learn the structure before the Seder so you can explain each section as you reach it.
  • Ha Lahma Anya is the introduction, said over the broken massah; remove the plate and pour the second cup to prompt questions.
  • Mah Nishtanah and Avadim Hayinu, the one question and its answer, are the core; tell the story through questions.
  • Teach each child in his own way, and go and find the child who is not at the table.
  • Use the midrashim, the verses and the plagues as springboards for discussion, not a text to rush through.
  • Explain Pesah, Massah and Maror, and try to show and feel that you yourself left Egypt.
