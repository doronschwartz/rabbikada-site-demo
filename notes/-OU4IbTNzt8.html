Recap

Chazal restricted medicine on Shabbat because remedies once required grinding herbs. Pikuach nefesh is different — a mitzvah overriding Shabbat, better done by a Jew than delegated to a non-Jew.

Part A — The Five Levels of Illness

Level

Description

What is permitted

1. Meichush be'alma — mild discomfort

Slight headache, sore throat, chapped hands — you carry on as normal

No medicine at all (no paracetamol; no rubbing oil into chapped hands)

2. Choleh be'ever echad — pain in one limb

Earache, heartburn, a headache you can still work through

A Jew may not medicate; you may ask a non-Jew to do something rabbinic (e.g. remove a mostly-detached painful nail; apply Vaseline)

3. Choleh kol gufo — whole-body illness

Flu; or one-limb pain so severe you can't function (bad migraine, bedridden toothache or stomach ache) — you wouldn't go to work

Medicine permitted; a non-Jew may do anything, even Torah-level, asked outright; a Jew may do rabbinic acts (preferably with a shinui) — and, per Chacham Ovadia, even a Torah-level act with a shinui if no non-Jew is available

4. Sakanat ever — risk to a limb

e.g. a severed finger

Today treated as pikuach nefesh — everything permitted

5. Pikuach nefesh

Any danger to life

Everything permitted — an obligation

Paradoxically, a slight ailment (Level 1) is treated more strictly than a worse one.

Level 3 examples

  • A non-Jewish helper may make you tea, or go to the chemist for medicine.
  • Alone and bedridden with flu late on Friday night, needing a hot bath: try to find a non-Jew; failing that, turn the hot tap on with an elbow or teeth (a shinui). (Q: may you close it afterwards? — a good question; the "drive home" leniency is for pikuach nefesh, and may not extend here.)
  • Lying with a migraine and the light on: ask a non-Jew; otherwise switch it off with an elbow. An LED is only rabbinic anyway; even a filament bulb may be switched off with a shinui in this situation.
  • Frail elderly people and young children (up to about 6–7, when they no longer need their parents for small things) automatically have Level 3 status. Q (elderly with ongoing skin needs) — not a blanket rule; it depends on the actual need.

Leniencies for Levels 1–2

  • Derech bri'im — what healthy people normally do isn't "medicine": prune juice for constipation, bananas for diarrhoea, a shot of whisky or vodka for a mild toothache (but not gargling — that's medicinal).
  • A course of treatment begun before Shabbat (antibiotics, a regular hand cream) may be continued — the decree targets someone who discovers a problem on Shabbat and anxiously improvises.

Exercise and massage

Exercising or a massage in order to sweat (perspiration was a recognised therapy) is forbidden. Exercise or massage for enjoyment or routine (e.g. a strict fitness programme) — strictly permitted, though hardly in the spirit of Shabbat (and you'll want a shower afterwards — cold only).

Creams and oils — memare'ach

Smoothing is forbidden (hides were smoothed in the Mishkan) only where a smooth layer remains — like wax smeared to seal a barrel.

  • Runny oil — no smoothing issue at all.
  • Cream — rub in a little at a time until fully absorbed, leaving no layer (rabbinic if a layer remains).
  • Thick ointment (Vaseline) spread as a layer — forbidden.
  • Separately, medicinal use must be permitted: sunscreen before sun exposure is prevention — fine (rubbed in); after-sun cream for a mild burn (Level 1–2) — not.

Thermometers

Measuring is forbidden on Shabbat (linked to writing — one usually records measurements). For a Level 3 patient — permitted; Level 2 — ask a non-Jew. If there is any chance of danger, take the temperature.

Part B — Muktseh

What it means

Muktseh (from katzeh, "set aside") — something not designated for use on Shabbat. A rabbinic decree against moving it — not against touching it. A heavy object (a car) may be touched; a light one that would move when touched may not.

Four reasons

  1. Ra'avad: limits carrying, as a fence around the prohibition of carrying.
  2. Rambam (a): a general fence — pens lead to writing, scissors to cutting, scales to measuring.
  3. Rambam (b): to keep Shabbat from becoming mundane — just as we walk and speak differently on Shabbat (no business or politics), we handle different things.
  4. Rambam (c): so that those who don't work during the week (people of leisure) also feel Shabbat is different.

The seven categories

Category

Examples

May it be moved?

1. Kli shemelachto le'issur — a tool whose main use is forbidden

Hammer, pen, sieve, empty pot or frying pan, a fan

Yes — for a permitted use (letzorech gufo: hammer to crack a nut, empty pan to hold apples) or for its place (letzorech mekomo: a pen on your bed). Not to protect it.

2. Muktseh machmat chesron kis — valuable forbidden-use items

A mohel's or shochet's knife; computer, washing machine; probably an expensive smartphone

No — not even for its place or use

3. Muktseh machmat gufo — not a utensil, no use

Stones, sand, money; raw flour, raw potatoes, raw meat or fish

No (unless designated before Shabbat, e.g. sand set aside for children to play with)

4. Muktseh machmat issur

Fruit that fell from a tree on Shabbat

No — lest one pick more

5. Muktseh machmat mitzvah

A lulav on Shabbat of Sukkot (also hadassim)

No — but an etrog may be moved and smelled

6. Nolad — came into being on Shabbat

An egg laid on Shabbat; milk a non-Jew milked on Shabbat

No

7. Basis — a base for muktseh

A table holding muktseh at the start of Shabbat

No

Notes on the categories

  • The status is fixed when Shabbat begins. A pot with food in it isn't muktseh and stays permitted after it's emptied; a pan empty at the start of Shabbat is muktseh all Shabbat.
  • Once lifted permissibly, you may carry it on and put it away — you needn't drop it immediately.
  • Garden tools left out in the rain: you may not bring them in to protect them. You may, however, genuinely decide to use the garden space (e.g. for a game), or use a hammer to crack a coconut, and move them in the process.
  • A fan may be repositioned (as long as there's no risk of unplugging it), or turned away if the draught bothers you.
  • A phone left ringing as an alarm: an old basic phone is a kli shemelachto le'issur — move it out of the room for quiet. An expensive smartphone is likely chesron kis. Value is judged objectively, not by sentimental attachment, and may change over time.
  • Paintings and picture frames: Ashkenazim treat valuables never moved (e.g. paintings) as muktseh. For Sephardim, chesron kis applies only to items whose use is forbidden — so a painting, a framed picture or a vase may be moved (not electrical ones).

Two ways to move any muktseh

  1. Tiltul min hatzad — moving it indirectly via something else (lift the sheet and shake the camera off your bed) — only for the sake of a permitted thing, not to protect the muktseh item.
  2. Tiltul begufo — with your body in an unusual way: kick it, blow it, push it with an elbow — permitted even to protect it (e.g. kick a banknote under the bed).

Q: Someone left an iPhone on your place in shul? — Move it with your elbows, or tilt the table it sits on (min hatzad, for your space). Asking a non-Jew — only where there's a mitzvah need (e.g. nowhere else to sit). Asking a non-Jew to do a Torah act with a shinui, or a non-Jew to ask another non-Jew — big questions for the final shiur.

Summary

  • Five levels: 1 — no medicine; 2 — a non-Jew for rabbinic acts; 3 — medicine permitted, a non-Jew for anything, a Jew with a shinui; 4–5 — everything.
  • Derech bri'im foods and courses begun before Shabbat are permitted. Exercise only for enjoyment. Rub creams in fully; no thick layers. Thermometers only for Level 3 (or via a non-Jew at Level 2).
  • Muktseh is about moving, not touching. Tools with forbidden uses may be moved for a permitted use or their place; valuables, stones, raw food, nolad and bases may not — except indirectly for permitted needs, or with your body.
