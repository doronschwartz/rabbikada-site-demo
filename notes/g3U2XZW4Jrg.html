Topic: Hanukkah and the battle of beauty between Shem and Yefet, Yosef and Egypt, Shimon HaSaddik and Alexander Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal (on Zoom), Tuesday 8 December 2020

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A puzzle in the Shulhan Arukh

The Rabbi opened with a halachic anomaly. Strictly speaking, the misvah of Hanukkah is fulfilled with one light each night. Yet when the Shulhan Arukh codifies the law, he does not write the basic misvah at all. He writes only the practice of adding a light each night. All the commentators ask why he left out the main halachah. To understand this, he said, we have to go back to the beginning.

The starting point is a verse in Zekharyah (9:13): "I will rouse your children, Zion, against your children, Yavan." The prophecy is that the Jewish people will triumph over Greece. But why does the verse call us Zion, and not Israel or Yehudah? That question, Greece versus Zion, was the theme of the evening.

Three meetings between beauty and something deeper

Shimon HaSaddik and Alexander. The Gemara in Yoma tells that Alexander of Macedon came to destroy the Beit HaMikdash, about a hundred years before the Hanukkah story. Shimon HaSaddik, the Kohen Gadol, put on the priestly garments and went out with the nobles of Israel carrying torches. When Alexander saw him he came down from his chariot and bowed. His escort asked why a great king bows to a Jew. He answered that the image of this man's face goes before him in victory on his battlefields. The commentators ask how the Kohen Gadol could leave the Mikdash in the eight garments, which may only be worn inside. We will see why, the Rabbi said.

Yosef and the wife of Potiphar. In that week's parashah, Vayeshev, Yosef is described as beautiful in form and appearance. The wife of Potiphar tries to seduce him, grabs his garment, and he flees, leaving it in her hand. Yirmiyahu describes Egypt itself as "a beautiful heifer." So here a beautiful Yosef meets Egypt, the land of beauty.

Shem and Yefet. When Noah lay uncovered, Ham saw and went to tell his brothers. The Rabbi noted that the letters of Ham reversed spell moah, brain: a backwards mind that sees things only in order to tell others, never to understand them. Then "Shem and Yefet took the garment" and covered their father. The verb is singular, vayikah, and Rashi explains that Shem exerted himself more in the misvah. Therefore his descendants merited the tallit with sisit, and Yefet's descendants merited burial. We trace ourselves to Shem; the Greeks trace themselves to Yefet, whose name means beauty.

Two kinds of light

To understand these rewards, the Rabbi went back to the first day of creation. "Let there be light," yet the sun was only made on the fourth day. The well-known answer is that there are two lights. The sunlight of the fourth day lets us see things. The light of the first day, the or haganuz, is insight. It was not destroyed but put away, hidden, still there but harder to reach.

Hazal say Adam used this light when he named the animals, a light by which one could see "from one end of the world to the other." That cannot mean seeing far, since naming animals does not need it. It means seeing into the essence of something. The Rabbi explained that Hebrew names are not merely descriptive. In English a chair is called a chair by convention. In the holy tongue the name expresses, and even causes, the essence of the thing. To name the aryeh, the lion, Adam had to understand what a lion is.

So Shem, whose name means "name," saw beyond what the eye sees. Yefet, "beauty," saw only the outside. Both covered their father, but for different reasons. Shem understood that the body clothes a soul and deserves dignity as its vehicle. Yefet covered the body because something beautiful should not be on display. That is why the reward differs. Sisit, with its blue thread, is meant to remind us of the sea, then the sky, then Hashem: it trains us to look at something and trace it back to its source. Burial honours the body, which for Yefet is the whole story.

Beauty that tells the truth

Every Friday night we say, "Charm is false and beauty is vain; a woman who fears Hashem, she shall be praised." But the Torah praises the beauty of Sarah, Rivkah, Rahel, Yosef and Esther. One commentator reads the verse differently: charm and beauty by themselves are false, but when there is a God-fearing person, then that beauty is to be praised. Outward beauty is praiseworthy when it reflects the inner person.

He gave an analogy. A beautifully wrapped present arrives, with ribbons and fine paper. You open it and find your pizza for supper. Not only is the pizza disappointing; now the ribbons themselves look like a lie, and you throw them away. Shlomo HaMelekh says the same: "A gold ring in a pig's snout is a beautiful woman without sense" (Mishlei 11:22). Even the gold loses its beauty. As the Rabbi put it, "a panim without a panim is not a panim": the outer face (panim) must reflect what is inside (penim).

This, he said, is what Yosef told the wife of Potiphar. All you want is my garment, my outer beauty. That is not who I am. So he left the garment with her. And it is no accident that the misvah of beautifying misvot, "zeh Eli veanvehu," was given at the Sea, just as the people left Egypt, the land of beauty without depth. Beauty is to be harnessed so that it points to something beyond itself.

Shimon HaSaddik, Zion and Yavan

Now the story of Alexander makes sense. The priestly garments were made "for glory and for splendour." The Kohen Gadol had to be handsome and dressed beautifully. Shimon HaSaddik went out to the Greek king with that beauty on display, and Alexander saw that behind it stood a person of depth. Greek culture glorified the body and outer refinement. Here was the same beauty, but pointing to something deeper, and Alexander bowed.

The Rabbi then turned to Zion. Hazal say that whatever happened to Yosef happened to Zion, and the numerical value of Yosef and Siyon is the same, 156. Zion is always connected with beauty in Tehillim: "beautiful in elevation, the joy of the whole earth," "out of Zion, perfect in beauty." A siyun in Hebrew is a signpost. A signpost that points to nothing is a lie. Zion's outer beauty is a signpost to the inner beauty of Jerusalem, the Beit HaMikdash.

Finally, look at the letters: Saddik, Yod, Vav, Nun. Inside Siyon is Yavan, Greece, with a Saddik in front of it. Zion is the beauty of Yavan with the saddik, with the inner dimension. That is why it is specifically Zion that triumphs over Yavan. "We do not negate the world," he said, and this ties in with the Rambam they had been learning. We do not fight Greece by saying beauty is unimportant. We answer with our own beauty, which reflects something deeper.

Back to Hanukkah

All too often we live like Greeks, seeing only the outside of things. Over the eight nights we light 36 lights in total. Hazal connect these with the 36 hours that Adam HaRishon enjoyed the hidden light: created on Friday morning, he had it for twelve hours, and even after the sin it stayed with him through Shabbat. On Hanukkah that light shines again, and we can reach it, because Hanukkah is about seeing beyond what meets the eye. That, he said, is our avodah over the festival. The class ended with good wishes for Hanukkah and a note that the regular Rambam class on Hilchot Deot would resume in January.

Summary

  • The verse sets Zion against Yavan, and the question is why Zion.
  • Shem saw the soul within the body; Yefet saw only outer beauty. Hence sisit for one and burial for the other.
  • Beauty is praised only when it reflects an inner reality; otherwise it is "a gold ring in a pig's snout."
  • Yosef left his garment with the wife of Potiphar, and Shimon HaSaddik wore his to meet Alexander: the same battle each time.
  • Siyon is Yavan with a Saddik. The 36 lights of Hanukkah reconnect us with the hidden light of insight.
