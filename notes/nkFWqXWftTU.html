Where 613 comes from

This was the third of a series for the month of May, after shiurim on living in the Land of Israel and on minhag, and before one on Ruth and conversion. In preparation for Shavuot, the Rabbi asked three questions: where does the number 613 come from, is it exact, and how do we count it?

The source is the last page of Makkot. Rabbi Simlai, in the third century, taught that 613 misvot were told to Moshe: 365 prohibitions, like the days of the solar year, and 248 positive commands, like the limbs of the body. (He noted the popular version, "248 limbs and 365 sinews", is not what the Gemara says.) The Gemara finds a hint in the verse "Torah tsivah lanu Moshe", "Moshe commanded us the Torah": the numerical value of Torah is 611, and the first two of the Ten Commandments we heard directly from God, making 613.

The idea grew over the centuries. The name Rut has the value 606; add the seven Noahide laws a non-Jew already keeps, and you have 613, fitting for the great convert. And Yitro, another convert, has the same letters as Rut with an added yud.

Exact, or a figure of speech?

Most Rishonim took 613 as exact. The Rambam explains it: every limb tells a person to do God's will, and every day of the year tells him not to go against it. The Geonim, including Saadiah Gaon and the Baal Halachot Gedolot, counted lists, and by the eleventh century poets were turning the list into song. The best known is the Azharot of Rabbi Shelomo ibn Gabirol, whose acrostic spells his name, and which Sefaradim read on Shavuot.

But some voices doubted it:

  • The Ramban was unsure whether this is an accepted tradition or Rabbi Simlai's own idea. All through the Gemara there are debates whether something is a misvah at all, such as warning a suspected wife, and nobody ever asks how the other side makes up the number.
  • The Rashbats, Rabbi Shimon ben Semah Duran, suggested that 613 may follow Rabbi Simlai's own understanding; since no other Sage counted, we kept his number, and if the true count is a little more or less, we are not particular.
  • The Ralbag felt the Rambam put himself under needless pressure: 613 is a beautiful, inspiring idea, like a rabbi's sermon, not a precise figure.

The Rabbi stressed that these are minority views.

The Rambam's complaint

The Gemara gives no list, so every counter had to build one. The first was the Baal Halachot Gedolot in the eighth century, and nearly everyone after him followed his method. This frustrated the Rambam. In the introduction to his Sefer HaMisvot he wrote that he had long been troubled by the mistakes of those who count, who seem frozen by one man's list. The poets he did not blame: "they were poets, not rabbis", and cared more about the sweetness of the verse than its content. But people in his day judged a statement by whether it agreed with those before, not by its content. So he would give his proofs, and fourteen principles, before counting a single misvah.

The Rabbi called the result beautiful: a system applied consistently that arrives at exactly 613. He mentioned the suggestion that the Sefer HaMisvot is a fine thing to learn on Shavuot night, and noted that the Rambam's Mishneh Torah opens each section with the misvot it covers.

The fourteen principles

  1. No rabbinic misvot. Hanukkah candles and Megillah were counted by some, and appear in the Azharot. "How can we say something rabbinic is included" in what was given at Sinai?
  2. Nothing derived by the thirteen rules of interpretation. Honouring an older brother or a stepmother is derived from an extra word in "honour your father and your mother". It is halacha, but not one of the 613. Otherwise the count would run to thousands: the Gemara says 1,700 such derivations were forgotten in the mourning for Moshe.
  3. Only misvot for all generations, because the verse calls the Torah "an inheritance". Not leaving over manna, or keeping the flocks back from Sinai, applied only then. Sacrifices still count: we cannot bring them only because we lack the Temple.
  4. No general commands. His parable: a parent tells the children no parties, don't open the door to strangers, lights out at ten, and then "do everything I told you". That is three commands, not four. So "be holy" is not a separate misvah.
  5. No reasons as misvot. "Do not bring sin upon the land" explains the ban on remarrying a divorced wife after she married another; it is not a second misvah.
  6. Count both the positive and the negative, when the Torah gives both: resting on Shabbat and not doing work; afflicting oneself on Yom Kippur and not eating.
  7. No details as misvot. Yibbum with all its laws is one misvah; the sliding-scale sin offering, animal, bird or flour, is one; the many blemishes that disqualify an offering are one.
  8. A negation of a positive is not a prohibition. "She shall not go out as the male servants do" simply limits the earlier law.
  9. Count the subject, not the repetitions. Shabbat appears many times, the ban on blood seven. Eating an insect earns five sets of lashes, because it is forbidden five times, yet it is one misvah. The Gemara says the Torah warns 36 times against wronging a convert; counting each would make some 6% of the Torah about converts.
  10. No preparations. Taking flour and baking the showbread are steps towards placing it on the table; pressing oil prepares for lighting the menorah.
  11. No parts of one misvah. The four species are one misvah, as someone in the class suggested.
  12. No parts of one task. The menorah, table and altar all fall under "make Me a sanctuary". He said he understood 11 and 12 as very similar.
  13. No counting by days. Seven days in the sukkah are one misvah; so are the daily offering and the Musaf of Rosh Hodesh. Yet the ban on work on each festival is counted separately, because the Torah gives it for each.
  14. Punishments carried out by the Beit Din are counted, but not those, like karet, that are in God's hands.

A challenge for Shavuot

The Rabbi set a challenge: while reading the Azharot on Shavuot, find the places where the poem breaks the Rambam's principles. "You will find quite a lot." Still, reading them is our custom and we should keep it: just do not derive laws from a poem. Better still, he said, use the long weekend of Shavuot to go through the Rambam's Sefer HaMisvot, available in English. "It's really liberating."

Asked whether the body really has 248 limbs, he said that if science says otherwise, it is probably meant as derash. Asked about Sefirat HaOmer, he said it is debated whether it is one misvah or forty-nine.

Summary

  • The number 613 comes from Rabbi Simlai in Makkot: 248 positive commands like the limbs, 365 prohibitions like the days of the year.
  • Most Rishonim took it as exact; the Ramban, the Rashbats and the Ralbag doubted that it is precise.
  • The Rambam, frustrated with earlier lists and poems, gave fourteen principles and counted exactly 613 by them.
  • The Azharot are a beloved custom, but they are poetry, not a source of halacha.
