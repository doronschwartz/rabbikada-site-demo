Topic: Why converting someone is a misvah, and the two physical requirements of conversion: milah and tevilah

Source: Gemara Yevamot 46-47; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268

Shiur: Brit Milah and Tevilah, the first class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, on Zoom, Wednesday 21 April 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why every rabbi needs to know this

The Rabbi opened by explaining why a course on conversion matters even for someone who will never sit on a Beit Din. In places such as America, community rabbis deal with conversions regularly, and in any community people walk in asking whether and how they can become Jewish, so a rabbi must know the basic halachot of gerut well enough to recognise a problem.

The course would run for nine sessions, combining halacha with the common practice of batei din today, which he deals with every week. The questions ahead included what to do when a candidate cannot have a brit milah; how a convert can be accepted when she will not cover her hair; whether we still discourage converts, and how a rabbi should respond when someone enquires; who must be present and what training they need; Reform and Conservative conversions; conversion for marriage; converting children and pregnant women; how long a conversion takes in England; whether to redo a conversion done elsewhere; and the Syrian community's practice of not accepting converts. He also promised a Sepharadi angle throughout, noting that Sepharadi poskim were, generally speaking, much more lenient on conversion.

Converting someone is a misvah

The central sugya of Hilchot Gerim is in Yevamot 46-47. When a candidate comes, we tell him of the hardships of the Jewish people. If he says "I know, and still I want to," we accept him and circumcise him immediately. Why the rush? Because one does not delay a misvah. So the Gemara states plainly that converting a sincere non-Jew is a misvah.

Which misvah? Two explanations in the Rishonim:

  • The Rambam: it is part of loving Hashem, "veahavta et Hashem," which means making Him beloved to people by showing them the beauty of Torah.
  • The Semag: it is the misvah to love the ger. The Rabbi noted that this is surprising, since one loves a ger once he is a ger, not in order to make him one.

Either way, he said, a rabbi who says "I do not get involved in conversions" may not have the right approach. Anyone who teaches sincere converts or performs their conversion, with the correct criteria, is doing a misvah.

Two essential requirements

The Gemara (Yevamot 46) brings three views. Rabbi Eliezer holds that milah alone is enough, as our ancestors in the wilderness were circumcised. Rabbi Yehoshua holds that tevilah alone is enough, as the mothers immersed. The Hakhamim say a person is not a ger until he has both milah and tevilah, and the halacha follows them. A third requirement, kabbalat misvot, was left for the next class. Someone asked about the korban a convert brings; the Rabbi agreed that it applies, but only when the Mikdash is rebuilt.

The order. The milah comes first and then the tevilah. The Ramban asks why. Since the wound must heal before immersion, doing tevilah first would make the person Jewish sooner. His answer is that the milah is a last test: if the candidate sees what he must go through and still wants to continue, he is committed. If tevilah was done first, the Ramban holds it is valid and the Nimukei Yoseph holds it is not. The Rema brings both, and the Shakh rules that the tevilah must be repeated after the milah.

A candidate who is already circumcised

Many male candidates today are already circumcised, so the Beit Din usually needs only hatafat dam berit, drawing a drop of blood. This seems simple, but it is a dispute of the Rishonim, based on the Gemara in Shabbat (135a):

  • Rabbenu Hananel: a non-Jew who was circumcised has no remedy and cannot convert. The Bayit Hadash limits this to someone born uncircumcised who was later circumcised, not to someone born without a foreskin.
  • The Rif, the Rambam, the Rosh and others: hatafat dam berit is always enough.
  • The Baal HaItur: someone born circumcised needs nothing at all, only tevilah.

The Shulhan Arukh rules that hatafat dam berit is done. The Rambam requires it only out of doubt, since perhaps the earlier circumcision is enough. That is why no berakhah is said over it, a point that comes back later in the class.

Someone asked whether, according to Rabbenu Hananel, Muslims, who are circumcised as descendants of Avraham, could ever convert. The Rabbi said he did not know: one could argue that their circumcision is already part of the covenant of Avraham.

In practice. Near the end of the process the Beit Din asks the candidate whether he is circumcised and relies on his answer. A full brit milah or hatafat dam berit is then done in the presence of three, and only at the end of the process, so as not to put a candidate through it before he is ready and so that it is truly done for the sake of conversion. Someone asked whether a candidate could have a medical circumcision early on, under anaesthetic. The Rabbi explained that what counts is that the milah is done leshem gerut before three. He would not advise an early medical circumcision: it is better to do a full brit milah for the sake of conversion than to bring oneself into a dispute, and anaesthetic can be used either way.

A missing organ. If a man has lost the organ, by accident or illness, the Shulhan Arukh rules that he is like a woman and needs only tevilah.

When milah is medically impossible

What if the organ is there but a doctor says a brit milah would endanger the candidate's life? Rav Kook (Daat Kohen 150) begins on practical grounds: if a doctor's letter were enough, the requirement would be uprooted. He then gives a reason. A man who cannot be circumcised is still an arel, and is not like a woman. The principle, the Rabbi explained, appears throughout halacha: being unable to do something (ones) exempts you from blame, but it cannot give you a status you do not have.

Rav Yehiel Yaakov Weinberg, the Seridei Esh, goes further. Even if the candidate wants to take the risk and is circumcised without harm, he should not be accepted, so as not to set a dangerous precedent. Such a person must accept that Hashem wants him to be a Ben Noah. The consensus of the modern poskim, the Rabbi summarised, is not to accept such a convert.

A ger who was never given hatafat dam berit

Rav Moshe Feinstein was asked about a convert who disappeared without giving his wife a get, and it emerged that the Beit Din had never done hatafat dam berit. The Rabbi asked the class whether she needed a get. Since the Rambam holds that hatafat dam berit is needed only out of doubt, he may be Jewish, and on that ground alone she could not be released. But Rav Moshe added two points. A Beit Din that made such a mistake raises questions about whether it was a fitting Beit Din, just as a get with several small mistakes makes us suspect a mistaken Beit Din. And this man was known never to have kept the misvot from the start, which shows he never truly accepted them. With these doubts together, a sefek sefeika, Rav Moshe allowed her to remarry without a get.

Between milah and tevilah

Almost every male convert spends a week or more between milah and tevilah. The Sis Eliezer (Rav Waldenberg) discusses his status. He is "a creation of his own," neither Jewish nor non-Jewish. He should not break Shabbat, because having entered one covenant he cannot break another. He cannot yet marry, and he does not make wine yayin nesekh. So the Beit Din tells candidates after the milah that they are "half Jewish" and must now be fully careful, and tries to keep the gap short, scheduling the milah only when the tevilah can follow within a week or two.

Preparing for the mikveh

Like any woman going to the mikveh, a male convert must remove every hasisah: cut and clean his nails, wash and comb his hair. The Shulhan Arukh adds that some say he shaves his hair, and the Shakh infers, comparing it to the Leviim who were shaved before their service, that this applies even when there is no hasisah. Yet, the Rabbi said, no Beit Din in England does this. When he learned this a few years ago he spoke to his Beit Din colleague Dayan Livnat, who was also puzzled by it and asked around in Israel, and was told that some Israeli batei din do shave. He found one explanation, in Betselel Hokhmah by Rav Betsalel Stern of Melbourne: the shaving is needed only for someone leaving another religion, such as a devout Christian or a priest, and not for someone with no real religious life. Someone asked what would then be done for a devout woman. The Rabbi said this has not yet come up, and it is something to think about.

Practical summary

  • Converting a sincere candidate is a misvah: loving Hashem according to the Rambam, loving the ger according to the Semag.
  • A convert needs both milah and tevilah (and kabbalat misvot, the subject of the next class). The milah comes first; if tevilah came first, it is repeated.
  • A candidate who is already circumcised needs hatafat dam berit before three, with no berakhah.
  • A man who has lost the organ needs only tevilah. One who cannot be circumcised for medical reasons is, by the consensus of modern poskim, not accepted.
  • Between milah and tevilah he must already be careful with Shabbat, and the Beit Din keeps that gap short.
