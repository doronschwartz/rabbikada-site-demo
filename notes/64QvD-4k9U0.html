Series Overview

New Monday night series — follows three completed sefarim over ~6–7 years:

  1. Derech Hashem (Ramchal)
  2. Da'at Tevunot (Ramchal)
  3. Emunot VeDe'ot (Rav Sa'adia Gaon) — the first systematic theology of Torah; studied by the Rambam who elaborated on many of Sa'adia's ideas

Parallel learning: Tuesday nights approaching the end of Rambam's Hakdama to Perek Chelek, after which the shiur will begin Moreh Nevuchim — so both sefarim will run in parallel.

Background: The Rambam

  • Born: Nissan, 1135 (main view) or 1138, in Cordoba
  • Died: Tevet, December 1204, in Fustat (Old Cairo)
  • Lived approximately 69 years
  • To the best of our knowledge, his only child was Rabbi Avraham (born when Rambam was ~51 years old)

Why the Name "Avraham"?

From a letter Rambam wrote to Rabbi Yosef ben Yehuda (the dedicatee of the Moreh Nevuchim):

"My son Avraham — Hashem gave him grace and blessing from the blessing of the one he is called after" — i.e., Avraham Avinu.

Rambam describes his son as:

  • Extremely humble (ענו ושפל שבאנשים)
  • Of great character and traits (טוב מידותיו)
  • Possessing a fine intellect and good nature (בעל שכל דק וטבע נאה)
  • Destined for fame: "יהיה לו בעזרת השם שם בגדולים בלא ספק"

Dating the letter: Likely written in 1201 (Rabbi Avraham aged ~15), a few years before Rambam's death in 1204.

Biography of Rabbi Avraham ben HaRambam

Key Dates

  • Born: Sivan (June) 1186, Fustat, Egypt
  • Died: 1237, aged 51
  • Father died when he was only 18–19 years old

Torah Learning

  • Learned most of his Torah — both halacha and machshava — directly from his father
  • There were things Rambam told him privately that he would not record in writing
  • Example: On the pasuk "עין תחת עין" — Rabbi Avraham says he received something from his father but cannot disclose it
  • Rambam himself in Moreh Nevuchim III:41 writes: "I have my opinion about this law which I'll only express verbally" (עם זאת יש לי דעה גם לגבי הלכה זו שתשמע על פה)

Public Life & Communal Role

  • Became Nagid (head of the Jewish community / representative to the government) at age 19
  • The position of Nagid continued in his family for ~150 years through to his great-great-grandson Rabbi Avraham Maimoni
  • Doctor like his father; manager of a hospital in Cairo
  • Traveled regularly between Fustat (Old Cairo) and New Cairo for hospital duties
  • Invoked Shir HaShirim: "כרמי שלי לא נטרתי" — lamenting he couldn't attend to his own development due to communal responsibilities

Fame & Recognition

  • At age 25 (1211), a group of Baalei Tosafot from France on their way to Eretz Yisrael stopped in Fustat specifically to meet him
  • Titles given to him:
  • "Young in years but great in wisdom" — Rabbi Yehuda Al-Harizi
  • חסיד — Maharam Alshakar (15th century Egypt)
  • נשיא אלוקים בתוכנו — "The Prince of God is in our midst"
  • שכינה שרויה בנינו — "The Shechina dwells among us through him"
  • ראש כל החסידים — "Head of all the pious"

Rabbi Avraham's Unique Approach

Personal & Experiential vs. Intellectual

  • Rambam emphasised the intellectual understanding of Hashem
  • Rabbi Avraham emphasised a personal, experiential encounter with Hashem
  • These complement each other — as Rambam himself writes (Moreh III:51): after intellectual understanding of God, the goal is constant focus and standing before Hashem — "וזהו עבודת השם המיוחדת"

The Question of Abstinence (Perishut)

Rabbi Avraham adopted practices of chasidut/perishut including:

  • Waking at night for prayer/meditation
  • Extensive bowing and prostration during prayer
  • Wearing uncomfortable clothing (e.g., coarse wool)
  • Various forms of self-denial

The apparent contradiction with Rambam: In Shemoneh Perakim (Ch. 4), Rambam explicitly criticises such practices:

"כסילים חסידים עשו אלה הפעולות... יענו את גופותם בכל מיני ענוי" — Foolish pious people afflict their bodies... these are bad things.

Resolution: Rambam himself acknowledges (in the same chapter) that certain Chassidim did these things as a form of healing — temporary measures to correct imbalance, like bitter medicine for illness. Rabbi Avraham may have seen his generation as needing this corrective.

Relationship with Sufism

  • Rabbi Avraham openly acknowledged learning from Sufi practices of abstinence and purity
  • His principle: He only adopted practices for which he could find evidence that the Nevi'im or Chassidim HaRishonim had engaged in them
  • This was not "influence" but reawakening lost Jewish practices that Muslims had originally received from us
  • What he rejected: e.g., the Sufi practice of repeating God's name many times — he found no Jewish source for this
  • He received criticism for some of these innovations (e.g., additional bowings in prayer)

His Father-in-Law's Influence

  • Married the daughter of Rabbi Chananel ben Shmuel HaDayan, himself a Dayan in Fustat and known as a Chassid — possibly where Rabbi Avraham developed his orientation toward chasidut

The Book: HaMaspik Le'Ovdei Hashem

Title

  • Arabic: כפאיה אלעאבדין — Kifayat Al-Abidin
  • Hebrew (by Rabbi Avraham himself): אומספיק לעובדי השם — with the "ו" prefix suggesting "a sufficient guide" rather than "the definitive guide"
  • Feldheim English title: "The Guide to Serving God" — but this loses the word "maspik" (sufficient/enough)
  • Better translation: "A Sufficient Guide to Serving God"

Concept: Just as Rambam wrote the Mishneh Torah as the sufficient guide for halacha, Rabbi Avraham wrote this as the sufficient guide for avodat Hashem in practice.

What we have and will be studying

The first three parts were practical halacha interweaved with musar — a very practical guide (like modern "practical laws of Shabbat" etc.), in contrast to his father's Mishneh Torah which codified the entire Torah including non-applicable laws.

Completion & Spread

  • Completed: 1230 (a few years before his death in 1237)
  • Spread to: Egypt, Syria, Lebanon, Safed, Algeria, and even France (Rabbi Aharon of Lunel mentions it)
  • Quoted by: Maharam Alshakar, the Radbaz (Rabbi David ben Zimra), and others

Translation History

  • 1927: First English translation by Samuel Rosenblatt (from Arabic to English — same translator as Emunot VeDe'ot)
  • 1965: First Hebrew translation by Rabbi Solomon Sassoon (grandson of Flora Sassoon), produced in honour of the wedding of his niece Mozelle to Rabbi Abraham Gabbay (later rabbi of Ohel David, Golders Green)
  • The 1965 Hebrew text is the basis of the Feldheim edition used today

Practical Notes

  • The sefer is not available on Sefaria
  • Available editions: Feldheim — green (Hebrew), purple (English)
  • Estimated study duration: approximately one year

Key Themes to Watch For

  1. The relationship between intellectual understanding and experiential service of God
  2. Rabbi Avraham's concept of chasidut and what it means in his context
  3. The tension/resolution between abstinence and Rambam's golden mean
  4. Insights into the Rambam himself through his son's writings
  5. The interplay between Jewish tradition and external practices (Sufi influence)
