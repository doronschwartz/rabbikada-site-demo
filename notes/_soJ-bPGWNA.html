Where we are

This class finished Siman 13, the decree of Hazal that a woman may not remarry while she is pregnant or nursing a child (meuberet umeneket haverah). After a quick review of the Gemara and the Rishonim from the last two weeks, the Rabbi read the Shulhan Arukh and the Rema and went through the main commentators.

An important point he stressed at the start: the decree of Hazal was only that a meuberet umeneket haverah may not marry. How long the wait lasts (15, 18, 21 or 24 months) was a later argument among the Tannaim and Rishonim. We rule 24 months.

1. The wait of 24 months

The Shulhan Arukh says that Hazal decreed that a man may not marry, nor do kiddushin with, a woman who is pregnant or nursing, until the child is 24 months old. These are 24 regular months, not counting the day of birth, even in a leap year. The Shulhan Arukh and the Rema both reject the 30-day count and the secular count (two of the four ways of counting from the review).

  • The Shulhan Arukh rules 24 months whether or not it is a leap year.
  • The Rema rules like the Maharam of Rothenburg and adds a month in a leap year.
  • The Pithei Teshuvah limits the Rema: it applies only if she is still nursing at that point. If she has stopped nursing by the 25th month, one can follow the Shulhan Arukh.

An 18-month ruling. A Rav Kluger, about 200 years ago, said that nowadays women rarely nurse for 24 months, so the 24 months can be read as 18. Rav Moshe Feinstein explains this in the Even HaEzer: Hazal's decree was only on a nursing woman. The period came later, and was set according to what was common then. So where it is no longer common, one can rely on 18 months. The Rabbi called this "a huge kula" and said one might need statistics on what is normal today.

2. The divorced woman (grushah)

The Shulhan Arukh says the decree applies to a divorcee too, so it follows Rabbenu Tam and not Rabbenu Shimshon. He also brings the Rashba. The Rashba understands Rabbenu Tam as speaking only where the baby is attached to her (makirah), meaning there is a real obligation to nurse (shibud) and a danger in giving the baby away. Seif 14 brings this limit of the Rashba first: the wait applies only where she is makirah, and not where she is free to refrain from nursing. But Seif 14 then also brings a second, stricter view: it applies even where she is not makirah and there is no shibud.

The Rabbi's reading: the Shulhan Arukh brings both, and we follow the later, stricter one (yesh vyesh). So we rule like Rabbenu Tam in every divorce case.

The Rashba's limit does not cover a widow. Even the Rashba agrees that a widow must wait, because she has no husband around and usually there is a shibud. The Beit Shemuel rules this way: a widow who claims her ketubah and has no duty to nurse still must wait 24 months.

Where there is any other reason to be lenient, he said, one may add the Rashba. He noted that Rav Ovadia Yosef was very lenient and would add the Rashba as one of the reasons. The Pithei Teshuvah, at the start of the Siman, quotes the Pnei Yehoshua, who has sharp words for rabbis who look for a heter in everything and says that those who are lenient have been punished with death and poverty. The Rabbi's lesson: look for a heter, but do not go overboard.

3. The woman whose child's father is unknown (mizanah)

This is a mahloket among the Rishonim. The Mordekhai says the decree applies even here, because the issue is the mother's natural love for her child. The Mahari Mints says the decree applies only where there is a possible shibud, and with mizanah there is none.

  • The Shulhan Arukh rules strictly: it applies to a mizanah as well.
  • The Rema says there are those who are lenient, and one can be lenient if she is mufkeret liznut (a promiscuous woman).

How does her behaviour matter? The decree was for the child's sake. One simple answer: where there is another problem, we may rely on the lenient opinions. The Shevut Yaakov offers a deeper one: the welfare of the child is not only about milk but also the home he is raised in. The Pithei Teshuvah brings a case from an Ishmaelite country where an illegitimate child is taken from his mother and brought up in a non-Jewish culture. In such a case one may let her marry even while she is still pregnant, so that the child is not taken. A well-known responsum has a similar ruling about a child who would be taken to a monastery. He says we do not tell her to marry and then divorce after the birth.

Most of these heterim apply after the birth; during the pregnancy it is much harder (see section 5).

What is mufkeret liznut? According to most Poskim it does not need to mean a woman who is with many men. Even a woman who lives with one man outside marriage counts.

A man claims the child. The Beit Shemuel says that if a man comes forward and says the child is his, we need not worry that it came from someone else. They can marry and stay together. The Pithei Teshuvah adds that this is true even if he is not sure the child is his, as he will still look after it.

If the father is a non-Jew. The Noda BiYehudah says it is permitted, as there is no shibud and the word haverah does not include a non-Jew. The Rav Akiva Eger and the Hatam Sofer reject this and say it is even worse, as a non-Jewish father will not look after the child and the mother's compassion is needed more. This is a mahloket of the Aharonim.

4. She is no longer nursing

A nursemaid or a vow. The Shulhan Arukh says that even if the woman gave the child to a nursemaid or weaned him within the 24 months, she may not remarry, as the nursemaid may retract. He rules against Rabbenu Yaakov, quoted by the Maharam, who permitted when she took a public vow (neder) or was under a great fear, as in the case of the Reish Galuta's court. Either we do not rule like the Reish Galuta case at all, or it is a special case that cannot be compared to a neder or a fear today.

The Rema says that if they married on that basis they need not separate. The Rabbi stressed that none of these factors alone is enough. But a combination can add up: a nursemaid, money set aside for the child beyond what he needs for the 24 months, or even child benefit. Then it becomes easier and easier to be lenient.

The Rosh. If the child died, the decree does not apply. If she weaned him in her husband's lifetime, or her milk stopped, or she cannot give milk, or the child was given to a nursemaid three months before her husband died and she did not nurse in those months, the Shulhan Arukh follows the Rosh. But the nursemaid alone, given in the husband's lifetime, is not enough. It must be combined with the other factors.

5. Do these heterim apply during the pregnancy?

This was the central question of the class. If a leniency applies, can she remarry while still pregnant? (She must still wait the three months of havhanah.)

The Rambam, who also gives the reason of daksa (pressure on the unborn child), would say no. Pregnancy is always forbidden for her, whatever the heter. Other Rishonim, who do not rule by daksa, might permit.

But the Beit Shemuel says no, even according to the Tosafot. The heterim apply only in the 24 months after birth and not while she is pregnant. Anything that forbids a pregnant woman also forbids her while nursing, and the other way round. The exception is a woman without breasts: she is not nursing and she is no longer pregnant after the birth, so she is permitted. So it will be rare to find a case where one can remarry during the pregnancy.

A man who cannot have children. The decree applies even if she wants to marry a saris.

Surrogacy. The Rabbi raised one question for thought. Some Poskim say that a married Jewish woman who carries someone else's embryo may be forbidden to live with her husband, as she is now a meuberet haverah. He noted that the decree of Hazal says she may not marry, and she is already married, so if it applied she would have to be divorced. He left this as a point to bear in mind and did not rule on it.

6. If they married anyway (Seifim 12-13)

The Shulhan Arukh says that if a man married a woman who is meuberet or meneket, we put him under a ban and force him to divorce her, unless he runs away for 24 months. He must pay her ketubah. An Israelite who divorces her may remarry her after the 24 months. A Kohen, who may not remarry a divorcee, would have to leave for 24 months and then return. Where only kiddushin were done, the Shulhan Arukh does not force a divorce, but the Rema requires one (not for a Kohen, who could then never remarry her).

Practical summary

  • The decree is 24 months from birth: the Shulhan Arukh counts 24 months, and the Rema adds a month in a leap year, but only if she is still nursing.
  • A divorcee and a woman whose child's father is unknown are both included in the decree. Rabbenu Tam is followed. The Rashba's limit may be added where there is another reason to be lenient.
  • If she was nursing and that has stopped, the Rosh's heterim apply. A nursemaid, a neder or a fear are not enough alone, but together they can be.
  • These heterim apply only after the birth, not during the pregnancy.
  • Rav Moshe permits relying on 18 months nowadays, a big kula.
  • If they married in breach of the decree, a ban and a forced divorce apply, and a Kohen has special problems.
