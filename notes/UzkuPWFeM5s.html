Topic: The third principle: God has no body and no form, and the Rambam's severe view of those who think otherwise Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 1 and Hilchot Teshuvah 3:7 with the Raavad; Moreh Nevuchim I:36 Shiur: Fourth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series at Tiferet Eyal on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Tuesday 10 December 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The principle

The last class of 2019 reached the third principle: God has no body and no bodily form. The Rabbi began with a confession: "of all the 13 principles... this is the most difficult principle to accept." Not because he believes God has a body, he stressed, "I absolutely don't", but for reasons that would emerge.

The Rambam writes that God is neither a body nor a force within a body, and nothing bodily applies to Him: no movement, rest or place. Hazal said, "Above there is no sitting and no standing." Every physical description in the Torah, walking, standing, speaking, is a metaphor, for "the Torah speaks in the language of people". His proofs: "To whom will you compare Me?" and "You saw no image." The Ani Maamin and the Yigdal ("He has no semblance of a body, nor is He a body") say the same.

More than "no physical body"

God has no spiritual form either. Angels have no bodies, which is why "how many angels fit on a pin" is a silly question, but each has its own level and purpose, which gives it a spiritual form. God has none at all.

The Rabbi then said plainly what he said sometimes shocks people: none of the physical descriptions of God in Tanakh are literal. "The voice of Hashem" in the Psalm of Friday night: God has no voice. "Hashem spoke to Moshe" means Moshe received a clear vision. "An outstretched arm" is not an arm.

The questions tested this: - A bat kol? That can be a physical voice God creates, like an agent; it is not God Himself. - Sinai? "We heard God" means the people had a perfectly clear perception of Him. - The burning bush? The bush really burned, which made Moshe stop and look. The fire was not God; the place was holy because of the vision there. - Avraham's three visitors? The Rambam holds it was a prophetic vision; the Ramban attacks him for that. Even if they were real, angels coming down to earth must "put on a spacesuit", a body. - "You will see My back, but not My face" means we can grasp something of God's ways, never His essence.

Why a separate principle?

The Rambam shows it follows from the first two. A body is limited, and God is unlimited. And God's oneness fills all existence, while a body is in one place. So why list it at all? Because many people in his time believed God had a body, and the plain words of the Torah encouraged them. He had to say it explicitly.

The Rabbi recalled Rabbi Sacks being asked why God created atheists: "To keep the rabbis in check." Criticism forces us to refine childish pictures of God. Much atheist criticism, he added, attacks a Christian, very physical reading of the Bible, not the Torah read through rabbinic eyes.

We cannot avoid physical images: "Avinu Malkenu", a King on a throne, God "up there". That is fine, as long as we trace them back. His analogy: builders measuring a room in units of "x" stop thinking of x as a roll of wallpaper, but they know what it means. Kabbalah, from the first page of Rabbi Hayim Vital, warns that all its images are borrowed terms. God is no more "up" than "down"; He is everywhere.

And since God has no form, He cannot be defined or known. Rabbi Yosef Albo quotes the saying: "If I knew Him, I would be Him." All the Torah tells us is what God does and how He relates to us, not what He is.

The difficulty: the Rambam's severity

The Rambam lists among those who lose their share in the World to Come "one who says there is one Master, but He has a body or form". In the Moreh Nevuchim (I:36) he goes further: believing God has a body is worse than idolatry. The idolater serves an intermediary but still believes in an incorporeal God above it. Someone who imagines God with a body does not believe in God at all.

The Rabbi gave two reasons why this troubles him:

1. The Torah's own words. Someone who studied the whole Tanakh sincerely, without guidance, could easily conclude God has a body. "Let us make man in our image." The elders "saw the God of Israel, and under His feet was like sapphire... and they ate and drank." "You will see My back." The Talmud even says God wears tefillin. This is the only one of the 13 principles with no explicit source in the Torah or Talmud. The first to call belief in a bodily God heresy, as far as he knew, was Rabbi Saadiah Gaon.

The Rambam's answer: the idolater is also misled by his imagination, and that is no excuse. "I do not consider an unbeliever one who cannot prove that God has no body, but I do consider an unbeliever one who does not believe it."

2. Great men held otherwise. The Raavad protests: "Why does he call such a person a heretic? Greater and better men than he believed this, because of what they found in Scripture and in the aggadot." The Raavad himself, the Rabbi stressed, agrees God has no body. His objection is to the harshness. Indeed Rabbi Moshe Taku, one of the Baalei Tosafot, wrote that God has a body. He cited the Talmud's parable of twin brothers, one a king, one a robber who was hanged, so that people said "the king is hanged". How could anyone confuse a man with a purely spiritual God? And the Rambam knew: he writes in his Essay on Resurrection that he met people who learn Torah all day and are unsure whether God has eyes and hands.

Someone suggested that ignorance does not protect you, any more than not understanding electricity stops a shock. The Rabbi agreed this is the Rambam's logic: for him the World to Come is the understanding of God a person has built, and a crude picture of God leaves nothing to build on. He cited Rabbi Bleich's analogy of a student who missed algebra through illness: no one blames him, but he cannot go on to calculus.

The Rabbi's conclusion

Most Rishonim, he said, including the Raavad, Rabbenu Bahya, the Rashbas and Rabbi Yosef Albo, hold that God certainly has no body, but someone who believes otherwise innocently and sincerely is not punished. "I think this is the most logical approach to take."

He told a story recorded by Rabbi Moshe Hagiz. A simple man put two hallot in the Ark every Friday, believing God ate them; in fact the shammash took them. When the rabbi of the shul stopped him, the Arizal rebuked the rabbi: no one had brought such a beautiful offering since the days of the Temple. Whether or not it happened, it shows that such belief can be sincere.

Teaching children, and why the Torah speaks this way

The Rambam says even children must be taught that God has no body. The Rabbi's own mother answered his childhood question "Where is Hashem?" with "Hashem is everywhere." Yet the Talmud praises the young Abaye and Rava who, asked where God is, pointed to the roof and the sky. We may speak of God "up there", as long as children grow up knowing He has no form.

Why does the Torah use such misleading language? The Rambam's simple answer: it speaks in human language. The Moreh Nevuchim and Rabbenu Bahya add that at Sinai it was too much to teach both that God is one and that He has no body. First the people had to accept one God; the rest would come later. It shows how much more important belief in one God was than this point.

He wished everyone well until 14 January and principle 4, creation.

Summary

  • God has neither a physical nor a spiritual form, and every bodily description of Him in Tanakh is a metaphor.
  • The principle follows logically from God's perfection and unity, but the Rambam stated it because many believed otherwise.
  • We may use physical images of God, as long as we trace them back to an incorporeal God.
  • The Rambam excludes from the World to Come anyone who thinks God has a body; the Raavad and most Rishonim protest that a sincere, naive believer is not punished, and the Rabbi agrees with them.
  • Children should be taught from the start that God has no body and is everywhere.
