Topic: Why Hashem created the universe, and why the good He gives has to be earned Source: The Ramhal, Derech Hashem 1:2:1-2 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 26 February 2024, the second class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

What is the point of it all?

Dayan Kada began by reviewing the first class: Hashem is one and has no parts, is infinite, depends on nothing and is perfect. One more description would be crucial tonight: He is the Creator.

The question for the evening was: is there a purpose to the creation of the universe? "The answer to that is very simple. Yes, there is. The question is, what is it?" He said this question had troubled him from the age of about twelve, and no answer satisfied him until he studied the Ramhal. We live perhaps ninety or a hundred years, many of them hard. What is the point of everything we go through?

He asked everyone for their answers, and met each one with "and so what?"

  • To enjoy the world. Then it is not a very good universe. Think of a normal weekday of work and worry, or of life a century ago with no heating. Life is not mostly enjoyment.
  • To develop ourselves. Lovely words, but for what? I develop myself, and die at ninety.
  • To serve God. Then God would be incomplete until we come along to serve Him, which contradicts His perfection. Hashem does not need anything from us.
  • To make a dwelling place for Hashem in the lower worlds, an idea from the Baal HaTanya. A beautiful Jewish idea, he said, but why create an imperfect world to be repaired, rather than a perfect one?
  • The struggle between body and soul. "That's 100% true," he said, and he would develop it later. But what is the struggle for?

To bestow goodness

The answer is one sentence, from Derech Hashem, and he asked everyone to learn it by heart: the purpose of creation was lehetiv mituvo lezulato, to bestow of His goodness upon others.

He then built the argument step by step, numbering the Ramhal's points himself:

  1. Hashem wants to bestow goodness.
  2. Since Hashem is perfect, the goodness He gives must be perfect goodness.
  3. True perfection is found only in Hashem. Even the finest human being is imperfect compared with Him.
  4. So the only perfect goodness He could give is His own.
  5. But Hashem cannot make another God, because part of the definition of God is that He is one. "As it were, God had a problem."

The solution is that Hashem remains God and allows human beings to cleave to Him. The Hebrew word devekut comes from devek, glue: to attach ourselves to Hashem and enjoy His presence.

The Queen's banquet

To explain this, he used the example of the late Queen, and invited everyone to think instead of whoever they admire most. Imagine the Queen wanted every citizen to have exactly what she has. Perfect goodness would mean making everyone a king or queen, which is absurd: there can only be one monarch. So instead she allows everyone to spend time with her, and imagine that you never tire of it and the pleasure stays as intense for years, for millennia. That is how Hashem gives perfect goodness without compromising His oneness.

Where does this happen? Not here, where the body, which wants food, drink and comfort, is a barrier. It happens in the World to Come, a world of souls. Anyone who pictures the World to Come as endless food and drink "has completely the wrong idea". What we feel here is only a taste. Hazal say that Shabbat is one sixtieth of the World to Come. One sixtieth, as in bitul beshishim, is the measure of something present but not experienced: our highest spiritual moment is only a trace of what is to come.

Then he took the analogy further. Imagine the Queen announces a banquet, years from now, in a stadium holding millions. The closer you sit, the more you enjoy her company. Who sits where? "The more royal you are," the more you have in common with her. Some will spend the time studying etiquette and practising; others will behave like hooligans and end up at the back; and some will be so unlike her that they cannot be given a seat at all. So too with Hashem: the more refined and Godlike a person becomes, the closer he can attach. Like magnets, how strongly you are drawn depends on you.

Gehinam, karet and consequences

Someone who cannot enter at all suffers karet, being cut off, the most severe consequence in the Torah. The soul simply has no ability to attach to the divine, and it ceases to exist.

Gehinam is a period of refinement involving spiritual pain, lasting at most twelve months. That is why Kaddish is said for a parent for no more than twelve months, and why the custom is to stop a week before, so as not to imply that the parent needed the full term. If someone is in Gehinam, he said, there is something to be glad about: it means he has a place in the stadium.

He compared this to an apple: a few bruises you cut out, but an apple bruised all over is thrown away. Most human beings are bruised apples who will enter the stadium after some process of refinement, but the longer the process needed, the further back they will sit.

He also rejected the word reward. Being paid ten pounds for arriving at the gym is a reward. Becoming fit from exercising is not a reward; it is a consequence. Moshe Rabbenu has a seat at the table, as it were, not as a prize but because he made himself the most Godlike person, "you are the person most similar to me."

Why not create us perfect?

Someone asked: why not create beings like angels, who simply cleave to God? He answered with a parable. Two young men drive identical Lamborghinis. One built a business and worked for five years to buy his; the other was given his by his father for his birthday. The first enjoys it far more, because it is truly his. More than that, his achievement is intrinsic to him, while the second's is accidental.

Hashem's goodness is intrinsic: He is good because that is what He is. So the most Godlike goodness a person can have is goodness he has acquired himself. The Ramhal writes that for the good to be perfect, the one who benefits from it must be its owner, not someone to whom it is attached by chance.

This is the purpose of the universe: a world with much good and much bad, and the choice between them. To earn the good, I must be able to fail. This is also why we have a body and a soul. The body pulls one way and the soul another, and that constant tug of war is how we become more like Hashem and able to cleave to Him more closely.

Questions from the evening

  • What about someone raised with no belief at all? He called this a fair and important question. A child is not responsible. But there comes a point where everyone must think beyond what he was brought up with, just as someone raised with prejudice cannot use his upbringing as an excuse at work. He was sure Hashem gives people opportunities to break away.
  • Does the starting point count? He compared someone from a violent criminal home who works on himself and ends up stealing but never killing, with someone from a deeply spiritual family who ends up a liar. Both the journey and the result count. The effort is taken into account, but a serious flaw remains a flaw. "How these calculations work I can't tell you"; only Hashem can weigh them.
  • Keeping the misvot without inner change. Someone who goes to shul and keeps Shabbat but is unchanged inside has missed the point.
  • How do we know what is good? Through revelation. The Torah tells us what good is, and the experience at Sinai was a small taste of what is to come. That would be the next topic.

He ended by stressing that this world is also there to be enjoyed. Sukkot, zeman simhatenu, the time of our joy, commemorates forty years of wandering in the desert. "The happiness is not the destination." Life is a journey, and we should enjoy it.

Summary

  • The purpose of creation, in the Ramhal's words, is for Hashem to bestow of His goodness upon others.
  • Perfect goodness exists only in Hashem, and there can be no second God, so He allows us to cleave to Him in the World to Come.
  • How closely we cleave depends on how Godlike we have become: a consequence of who we are, not a reward.
  • Gehinam is a refinement of up to twelve months for those who will enter; karet means being cut off entirely.
  • Goodness we earn is intrinsically ours and most like Hashem's, which is why we were given a world of good and bad and the freedom to choose.
