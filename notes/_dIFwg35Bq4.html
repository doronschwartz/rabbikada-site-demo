Women and children

The previous class had discussed at length whether the misvah of sukkah is eating or dwelling. With Sukkot less than a week away, this class turned to Siman 640: who is exempt.

Women are exempt, because sukkah is a positive misvah bound to a time. Everyone agrees there is still a misvah for a woman to sit in the sukkah. The question is the berakhah. Ashkenazim hold a woman may take the misvah on herself and say the berakhah, on the sukkah, the arbaah minim or the shofar. The Shulhan Arukh, followed by Sefaradim, holds she does not, since she cannot say "who commanded us." The Rabbi noted that many Sefaradi communities, in Iraq, Afghanistan and Morocco among others, did have women say these berakhot. A woman who knows that is her family's tradition may continue it; otherwise she should not say the berakhah. A person whose gender is in doubt sits in the sukkah out of doubt, without a berakhah (for Sefaradim).

Children: a boy who no longer needs his mother, around five or six, is obligated by the Rabbis, for his education. The age depends on the child: can he sit at the table and eat without his mother? A small child who needs his mother does not obligate her to sit outside. The Rabbi pointed out that the sources assume mothers looked after children; where the father can look after him, the child may be obligated younger. Whatever one does about encouraging a child, actively feeding a seven-year-old boy bread outside the sukkah would be wrong.

The ill and the uncomfortable

An ill person and those caring for him are exempt. This is not about danger: a slight headache or a small stomach ache is enough, because "you dwell in it as you would live" (teshevu keein taduru). At home, if a room bothered you, you would go to another room. The carer is exempt because one busy with one misvah is exempt from another. If your son is in bed with flu and you are looking after him, you may eat with him indoors. If your frail mother needs you to visit her, you need not wait until you get home; you may eat while caring for her. The Shulhan Arukh rules that the carer is exempt only while actually needed.

One in discomfort (mistaer) is exempt himself, but not those around him. Wind, flies, a bad smell, building works next door: anything for which you would change rooms at home. This applies only if the problem was unexpected. You cannot build next to a building site, or in a place full of flies, and then claim exemption.

Someone asked: if I know it will be cold, must I put in a heater? The Biur Halacha, quoting the Levush, says one need not carry pillows and blankets out each night; that is a real burden. But if there is room to keep them in the sukkah, one should. The Rabbi applied this to the indoor sukkah, now common in new Jewish homes: if a bed or heater can stay there for the week, you should use it. If everything has to be wheeled in and out each time, that is arguably a discomfort and not required.

He stressed one common mistake. If it is cold and you sit in the sukkah anyway because of the lovely atmosphere, "that's lovely", but you cannot say Leshev BaSukkah, because one in discomfort is exempt. Wrap up properly, coat and double socks, so that you are comfortable, and then you may say it. With Sukkot so late this year, he could not see how anyone could say the berakhah at night in this weather in England without wrapping up.

Limits of discomfort

  • A place you could never sleep in. The Rema says that if you built where you could not sleep at night, for fear of thieves, even your daytime meals there do not count, since it is not a place one can live in. The Rabbi recalled advising someone with rats in the garden at night: theoretically no one could ever sleep there, so it is not a sukkah, even by day.
  • Darkness. If the candles blow out on Friday night, eating in the dark is a discomfort and you may go inside. We do not require you to carry everything to a neighbour's lit sukkah, though for one sandwich, if he does not mind, go.
  • Wind. You may hang sheets on the sides, but nothing beneath the sekhakh.
  • Curling up. The minimum sukkah is seven by seven tefahim, enough for your head and most of your body. Having to curl up to sleep is not a discomfort; most people do. "Be honest with yourself."
  • No better alternative. If the bad smell is just as bad at home, you are not exempt, since moving does not help.

Mourners, a hatan and those on a misvah

A mourner (avel) is obligated: his distress does not come from the sukkah. The Mishnah Berurah asks about an onen, whose relative has died but not yet been buried. An onen is exempt from all positive misvot but may not do anything forbidden. Eating a kebeisah of bread outside the sukkah is forbidden, so if he wants to eat bread he goes into the sukkah, without a berakhah.

A hatan, his companions and his wedding party are exempt all seven days of the Sheva Berakhot, because the young couple need privacy, which a sukkah does not give. If he wishes to sit there he may, but without the berakhah until the seven days end. Some opinions disagree, so the Mishnah Berurah suggests the hatan sit there without a berakhah, and Rav Ovadia Yosef writes similarly.

Those on the way to a misvah are exempt day and night, so their minds stay on the misvah: rescuing captives, a soldier, or more subtly, travelling to visit one's rav on the festival.

Travellers for any purpose, even leisure, are exempt while travelling. When you stop for the night, try to find a sukkah; if staying several nights, build one. Why not tell people not to travel on Hol HaMoed? Because you live in the sukkah exactly as you live at home, and at home you may decide to go on holiday. Simhat Yom Tov is itself a misvah from the Torah.

The Rabbi questioned the value of sukkot at theme parks and zoos: a day visitor was exempt and could eat his sandwiches anywhere, but once a sukkah is there he must use it. Still, those who build them merit people with more misvot, and that should be commended. On a cruise ship, which is a fixed place to live, one would in principle need a sukkah.

Siman 641: Shehiyanu on building

With five minutes left he began 641. In principle one could say Shehiyanu on building a sukkah, a once-a-year preparation for a misvah, but already in the Gemara we rely on the Shehiyanu of Kiddush. So on the first night it covers the festival, the misvah of sitting, and the building. The Rema adds that if you ate the first night indoors because of rain, you say Shehiyanu when you first eat in the sukkah. He left questions for next time: what if you ate the first night at a friend's sukkah, or in a covered sukkah, and does the Shehiyanu on the lulav help?

Practical summary

  • Sefaradi women sit in the sukkah as a misvah but do not say the berakhah, unless they know their family's custom was to say it.
  • Boys from about five or six should eat in the sukkah; do not feed an older boy bread outside it.
  • Even mild illness exempts the patient and his carers while they are needed; mild discomfort exempts only the one affected, and only if unforeseen and if moving helps.
  • If you sit in the cold without being comfortable, do not say Leshev BaSukkah; wrap up properly if you want to say it.
  • A hatan is exempt for the seven days of Sheva Berakhot and sits without a berakhah; a mourner is obligated.
  • Travellers are exempt on the road, but should seek a sukkah where they stop for the night.
