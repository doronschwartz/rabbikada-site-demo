Topic: Praying Arvit after Plag HaMincha on Friday, eating before nightfall, and the Friday night Amidah (Hashkivenu, Vayekhulu, Atah Kiddashta) Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Simanim 261:2, 267 and 268 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 6 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (early 2014, estimated).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why Arvit can be prayed early on Friday

From Plag HaMincha on Friday, a person may light candles, pray Arvit, accept Shabbat and begin the meal, long before nightfall. The reason, as seen in earlier classes, is that in the past Shabbat was accepted by saying "Barukh Hu" at the start of Arvit. Since people wanted to accept Shabbat early, they prayed Arvit early.

Mincha and Arvit: the background

The Gemara records a dispute. According to the Sages, Mincha can be prayed until night, but Arvit only from nightfall. According to Rabbi Yehudah, Mincha can be prayed only until Plag HaMincha, and Arvit from Plag. Each opinion is lenient in one way and strict in another. The Gemara does not rule and says, in effect, that you may choose either.

The Rishonim add a rule: you may not contradict yourself (tartei desatrei). You cannot follow the Sages for Mincha, praying after Plag, and Rabbi Yehudah for Arvit, praying also after Plag, on the same day. Most Rishonim say you cannot even switch from one day to the next. The Meiri and others say that only the same day is forbidden.

The Mishnah Berurah applies this to Friday. Even if you normally follow the Sages and pray Arvit at nightfall, on Friday you can pray Arvit early to bring Shabbat in sooner, but you should not contradict on the same day. Pray Mincha before Plag and Arvit after, or, if you pray Mincha after Plag, wait for Arvit until after sunset. He allows the contradiction in a congregation, as the Ben Ish Hai does, because it is hard to get people to come twice or to wait. The Rabbi noted that for an individual, say someone on holiday praying alone, this leniency does not apply.

The Rabbi pointed out that in England in the summer, with sunset at 9.00 and nightfall at 10.30, Plag is about 7.30, so praying Mincha early enough is impractical. Even Ashkenazim rely on the leniency. Sefaradim follow the Ben Ish Hai and others even during the week.

An important point is that if you pray Mincha shortly before sunset, say fifteen minutes before, and Arvit after sunset, even an individual is fine, since sunset is a doubtful time (safek). That is the custom in many communities. Nowadays the ideal is Mincha a quarter of an hour before sunset and Arvit straight after. He noted that women can rely on the leniency, as they are busy with the house. Whoever prays Arvit before nightfall must repeat the Shema after nightfall.

Eating before nightfall

A person who prays Arvit after Plag and lights candles can sit down to the meal, even in full daylight. Some authorities say the meal itself should be eaten at night, for two reasons: some opinions do not hold of the "addition" to Shabbat, and even if it is Shabbat, it is not yet the 24 hours of Shabbat. According to those, the solution is to eat at least a kezayit of bread (about 30 grams) after nightfall.

Eating less than half an hour before nightfall

It is forbidden to start a meal within half an hour before nightfall until you have prayed Arvit and read the Shema. When Arvit is early, this raises a question. If nightfall is at 10.30 and you come home at 9.00, there is no problem. But if nightfall is at 9.00 and you start at 8.30, there may be.

For Sefaradim, the Beit Yoseph relies on the opinions that the Shema read after Plag counts, and also this is only a rabbinic restriction. So there is no problem. For Ashkenazim, the Mishnah Berurah is stricter, but he also seems to permit it in another place, so one can be lenient when needed. Appointing a watcher (shomer) to remind you is also valid, even if the watcher is eating too. The Rabbi suggested that if two people eat together and remind each other, it is certainly fine.

Counting the Omer is more difficult. If you prayed before sunset you have not yet counted, which is a real problem (some communities count from sunset), so you need a shomer who is not eating, or you can make Kiddush, wash and eat only a kezayit, then count later and continue.

Hashkivenu: no "Shomer Amo Yisrael"

On Friday night we do not say "Shomer Amo Yisrael La'ad" at the end of Hashkivenu, because Shabbat itself is protection. In the Sefaradi custom the blessing is cut short after "Ve'agen Ba'adenu" and then continues "Ufros alenu sukkat shalom", ending "HaPoreis sukkat shalom aleinu ve'al amo Yisrael ve'al Yerushalayim". We answer Amen, since it ends a set of blessings. The word "Rahamim" is disputed. Maran does not mention it, so someone with no custom should leave it out.

The Ashkenazim say the whole blessing, ending "HaPoreis sukkat shalom". Their reason: Shabbat only protects us if we keep it, and some feared they might not. If someone forgot and said the usual ending, if he catches himself within about three seconds he says "HaPoreis sukkat shalom"; otherwise he does not go back.

The Vilna Gaon disliked this practice because of the rule of linking redemption to prayer. The Rabbi said the Hashkivenu blessing is a continuation of the redemption, since it is also salvation from harmful forces, so it is not an interruption.

Vayekhulu and the Friday night Amidah

Saying "Vayekhulu" makes us partners in creation. Declaring that God finished the work makes us witnesses and, in a sense, partners. We say it three times: in the Amidah, together after it, and before Kiddush. Someone who forgot it in the Amidah does not go back, but should say it again afterwards. Different customs exist about "Bo'i kalah"; each should follow his own.

The three Shabbat Amidot are explained as Atah Kiddashta (betrothal), Yismah Moshe (joy) and Atah Ehad (union).

Mistakes in the weekday blessing (268)

If a person begins a weekday blessing by mistake, he completes it and goes back to Atah Kiddashta, even if he is several blessings in. A person who says "Atah Honen" by mistake is advised to take care, since mistakes are not a good sign for the week, and should pray from the siddur. The reason is that the Shabbat Amidah is really a shortened weekday Amidah, shortened so as not to burden people. The Rabbi gave reasons: the fear and concentration of the Amidah is hard to sustain for long, so the Sages let us add songs instead of lengthening it.

In Musaf there is a dispute: the first view finishes the blessing, the second stops. The poskim rule like the second because of doubt about blessings. The prayer leader should stop in the repetition. Atah Honen is more complicated, as "Atah" can also begin "Atah Kiddashta" or "Atah Ehad", and was left for the following week.

Practical summary

  • On Friday you may pray Arvit after Plag HaMincha. Do not pray Mincha after Plag and Arvit soon after on the same day, unless in a congregation where it is difficult.
  • Ideal: Mincha about fifteen minutes before sunset, Arvit just after.
  • Sefaradim may begin the meal early; Ashkenazim should appoint a shomer if within half an hour before nightfall.
  • On Friday night, do not say "Shomer Amo Yisrael"; end with "HaPoreis sukkat shalom".
  • If you start a weekday blessing in the Amidah, finish it and go back to Atah Kiddashta; in Musaf stop and go back.
