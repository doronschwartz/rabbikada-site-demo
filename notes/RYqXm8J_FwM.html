Topic: The time for lighting the Hanukkah candles: earliest time, lighting versus Arvit, how long they burn, and a spouse who comes home late Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 672 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 15 December 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Questions to carry through the class

Siman 672 is "the time for lighting the Hanukkah candles". The Rabbi opened with practical dilemmas and returned to them as the sources came: - If I pray Arvit at 4:30, do I pray first or light first? - If I can light just after sunset or at 11 at night, which is better? - My wife and children are home at 4:30 but I come home from the office at 7. Who lights, and when? - I have an evening event and must leave at 4. Can I light before I go?

He added that lighting in shul is a later custom and not part of the basic obligation.

The earliest time

The Shulhan Arukh says not to light before the sun begins to set, but at the end of the setting. London sunset in the coming week is about 3:50 and nightfall about 4:20 to 4:25. What does "the end of the setting" mean? The Shulhan Arukh follows the view that it is the end of sunset, and the Rema is silent. The Ben Ish Hai and the Mishnah Berurah seem to imply the beginning, and the Vilna Gaon was strict about it. For Sephardim there is plenty to rely on to light once it begins to get dark.

The Gemara speaks of the sun's setting and not of nightfall, to teach that Hanukkah does not depend on night the way Kriat Shema does. The law is to light when the light can be seen. He compared Birkat HaLevanah, where one waits only until there is enough moonlight to benefit. So there is no need to wait for full nightfall by the minute, and lighting a little before it is fine. People also worked in the fields and came home over a stretch of time, so the Sages meant a range and not a single minute. In Israel sunset to nightfall is only about thirteen and a half minutes, so the difference matters less.

Light first or pray first?

The Rabbi asked the class to reason it out: - The misvah of the candles has a short window, while Arvit can be said until late. - The counter-argument: we do the more frequent practice first, and every misvah is better done early. Praying at 4:30 beats praying at 10. - His answer: you have the whole night to pray, whereas Hanukkah, though you may light all night, drops to a lower level after the half hour. So when the time for lighting has come, the candles come first. If it is still two minutes before nightfall, Arvit simply waits for its time and there is no question. - In Israel many small shuls pray Minhah just before sunset and Arvit after. In London we cannot wait that long, so we begin just after sunset, and that is fine too. - Where a minyan is at stake, a rabbi must weigh it practically: if fifteen pray Minhah but only twelve can return, he would accept losing the three.

Have the candles and wicks ready beforehand, so time does not slip away.

How long must they burn?

The candles must last half an hour after nightfall. After that you may put them out, and even use the light for reading. Almost every year there is a tragedy from a menorah left burning when the family goes out, so there is no reason to leave them. The Mishnah Berurah says that using more oil beautifies the misvah but is not required, and oil is expensive.

The Rabbi gave two reasons to be less particular now: we light indoors, so the publicity is mostly for the family, and with electric light people are out late anyway. The Rabbi of Brisk held that the oil should last until the street is empty, nine or ten at night, but everyone else disagrees, since the Sages fixed half an hour.

If the half hour has passed, one can still light during the night, but at a lower level.

A spouse who is late

  • The halakha asks for lighting at the right time, whether by husband or wife, since the misvah is on the household.
  • The Rabbi added that the relationship, the children, the family atmosphere and the value of lighting together all count.
  • If one parent is away, wait a reasonable time, while people are still out. If the spouse would be upset at your lighting without them, honour that wish.
  • If one will only be back at 9 or 10, the other lights at the proper time with the children, at 4:30.

The last case, lighting before leaving for an evening event, was left unfinished: the light must last half an hour after nightfall, and the safety question if no one is at home is real.

Practical summary

  • Do not light before the sun begins to set; for Sephardim, lighting once it starts to get dark, shortly after sunset, is well founded.
  • When the time has come, light before Arvit: the candles have a narrow window.
  • The candles must burn half an hour after nightfall; then they may be put out.
  • If the time was missed, one may still light during the night.
  • If a spouse is late, wait a reasonable time, but not far past the time when people are still outside.
  • Prepare wicks and oil in advance, and do not leave a menorah burning in an empty home.
