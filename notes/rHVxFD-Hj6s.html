When did the Purim story happen?

People often assume Purim comes after Hanukah because it falls later in the year. The opposite is true: the Purim story is a few hundred years earlier than the story of the Maccabees.

The Rabbi set out the background. After the giving of the Torah, the Shekhinah rested in the Mishkan for over four hundred years until Shlomo HaMelekh built the first Beit HaMikdash. Then came the split into two kingdoms: ten tribes in the north, and Yehudah and Binyamin, with the Kohanim and Leviim, in the south, where the Mikdash stood. "When we're not together and there isn't unity, we often forget what keeps us together," and we fall prey to our enemies. The north was exiled by Assyria and the ten tribes were lost. Later Nevukhadnesar destroyed the Mikdash and exiled the south to Babylonia, modern Iraq.

Yirmiyahu had prophesied a return after seventy years. But people build communities in exile, and many gave up hope; some moved on to Persia. By the end of the seventy years Persia had overtaken Babylon, and Ahashverosh ruled 127 provinces. By his calculation the seventy years had passed with no return, so he concluded the Jews were finished and threw a feast using the vessels of the Mikdash. The food and wine were kosher, but the hakhamim of the time warned against attending because of those vessels. The Gemara says that attending was the sin that led to the decree.

A story driven by kavod

Human beings want to be respected. We also carry a divine spark, so we seek perfection, and we want others to recognise it. That is dangerous when our sense of worth depends on the opinion of others. Read the Megillah carefully, the Rabbi said, and you will see it is all about kavod.

  • Ahashverosh summons Vashti "to show the peoples and princes her beauty." She refuses to be put on display. His advisers warn that every wife in the empire will stop honouring her husband, and advise that she be removed.
  • Haman has everyone bowing to him except Mordekhai, and "Haman was filled with rage" (3:5). Later he comes to the palace at midnight, so consumed with his own honour that he forgets people are sleeping. Asked what to do for the man the king wishes to honour, he thinks, "Whom would the king wish to honour more than me?" He ends up leading Mordekhai through the streets and goes home mourning, his head covered.

Now contrast the heroes. When Mordekhai uncovers the plot of Bigtan and Teresh, Esther tells the king "in the name of Mordekhai." A newly chosen queen could have taken the credit; she did not. Later Esther instructs Mordekhai to gather the Jews to fast, and "Mordekhai did all that Esther commanded him." He was the head of the Sanhedrin, the greatest sage of his generation, taking instructions from his much younger niece.

Two kinds of kavod

The word kavod comes from kaved, heavy. We honour those who carry weight in our eyes. But weight means different things to different people. For Ahashverosh it was having the most beautiful wife. For Haman it was political recognition and money; he even boasted to his family about his wealth. For Mordekhai and Esther it was doing what is right and pursuing the truth.

Borrowing a phrase from Rabbi Sacks, the Rabbi explained that power, prominence and wealth are zero-sum games. If I have more, you have less, so people who want them end up fighting. Haman fights Mordekhai; Ahashverosh fights Vashti. Love, friendship, influence and truth are the opposite: the more you share them, the more there is. They lead to cooperation, not competition.

That is why the musar books speak of kavod hamedumeh, illusory honour. If everyone had it, it would be worthless; it is only valuable because others lack it. Real kavod is the kind that grows when shared. The villains chase the first kind and lose. Those who see Hashem in their lives value the second and win.

God is hidden, but present

The Gemara in Hulin asks, "Where is Esther hinted at in the Torah?" and answers with "VeAnokhi haster astir panai," "I will surely hide My face." Only two of the twenty-four books of Tanakh lack the name of God: Esther and Shir HaShirim. In Shir HaShirim, a love song, there is no need to name the beloved. But Esther is a frightening book, with language about destroying, killing and annihilating the Jewish people unlike anything else in Tanakh, and yet God is not named. Purim is also the only festival whose story happens outside the Land of Israel, not even on the way there. It is the story of exile, and that is why we identify with it.

The theme is hashgahah peratit, seeing Hashem in hidden times. The first word of Vayikra is written with a small alef. Without it, the word reads vayikar, "it happened": life as a string of chance events. With the small alef it is vayikra, "He called": I hear someone calling me. That is what Mordekhai told Esther: perhaps this is why you became queen. The Rabbi recommended Viktor Frankl's short book Man's Search for Meaning, where Frankl describes hearing that call even in the camps.

The whole Megillah can be read as coincidence: a party, a queen removed, a plot overheard, a man hanged on his own gallows. Hashem's name is absent, but He runs through every step. That is one reason the halachah requires reading the Megillah in order: out of order, you lose the thread. The word hateva, nature, has the same numerical value as Elohim, 86. One can sink into nature and forget there is a God behind it, or see Him in it.

He added that this is why we increase joy for the whole of Adar. The Gemara finds Haman hinted at in "Hamin haEs," "Did you eat from the tree?" Adam and Havah had all of paradise and wanted the one tree. Haman had everyone bowing except one man. Seeing Hashem in everything, and thanking Him each morning in Birkot HaShahar for what we take for granted, brings happiness.

Laws for Purim on a Friday

That year Purim fell on a Thursday night and Friday, during lockdown.

  • Megillah: read at night and by day. The daytime obligation is greater, so anyone who comes at night should also come in the day. Those unable to go to synagogue can hear a live Zoom reading.
  • Mishloah manot: one portion with two items to one person. Someone unable to go out can send it with an agent, even a taxi. If even that is impossible, there are authorities who allow giving it to someone in one's own household, in difficult circumstances. An agent is better where possible.
  • The seudah on Friday: normally one should not hold an unusually large meal on Friday, but a misvah meal is permitted all day. One may eat the Purim meal in the morning, which some prefer so as to come to Shabbat with an appetite, or after midday. A third option is to begin the meal before Shabbat and continue into it, making Kiddush at nightfall in the middle of the meal. All three options are acceptable.

Summary

  • Purim happened in the Persian exile, centuries before Hanukah, after the feast with the Temple vessels.
  • The villains of the Megillah chase honour that cannot be shared and end up fighting; Mordekhai and Esther pursue truth and work together.
  • Real kavod is the kind that grows when shared: love, friendship, influence, truth.
  • Hashem's name is hidden in the Megillah, but He runs through it; the small alef turns "it happened" into "He called."
  • Read the Megillah by day as well as by night, and plan the Friday seudah using one of the three accepted options.
