Topic: The eleventh principle: reward and punishment, understood as the consequences of our choices Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Teshuvah 8 and 9:1; Mesillat Yesharim Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's monthly Sunday-night series in Wembley on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, May 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

"Shattering some idols"

The Rabbi opened by saying he hoped to "shatter some of the idols" we grow up with about reward and punishment.

The Rambam's eleventh principle: God rewards those who keep the Torah's commands and punishes those who transgress them. The greatest reward is Olam HaBa, the World to Come. The severest punishment is karet, being cut off. His proof is Moshe's plea after the golden calf, "blot me out of Your book", and God's answer, "whoever has sinned against Me, him will I blot out." Here, unusually, the Ani Maamin and the Yigdal match the Rambam exactly: "He rewards man with kindness according to his deeds."

The principle follows from principle 10. Because God knows our deeds, words and thoughts, there are repercussions for our choices. The Torah is full of examples from the start: Adam, Havah, Kayin and Noah.

Why "reward and punishment" misleads

The English words carry a Christian tinge, he said. He told of an elderly relative who once asked him to promise that his students would form a circle around him to protect him from the fires of Gehinnom. "Sometimes it's better to leave them in this theology," he said, but tonight's aim was a more refined understanding.

The better word is consequences. Just as parents are told to speak to children about consequences rather than rewards and punishments, our choices have natural results. Even middah keneged middah, often translated "tit for tat", does not mean a vengeful God. It means that each person chooses his own identity and destiny.

Olam HaBa: the identity you build

Because we have free will, we create our own identity in our 120 years. We will only fully experience that identity after death, free of the body's limits, and we cannot change it after we die. This is why Hazal call this world a corridor to the World to Come.

He quoted the Ramhal: man was created only to delight in God and enjoy the radiance of His presence, the place for this is the World to Come, and the path to it is this world. Olam HaBa, the Rabbi explained, is sharing existence with God, who fills all existence. The more physical and short-sighted a person's life, the greater the barrier between him and God. The more a person lives with Torah ideals, spirituality and selflessness, the smaller the barrier.

He pointed out that we quote "All Israel has a portion in the World to Come" every Shabbat, but the Mishnah continues with a list of those who do not.

Two kinds of "punishment"

Regret. A rasha, he said, is not a monster with fangs. He is simply someone who lives in the moment without a vision. Gehinnom for him is the realisation of what he could have been, like the heat we feel remembering a bad decision. The Talmud says that each person is "burned" by the canopy of the one above him. Take it conceptually, he said: "I was in school with him... I could have been that."

Karet. Thirty-six sins in the Torah carry karet. The Rambam explains it as the gravest consequence possible: the soul is cut off and ceases to exist, "and perishes like a beast". He quoted the Rambam (Hilchot Teshuvah 8): "the ultimate nullification after which there is no renewal, and the ultimate loss which can never be recovered". The Rabbi said that should make us shiver. But such a person is "the minority of the minority". Most people who go wrong still know deep down what is true. They just "get stuck in the muck".

Rosh Hashanah and the scales

The Rambam says that on Rosh Hashanah each person's merits and sins are weighed: the righteous sealed for life, the wicked for death, and the beinoni left until Yom Kippur. As a child the Rabbi wondered how anyone could have exactly equal misvot and sins. The answer is that this is not a bank balance but a weighing of values and direction. The saddik has a clear vision. The rasha has lost his way. The beinoni is pulled both ways.

His proof came from the laws of marriage. If a man known to be wicked betroths a woman "on condition that I am a complete saddik", the betrothal is treated as possibly valid, because "perhaps he thought of teshuvah in his heart". But the Rambam elsewhere requires verbal confession for teshuvah. The commentators answer that confession is what removes particular sins, but teshuvah is above all a change of vision and identity, and that can happen in a moment. "That's what Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are all about... Who am I? Where am I heading?"

Why, then, does the Torah speak of God's anger? Because that is how we understand. A parent who shouts at a child running into the road is not taking revenge. "I have a vision for you, and you're not living up to it... because I love you."

Not for a prize

Antigonos of Sokho taught: do not be like servants who serve their master in order to receive a reward. Serving God is not collecting prizes. It is making the right choices. Yet his students Sadok and Baitos misunderstood him and concluded there is no World to Come at all. From them came the Sadducees, who denied the Oral Torah.

Reward in this world?

The Talmud (Kiddushin) says there is no reward for misvot in this world. A man climbs a ladder at his father's request to send away a mother bird, two misvot promising long life, and falls to his death. The long life, the Talmud concludes, is in the world that is entirely long.

But the Torah is full of worldly blessings and curses. The Rambam answers in Hilchot Teshuvah (9:1). Those promises are true, but they are not the reward itself. If we keep the Torah with joy, God removes obstacles like sickness, war and famine and grants plenty and peace, so that we are free to study and do misvot and so earn the World to Come. If we abandon the Torah, the things that would help us are taken away.

The Rabbi pointed out how often in this passage the Rambam pairs doing the misvot with understanding them: "according to his deeds and the extent of his knowledge". For the Rambam, Olam HaBa is shared existence with God, and that needs some understanding of God, not only actions.

Why the Torah does not mention Olam HaBa

The Torah never mentions Olam HaBa explicitly, but there are hints. Bilam says "let my end be like his". Avraham "was gathered to his people", though he was the first to be buried in Mearat HaMakhpelah. Yeshayahu says "your dead shall live". The Torah also never explains what God is; some things are taken as given. The Ibn Ezra says most people could not grasp it. The Maharal says the Torah's claims rest on what people experienced, like Sinai, while no one has returned from the World to Come.

Summary

  • Reward and punishment are best understood as the consequences of our own choices, not the acts of a vengeful God.
  • In this life we build the identity we will live with forever; Olam HaBa is sharing existence with God.
  • For most people, "punishment" is the regret of what they could have been; karet, the end of the soul, is for very few.
  • Teshuvah and the Rosh Hashanah judgment are about our vision and direction, not a balance of coins.
  • Worldly blessings help us serve God; the real reward is in the World to Come, and it needs understanding as well as deeds.
