The two early views

The Rabbi recalled where the previous day's class had ended. In the early nineteenth century there were two main opinions on opening and closing an umbrella on Shabbat or Yom Tov, even where there is an eruv, so that carrying is not the issue.

Some permitted it. The umbrella, they said, is an existing tent: you are only opening something already made, like the folding chair the Gemara permits.

Others disagreed. A folding chair may be opened because you use the space on top of it. An umbrella is used for the space underneath it, as shelter from the rain, and that is making a tent. So an umbrella is forbidden on Shabbat.

What became accepted

Over time the prohibition became more and more accepted. This was also the view of the great majority of the Hakhamim of Sefarad, among them the Hida (Rabbi Hayim Yoseph David Azulai), Rabbi David Pardo, the Ben Ish Hai, and more recently Rav Ovadia Yoseph and Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul. The widely accepted practice among both Ashkenazim and Sefaradim is not to open or close an umbrella on Shabbat, even where there is an eruv.

Two more lenient voices

Some Sefaradi authorities took what the Rabbi called "a slightly, and I emphasise, a slightly more permissive approach".

Rav Ben Sion Meir Hai Uziel, the former Chief Rabbi of Israel, wrote that since this is a dispute and we cannot be sure, one should refrain from using an umbrella on Shabbat. But because there are those who permit it, we have no authority to criticise people who do use one. In a dispute, be strict yourself, but there is no need to tell others off.

Rav Yoseph Messas, who came from Morocco and was later Chief Rabbi of Haifa, went further. He wrote that he could not decide between the views, so a God-fearing person should refrain. But if the synagogue is far, the rain is torrential, and waiting for it to stop would mean missing the service, while walking in it would soak one's clothes and might lead to wringing them out, then in such pressing circumstances someone who is lenient has what to rely on. Even so, he wrote that it is better not to.

The main approach of the Hakhamim, the Rabbi stressed, remains that an umbrella may not be used on Shabbat.

An umbrella opened before Shabbat

What if it was opened before Shabbat? Here the tent already exists. Some still forbade carrying it because of marit ayin: people will think you opened it on Shabbat.

Rav Ovadia Yoseph rejects that reason. The Gemara has cases of marit ayin, but after the Talmud was completed later authorities do not have the power to create new decrees of this kind. Still, he says it is uvdin dehol, a weekday activity. It is not in the spirit of Shabbat. So even an umbrella opened before Shabbat is better not used. The Rabbi described this as a meta-halachic consideration, and as the general consensus: not forbidden as making a tent, but not in the spirit of Shabbat.

He mentioned a report he had once read about a community in Frankfurt, in the time of Rabbi Breuer, the student of Rabbi Samson Raphael Hirsch, where a non-Jew stood at the synagogue door to close people's umbrellas as they came in and open them as they left.

A non-Jew holding the umbrella

A non-Jew carrying an umbrella on your behalf is permitted. Asking a non-Jew is allowed here, and many poskim write that a non-Jew may open the umbrella and carry it over you as you walk.

Practical summary

  • Opening and closing an umbrella was debated in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries; the accepted practice among Ashkenazim and Sefaradim is to forbid it, even with an eruv.
  • Rav Uziel held one should refrain, but not criticise those who rely on the lenient view.
  • Rav Yoseph Messas allowed some leniency in pressing circumstances, such as torrential rain on the way to shul, while still advising against it.
  • An umbrella opened before Shabbat: Rav Ovadia Yoseph does not forbid it as marit ayin, but advises against it as a weekday activity.
  • A non-Jew may open and carry an umbrella for you.
