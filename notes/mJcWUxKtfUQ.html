These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Who is wise, strong, wealthy and honoured?

Chapter 4 is long, the Rabbi said, and the Rambam often refers back to the Shemonah Perakim in it, so he did not expect to finish it in one evening.

Mishnah 1. Ben Zoma: who is wise? One who learns from every person. Who is strong? One who conquers his inclination. Who is wealthy? One who is happy with his portion. Who is honoured? One who honours others.

The Rambam refers to Chapter 7 of the eight chapters, where he explained the Gemara that prophecy rests only on one who is wise, strong and wealthy. Human perfection has two parts, intellectual and moral. The hakham has perfected his intellect. The gibor has self-discipline over all his traits. The ashir is singled out because contentment with one's life is one of the most important traits of all. Honouring others, getting on with people, is another.

The Rabbi found it striking that ordinary people define all four externally: the wise man has many students, the strong man conquers cities, the wealthy man has a big house and a car, the honoured man is stood up for. The Mishnah turns each one inward. "The only person which we can change in life is ourselves."

The reward of a misvah is the misvah

Mishnah 2. Ben Azai: run to a light misvah as to a severe one and flee from sin, for one misvah leads to another and one sin leads to another; the reward of a misvah is a misvah, and the reward of a sin is a sin.

The Rabbi called it a grave mistake to keep the picture of reward we teach children. Rightly, we give a child a sweet or five pounds for coming to shul on time. But many adults keep that picture and imagine Olam HaBa as a bank vault, each misvah adding coins and each sin deducting them. Olam HaBa, as we saw in the Shemonah Perakim and as the Ramhal explains at length, is the identity a person creates. The greatest reward for a misvah is that you have become that person.

He repeated two analogies he has used before:

  • The stadium. HaShem is, so to speak, in the middle of the pitch. The more a person has perfected his intellect and character, the closer his seat. Sin moves you further back. Someone so far removed that he cannot enter at all has karet.
  • The apple. An apple bruised all over is thrown out: that is karet, when the identity simply ceases. One with some bruises needs cutting: that is Gehinam, at most twelve months. One with few marks needs only gentle care, and gets a front seat.

"God is not a vending machine." He preferred not to call it "reward" at all, since a reward is arbitrary, like a holiday for passing exams. Going to the gym and getting fit is not a reward but a result. That is what misvot are.

The Rambam picks out the word run: do misvot with alacrity. Moshe set aside the three cities of refuge east of the Jordan though they would not work until all six existed, fourteen years later, in a land he would never enter. He had the opportunity now, and took it.

Mishnah 3. Do not despise any person or dismiss anything, for every person has his hour and every thing its place. "Never burn your bridges."

"Be very, very humble"

Mishnah 4. Rabbi Levitas of Yavneh: be very, very low of spirit, for the hope of man is the worm. Here, the Rabbi said, the Rambam "waxes lyrical".

To understand it he went back to Chapter 4 of the eight chapters. Every trait has two bad extremes and a good middle, and the wise person keeps to the middle. Because staying exactly in the middle is so hard, the pious lean slightly towards the safer extreme, for example slightly generous rather than slightly mean.

He asked the class for the opposite of arrogance. Someone answered humility. Not so: anavah, humility, is the middle. The opposite extreme is shefal ruah, lowness of spirit. The Rambam says this is the one trait where the hakhamim told us not to stay in the middle at all, because arrogance is so dangerous. Go all the way to the other extreme.

The story. The Rambam quotes a pious man asked about the happiest day of his life. He was travelling in the lowest class of a ship, and a wealthy passenger, regarding him as worthless, relieved himself on him. He was astonished at such arrogance, but then realised that it had not touched him at all, and that was his great joy. That, says the Rambam, is lowness of spirit.

What lowness of spirit is, and is not

The Rabbi warned against misunderstanding. Lowness of spirit does not mean thinking of yourself as nothing.

  • The word anavah. He cited a Hasidic explanation from anah, to answer: humility is a response to what you have been given. "Your talent is God's gift to you. What you do with it is your gift back to God." Humility and confidence go together.
  • Rav Yosef. When the Mishnah said humility ended with the death of a great sage, Rav Yosef said: do not say that, for I am here. That is not hypocrisy. Humility is knowing who you are and responding to it.
  • Hurt is still the middle. Feeling upset when someone belittles the gifts HaShem gave you is still anavah, still the right path.
  • Two pockets. A Hasidic rebbe carried two slips of paper: "For me the world was created" and "I am dust and ashes."
  • A saying he loves: "Humility is not thinking less of yourself but thinking of yourself less."
  • Rabbi Sacks and the Rebbe. Rabbi Sacks described meeting the Lubavitcher Rebbe as a student: what struck him was that in the room "all there was was me". Some people make every conversation about themselves; others need no affirmation and make you feel the world is about you.

Lowness of spirit, then, is having so little ego that whatever others do to you does not change how you see yourself, "because I know my own worth".

A participant asked whether "Moshe was very humble" means he was unaffected by what was said about him concerning his wife. "Precisely," said the Rabbi, and the Rambam says this at the end of the piece. The verse sits in the middle of the story, between Miriam and Aharon's criticism and HaShem's response, to teach that Moshe was not offended. They were punished for how prophets should not speak, not for upsetting him. "I'm not saying that we can do this. I promise you I'm not. We all have huge amounts to work on in this area." But we need to know the destination.

Hazal on humility and arrogance

The Rambam gathers sayings of Hazal. Humility is greater than wisdom, and whenever the Torah describes HaShem's greatness it also describes His humility. Moshe reached perfection of intellect and character, yet HaShem praised him above all for humility, and he said "what are we?" David said HaShem will not despise "a broken and crushed heart". The Rabbi said that verse always moves him. Hazal compare arrogance to idolatry and forbidden relations.

Rava and Rav Nahman. Rava said a scholar should keep an eighth of an eighth of pride. The Rabbi pictured a scale of 64, where 1 is the most arrogant: Rava says go to 63, not 64. Rav Nahman said not even that, and the Rambam rules like him. The destination is shefal ruah. "If we get to anavah, we should be very happy with ourselves." But there is a further step.

Hillul HaShem. Whoever profanes HaShem's name in secret is punished in public. The Rambam clarifies that unintentional profanation is far less severe than deliberate, but because of its gravity the consequence is still public.

A teaser for next week

The Rabbi read the opening of the Rambam's next piece, on Mishnah 5. The Rambam says he decided not to comment, because it is clear and because most of the great rabbis of his time, perhaps all, would be upset. Then he changed his mind and wrote it anyway, without regard for earlier or present authorities. The Rabbi said the Rambam wrote this in his early twenties. Straight after a long piece on humility, it shows exactly what lowness of spirit means: total confidence in the truth, unmoved by what others think. That piece was left for the next class.

Summary

  • Wisdom, strength, wealth and honour are defined internally; the only person we can change is ourselves.
  • The reward of a misvah is the misvah: Olam HaBa is the identity we create, not a bank of points.
  • Every trait has a middle path, except arrogance, where the hakhamim say go to the far extreme.
  • Lowness of spirit is not low self-worth but freedom from ego; Moshe is the model.
  • The Rambam rules like Rav Nahman: not even a trace of pride.
