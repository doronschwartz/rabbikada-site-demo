Topic: Opening and carrying an umbrella on Shabbat and Yom Tov: the principles, the history of the debate, and the contemporary ruling Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 315; Noda BiYehudah; Hatam Sofer; Hazon Ish Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Torah Way, North West London, Tuesday 25 May 2021 (14 Sivan)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A topical theme

After weeks of miserable weather, the Rabbi chose the most topical subject he could think of: umbrellas on Shabbat. He traced the history of the question, the approaches of Sefaradi and Ashkenazi poskim, and the ruling followed today.

The question only arose in the mid-eighteenth century, when umbrellas became common in Europe, where it rains. It reached the poskim of the Middle East much later. He stressed at the outset that the whole discussion applies only where there is an eruv, or on Yom Tov. Without an eruv, carrying an umbrella on Shabbat is forbidden anyway, possibly even by Torah law.

The principles: a temporary tent

Building is one of the thirty-nine melakhot. A permanent tent (ohel keva) is forbidden by Torah law. Hazal also forbade a temporary tent (ohel arai). According to most authorities this is rabbinic, though the Rif seems to imply it may be Torah law.

An ohel means a roof covering a space of at least a tefah (about 8 cm) underneath. A roof with walls is forbidden. A roof on poles without walls is normally permitted, with one exception: if it is put up to protect from the sun or the rain (lehagen), it is forbidden even without walls.

His example: laying a book across two piles of books creates a space underneath, but it is permitted, because there are no walls and nothing is being protected. If you were doing it to store or hide something underneath, it would be a problem.

Kisei traskal: a tent that is already made

The Gemara in Shabbat discusses a folding chair (kisei traskal), which may be opened and closed on Shabbat even though it creates a space underneath. Rashi explains that you are not making anything: the ohel already exists, and you are only stretching it open. This is ruled in the Shulhan Arukh (315:5). Modern examples are a folding table or a pram hood. For a pram, the poskim advise leaving a tefah open before Shabbat, to avoid the question altogether.

How far does this go? The Magen Avraham permits putting up a ready-made huppah that simply opens out. The Hazon Ish understood that this is because it is a kisei traskal. But the Beit Meir and many Aharonim disagree: a kisei traskal is permitted only when it is not used for protection from the sun or rain. The huppah is allowed because it is put up for honour, not as real shelter.

The Beit Meir adds a further principle. A covering that exists only while someone holds it up, such as a tallit stretched over a couple, is not an ohel at all.

The first responsa

The first teshuvah on umbrellas he knows of is the Mikhtam LeDavid, by Rav David Pardo of Venice. Some umbrellas of that time had to be tied, which raises its own problems.

The Noda BiYehudah (Tinyana) ruled firmly that even an umbrella that opens by itself is forbidden. It is a kisei traskal, but a kisei traskal may not be used for protection, and an umbrella exists to protect from rain. He holds the prohibition is rabbinic. He wrote that he spoke about it publicly and his community stopped.

The Hatam Sofer, surprisingly, was lenient, though he did not advise it himself. Among his reasons: an umbrella stands only while someone holds it, so it is not an ohel; and its sides do not reach the ground, as the walls of a tent must. He also noted that many people used them. Another authority wrote that he would have liked to be lenient, but could not bring himself to rule against the Noda BiYehudah.

How the ruling became settled

Over time the consensus of both Sefaradi and Ashkenazi poskim became that umbrellas are forbidden: the Maharsham, the Ben Ish Hai, and today the Shemirat Shabbat KeHilkhatah and Rav Ovadia Yoseph, who writes that those who use them are breaching an accepted fence.

The Hazon Ish is especially interesting. Since he held that a kisei traskal is permitted even for protection, he had no technical reason to forbid an umbrella. He tried other arguments, but concluded that it has become the established practice of Am Yisrael not to use an umbrella on Shabbat, and permitting it would be "peris geder", breaching a fence. A rav should not permit something people regard as forbidden.

Four points to know

  1. Opened before Shabbat. If the umbrella was open before Shabbat and is carried within an eruv, no ohel is being made. Some still forbid it because of marit ayin. But Rav Ovadia Yoseph often stresses that we cannot invent new decrees, and Rav Hayim Sonnenfeld (Salmat Hayim) wrote that he would not recommend it, but one need not rebuke someone who does it.
  2. Asking a non-Jew. The Mikhtam LeDavid and the Hida permit asking a non-Jew to open the umbrella and carry it alongside you. The Rabbi mentioned a report of a Frankfurt synagogue where a non-Jew opened and closed the congregants' umbrellas on rainy days.
  3. Lesser-known Sefaradi voices. Rav Ben Siyon Meir Hai Uziel (Mishpetei Uziel), Rishon LeSiyon some seventy years ago, forbids it but would not criticise those who are lenient. Rav Yoseph Messas of Morocco (Mayim Hayim) calls it a real dispute: one should normally put up with the rain, but someone whose synagogue is far away in heavy rain may be lenient. Rav Yishak Yoseph (2015) says it is better not to, and that common sense must guide each case.
  4. Stationary umbrellas. Rav Ben Siyon Abba Shaul forbids a hand umbrella, but a pram canopy or garden parasol that was open before Shabbat is fine: one may push the pram within an eruv or sit under the parasol. One may not open it on Shabbat.

Practical summary

  • The discussion applies only within an eruv or on Yom Tov.
  • A temporary tent that protects from rain or sun is rabbinically forbidden, even when it is ready-made (according to most poskim).
  • After two centuries of debate, the accepted ruling of Sefaradim and Ashkenazim is not to open or use an umbrella on Shabbat.
  • An umbrella opened before Shabbat is less problematic; some authorities say one need not protest.
  • A non-Jew may open and carry an umbrella for you.
  • A pram canopy or garden parasol left open from before Shabbat may be used, but not opened.
