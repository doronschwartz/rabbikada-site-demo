Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 15, Seifim 16-26 (aunts and great-aunts, daughters-in-law, stepsons, brothers' wives, stepbrothers, the father-in-law's wife, nephews' wives, and the wife's sister) Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 19 March 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi said the plan was to work through the remaining relatives one at a time from Seif 16, and that from Seif 27 the Siman becomes one large sugya from Yevamot about kerovot and similar cases. He then summarized the categories learned so far:

  • Seif 1: a Torah prohibition means the kiddushin does not take effect and no get is needed. A rabbinic one means the kiddushin takes effect and a get is needed.
  • Seifim 2-7: mother and stepmother. Hazal add further generations. The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh continue the prohibition endlessly only along the same gender, while Rashi holds it always continues.
  • Seifim 8-9: aunts through marriage. Only the wife of your father's paternal brother is forbidden by the Torah; the other three are rabbinic.
  • Seifim 10-11: a sister is forbidden in all cases, but a stepsister may be married even if they lived together.
  • Seifim 12-15: the wife's daughter, granddaughter, mother and grandmother. The Shulhan Arukh rules that the sheniyot continue without a break, but also cites the Rambam, who holds there is a break.

Seifim 16-17: blood aunts, cousins and nieces

The sister of your father and the sister of your mother are forbidden by the Torah, however the siblings are related, whether through the father or only through the mother. So all four kinds of direct aunt are forbidden by the Torah, unlike the aunts through marriage, where only one of four is.

You may marry the daughter of your uncle on either side, so cousins are permitted. An uncle may marry his niece, and the Rema says later that this is even a misvah. The Rabbi added: "Obviously we don't advise to do nowadays because of the genetic concerns."

Seif 18: the next generation, great-aunts

The first lines repeat Seifim 8-9, but the Shulhan Arukh brings them again in order to move to the next generation. The Rabbi went through it slowly and offered to repeat.

Aunts through marriage. Only one of the four is a Torah prohibition, so you would expect Hazal to add a sheniyah only there. The other three are already rabbinic, and a sheniyah on a sheniyah is not added. The Shulhan Arukh says that the wife of your grandfather's brother in these three cases is permitted, and this is agreed.

Blood aunts, and the one Torah case through marriage. Here the prohibition is from the Torah, so you would expect the next generation to be forbidden by Hazal. The Shulhan Arukh rules that the wife of a great-uncle and the great-aunts are permitted, and adds "yesh omerim" that it is forbidden.

Forbidden

The Rabbi said the logic seems to favour Rabbenu Tam, since this is a typical sheniyah. But the Gemara apparently felt the relationship was already too distant and left a doubt. A doubt in a rabbinic matter is decided leniently, as the Rambam says, and the Shulhan Arukh rules that way. He called Rabbenu Tam the outlier.

A point from the commentator on the double rabbinic case. The wife of your mother's brother, where your mother shares the relationship with him through their mother, is rabbinic twice over. Some wanted to be lenient, so that a man who had already married her could stay with her. The commentator rejects this, since the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh treat it as a prohibition like the others.

Seifim 19-20: the daughter-in-law and further generations

The wife of your son is forbidden by the Torah. Hazal add the next generation down, so the wife of your grandson is forbidden rabbinically, and the Gemara says this continues without end. The Rabbi noted that, taken literally, the wives of Yaakov Avinu's descendants would be forbidden to him. This is what troubled the Ramban in the earlier discussion of the wife's descendants: how can the Gemara apply a rule to your own descendants' wives when you cannot marry your own great-great-granddaughter? The Ramban answered that the Gemara's case was only the wife's side, and that the descendants' wives show a fortiori that your own are forbidden.

This applies when the line comes down through a son. For the son of your daughter, the daughter's son's wife is forbidden rabbinically (one sheniyah), but the next generation's wife is permitted. Anything coming down through a daughter stops after that one step.

Seif 21: the stepson's wife

The wife of your wife's son from an earlier marriage is permitted, even to be married at the same time as your wife. The stepson may likewise marry his stepfather's wife. The Rabbi called this straightforward.

Seif 22: the brother's wife

The wife of your brother is forbidden in all circumstances, even after he divorces her and even after he dies, unlike your wife's sister, who is permitted after death. The one exception is yibbum, when he dies childless. Even there the permission applies only to a brother through the father, which is one reason the havhanah issues matter: with a doubt about the father the yibbum itself is in doubt.

Seifim 23-24: stepbrothers, and the father-in-law's wife

Two stepbrothers who grew up in one house may each marry the other's wife after a divorce. This is like the permission to marry a stepsister in Seif 11. The Bavli says there is no concern for marit ayin, since people know they are stepbrothers. The Yerushalmi forbids both cases, but the Bavli takes priority and the Rishonim rule accordingly.

The same applies to the former wife of your father-in-law, who is not the mother of your wife. The Yerushalmi forbids it for marit ayin, and gives two possible reasons: people would think she is your mother-in-law, or they would confuse your father-in-law with your father and think you married your father's wife, which is a Torah prohibition. The Rabbi did not think the second reason was the simple one.

The question is whether the Bavli's rejection of marit ayin for stepbrothers also covers this case. One could say yes. Or one could say the Gemara rejected it there only because step-relatives living together are well known, while a father-in-law's wife is less known, so concern remains.

Permitted

The Rabbi mentioned the story that Rabbenu Tam stopped a wedding at the meal for this reason, and that the Rosh cites a wedding cancelled in Provence. The Beit Yoseph notes that the custom was to be lenient. The Shulhan Arukh rules that it is permitted, and brings the other view as yesh omerim.

Two further points: - The Beit Shemuel says that if the couple already married, perhaps on the advice of a rabbi, one can be lenient. The reasons are that the concern is only rabbinic marit ayin, and that the stringent view is a minority. - Rav Ovadia Yoseph has a responsum on a case where the man's own wife had died. The Rabbi explained that the Yerushalmi's isha ubitah concern applies only while she is alive. Rav Ovadia says Ashkenazim should follow the Beit Shemuel, and if they already married, one may be lenient.

Seif 25: the nephew's wife and the niece

You may marry the wife of the son of your brother and the wife of the son of your sister, that is, your nephew's wife. This follows from the permission to marry cousins. Likewise you may marry your niece, and it is even a misvah, though it is not done today.

A question from the class: the laws of witnesses. A participant noted that relatives are disqualified as witnesses in a much wider way. The Rabbi agreed that the laws of witnesses are stricter in some respects, but there is no concept of "it never stops" (eino hefsek) as there is with arayot. He recalled that the Rishonim disagreed on how far down a relative is disqualified, and said it is interesting that the two areas work differently.

Seif 26: the wife's sister, and the Rema on a rumor

The sister of your wife is forbidden by the Torah while your wife is alive. This is the one ervah that becomes permitted after a death.

The Rema concludes the Siman: all these relatives through marriage are forbidden even if the marriage was only a rumor (kol). The Rabbi asked the class where else in marriage law a rumor is enough. They recalled that a kiddushin made because of a rumor leads to a rabbinic prohibition, and that a woman who was married on a rumor, divorced, and then married another man may not return to the first. He said that this makes four places where a rumor matters. He did not start Seif 27, and the class ended there.

Practical summary

  • All blood aunts, the sisters of a father or mother, are forbidden by the Torah however the siblings are related. Cousins may be married, and an uncle may marry his niece, though this is not advised today.
  • For great-aunts, the Shulhan Arukh permits, citing Rabbenu Tam's view that they are forbidden.
  • The wife of a son is forbidden by the Torah and the prohibition continues down the male line without end. Through a daughter it stops after one step.
  • A stepson's wife, a stepbrother's wife and a nephew's wife are permitted. A brother's wife is forbidden always, except in yibbum.
  • The father-in-law's former wife (not the mother of one's wife) is permitted in the Shulhan Arukh, but Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh forbid it. The Beit Shemuel and Rav Ovadia Yoseph allow leniency after the fact.
  • The wife's sister is forbidden only during the wife's life, and a rumor of marriage is enough to create the prohibition on all these relatives.
