Topic: What Jews mean when they say they believe in God Sources: The Ramhal, Derech Hashem 1:1; the Rambam, Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 1:1-5 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 29 January 2024, the opening class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why a series on the fundamentals

Dayan Kada opened the series by explaining why it was needed. People say they believe in Judaism, or that they believe in God, but very few can say what God is or is not. "It doesn't make much sense saying you believe in something if you don't understand what you are believing in."

The coming classes would deal with questions we rarely stop to think about in our busy lives: why Hashem created the universe, what the World to Come is, reward and punishment, and why good people sometimes suffer while others seem to do well. Before all of these, he said, we have to start with the fundamental of the fundamentals: HaKadosh Barukh Hu.

He held up the source sheet. When he printed it, the front page came out blank. By the end of the evening, he said, they would see that this was fitting.

The Ramhal and the importance of structure

The series is based mainly on Derech Hashem by Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto, the Ramhal (1707-1746). Dayan Kada gave a short introduction to him. He was a great scholar in Venice by the age of twenty and also taught Kabbalah. This was only a few decades after the false messiah Shabbetai Svi, whose movement had relied heavily on Kabbalah, and the rabbis of the time were wary of a young man teaching such things. The Ramhal moved to Amsterdam, where he wrote many of his books, and in 1743 settled in Akko in the north of the Land of Israel, where he died in a plague a few years later.

He described the Ramhal as a rationalist and a Kabbalist at once. Nothing in Derech Hashem is his own invention: he gathers the words of Hazal and the Rishonim and organises them into a clear path.

The Ramhal's introduction compares knowledge with structure to a garden laid out in beds and paths, and knowledge without structure to a thicket of reeds. The plants may be the same, but one is a pleasure to walk through and the other is a burden. A mind full of unconnected details "toils, despairs, tires, and has no pleasure."

Dayan Kada said this is one of his main complaints about Jewish education: people are taught many details but never the framework, and like students cramming for an exam, they forget them a month later. Hazal taught that Torah should be held as principles, each containing many details. Someone who knows the twenty principles of Hilchot Shabbat understands it far better than someone with a muddle of rulings.

Around the table: what does God mean to you?

He then asked everyone present to define God, and showed how quickly the answers ran into trouble.

  • "God is everywhere." Then is God in the table? No, God created the table. So He is not everywhere?
  • "Our Father." Why only a father and not a mother? It was agreed that God has no gender, so the word is a way of speaking.
  • "He protects us." Then how do we explain the tragedies of history?
  • "He is a part of each of us." Dayan Kada warned that this is dangerous. To say Hashem has parts is "on your way to heresy." Every soul comes from Hashem, but that does not make the soul a piece of God. He said that this idea needs careful explanation and is not the classical understanding.
  • "He is in heaven." He is not in heaven any more than He is here. We look up when we pray just as we naturally look up to a tall person and give him more gravitas. It is a way of speaking, not a location.

The point of the exercise was to show that people who go to synagogue and send their children to Jewish schools still struggle to say what God is. Someone asked whether we must all agree. He answered that everyone is free to think, but his role was to teach what the traditional sources say.

Asked whether they would be certain by the end of the series, he said belief in God and Torah is "beyond reasonable doubt". He was confident their belief would be stronger by the end.

The Ramhal and the Rambam define God

Derech Hashem begins: every Jew must believe and know that there is a first being, pre-existing and eternal, who brought into being and brings into being everything that exists, "and this is God." The Rambam opens the Mishneh Torah in almost the same words: the foundation of all foundations is to know that there is a first being, the Masui Rishon, who brought everything into existence. If nothing else existed, He would still exist, because everything needs Him and He needs nothing.

Dayan Kada pointed out that both describe this being first and only then say: this is what we call God.

To believe and to know. You would never say "I believe that two plus two equals four." You know it. We begin with emunah, belief, and our task is to turn that belief into knowledge, a real inner sense of Hashem. The misvot are tools for this. We stop work on Shabbat each week to remember that there is a Masui Rishon who created the world, and that the world had a beginning.

Brought into being and brings into being. When a person builds a computer and sells it, there is no further connection between them. Creation is not like that. Every moment the universe exists is because Hashem is willing it, not because He willed it once in the past.

Eternal. Everything physical, even the sun and the farthest galaxies, will one day come to an end. Something spiritual does not.

Beyond time. Time itself is a creation. On how God can know the future while we have free choice, the Rambam's approach is that the question only arises because we live in past, present and future, while Hashem is above time.

Perfect, independent, simple, one

Perfect. He posed the old puzzle: can God create a rock so heavy He cannot lift it? After a lively discussion, he gave the answer. We hear the word "can't" and think it means a weakness, but that is not always so. Hashem cannot catch a cold, and He cannot make Himself disappear. That is not an imperfection; it is part of His perfection.

Necessary and independent. We exist, but we do not have to exist. Hashem must exist. If the whole universe ceased to be, nothing about Him would change.

Simple. A person can be clever, compassionate and short-sighted, all separate qualities. We cannot give Hashem separate attributes. Someone asked about the Thirteen Attributes we repeat on Yom Kippur. Following the Rambam, he explained that these describe how we experience Hashem's providence, all as one, and that we repeat them so that by the end of Yom Kippur we become compassionate, merciful and slow to anger ourselves.

No body. When the Torah says Hashem stretched out His hand over Egypt, it speaks in human language. "Hashem cannot make a hand for Himself," because anything with a boundary is a limit. However large you imagine something, doubling it again and again, it still ends somewhere. Someone raised verses such as "Let us make man" and "face to face." He agreed that such verses exist, and said the Talmud already asks about them; we read them with the interpretation of Hazal, the Rambam and the Ramhal. "Face to face" means the most direct spiritual connection a human being has ever had.

The blank page

The more we try to pin Hashem down, he said, the further we get from Him. The most we can say is this: the first existence, Creator and still creating, eternal, perfect, independent, simple and one. And even this is limited by our language.

That is why the empty front page was fitting. Many observant people, he said, picture Hashem on a throne with a long white beard, because of the imagery in our prayers about rising from the throne of judgement to the throne of mercy. The imagery helps us relate, but we must not stay with it. When he prays, he said, he thinks of himself as speaking to existence: everything, including himself, though not as parts of God. When three people sit together and speak words of Torah, the Shekhinah is with them. This means that they become more in touch with that existence, not that Hashem has moved from up there to down here.

He warned that this was the hardest and vaguest class of the series, "because how can finite talk about infinite?" The next one, on why Hashem created the world, would be far more tangible.

Summary

  • Belief needs understanding, and understanding needs a framework of principles, not a pile of details.
  • The Ramhal and the Rambam both define God first as the Masui Rishon, the first existence on whom everything depends, and only then give Him a name.
  • Hashem is Creator in the present, eternal, above time, perfect, necessary, independent, simple and one, with no body and no parts.
  • Some things Hashem "cannot" do are not weaknesses but part of His perfection.
  • The imagery of Torah and prayer helps us relate, but true closeness comes from letting go of the pictures and turning to Hashem as all of existence.
