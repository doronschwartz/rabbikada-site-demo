Topic: When Shabbat begins on Friday: Bein HaShemashot, Rabbeinu Tam and the Geonim, and what to do in practice

Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 261:1-2, with the Mishnah Berurah

Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Rabbi Bassous's shul, part 2 of a Hilchot Shabbat course (recorded around 2013-15, exact date not stated).

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two Gemaras that seem to disagree

The class begins with a contradiction. The Gemara in Pesahim says that from shkiah (sunset) until Set HaKokhavim (nightfall) is four mil. At 18 minutes a mil, that is 72 minutes. But the Gemara in Shabbat says that Bein HaShemashot, the twilight in between, is three quarters of a mil, which is 13 and a half minutes. So is twilight 72 minutes or 13 and a half?

Rabbeinu Tam

Rabbeinu Tam solves it by saying there are two sunsets. Tehilat Shkiah is the sunset we see, when the sun starts to go down. From then until nightfall is 72 minutes, but it is still full day. Sof Shkiah, the end of the sun's setting, comes about 58 and a half minutes later. Only then does Bein HaShemashot begin, and it lasts 13 and a half minutes, ending at the 72 minutes.

The Rabbi gives an example. If sunset is at 4.15, then 72 minutes later is 5.27. For Rabbeinu Tam the day continues until about 5.13, and Bein HaShemashot runs from there to 5.27.

The Geonim

The Geonim (including Rav Hai Gaon and Rav Sherira Gaon) explain the Gemaras differently. For them Bein HaShemashot starts straight away at sunset and lasts 13 and a half minutes, and then it is night. The four mil in Pesahim means that all the stars will have come out by 72 minutes, which has no practical halakhic consequence. Night is defined by three small stars, which appear much earlier.

Everyone agrees that Bein HaShemashot is 13 and a half minutes. The only question is when it starts: at the beginning of sunset (the Geonim) or near the end of 72 minutes (Rabbeinu Tam).

What the Shulhan Arukh says, and the custom

The Shulhan Arukh rules like Rabbeinu Tam: the day continues for a long time after the visible sunset, and Bein HaShemashot only begins about 58 and a half minutes later. Yet most Sephardim today do not rely on this and hold from the moment of sunset like the Geonim. Why?

The Hida already asked this. His answer is that the ancient custom of Sephardim, even in the time of the Beit Yoseph, was like the Geonim. The Shulhan Arukh records what comes out of the Gemara, but the custom had long been otherwise, and the custom is what we follow. So no melakhah may be done from sunset, and a person should accept Shabbat a few minutes before.

A questioner pointed out that Rav Ovadia Yoseph always insisted on ruling like Maran. The Rabbi said it is a good question, but that Rav Ovadia says clearly here that we do not follow Maran: the custom is like the Geonim, and a long-standing custom is very weighty. He noted that in prayer, too, we sometimes follow custom against Maran.

He tells a story from the Tzitz Eliezer. A man in Jerusalem who was strict to follow Rabbeinu Tam died on Shabbat morning, and his widow asked the Hevra Kadisha to change the order of burial out of respect for his stringency. The ruling was that since the custom of the place had always been like the Geonim, there was no reason to be strict for him, and he was dealt with in his turn.

The third opinion: the Yereim, and the time to stop

The Mishnah Berurah brings a third view, that of the Yereim. For them Bein HaShemashot starts 13 and a half minutes before sunset, so already at that point it is doubtful whether it is day or night. That is why the custom in England is to light candles 15 minutes before sunset. The Mishnah Berurah says that 20 minutes before sunset satisfies every opinion, even those who hold a mil is 24 minutes. In Israel the Sephardi custom is about 20 minutes, and some are strict for 40, as in Jerusalem. The Rabbi says 20 minutes is more than enough.

Questions from the class

Praying Minhah after sunset. During Bein HaShemashot it is a doubt whether it is day or night (a safek sfeika), so Minhah may still be prayed in this period, and for Rabbeinu Tam it is still full day.

Running late. Someone is stuck in traffic and the printed time arrives. The Rabbi says he must stop driving and leave the car at the side of the road. He may possibly ask a non-Jew to park it nearby in a case of real need, but not to take it home. The printed time in the calendar is already 15 minutes before sunset. Using a few minutes beyond it is for a pressing need, perhaps once in a few months, and not something to do every week. From sunset itself, nothing may be done by oneself.

Accepting Shabbat

How do you accept Shabbat? You say that you are accepting it now. The Shulhan Arukh brings a dispute whether accepting it in the heart alone works. Some say it does, but it is better to say it.

This matters especially in winter. If candle lighting is at 4.00 and sunset is at 4.15, and you come to Minhah at 4.00, you cannot accept Shabbat before praying Minhah, because once you accept Shabbat you cannot pray the weekday prayer. By the time you finish Minhah it will already be sunset, too late to accept Shabbat. So the Rabbi suggests that during the Amidah repetition, or just after it, you think or say "I am accepting Shabbat" for the earlier acceptance. If you did not accept at all, the Acharonim say that the minimum of acceptance happens by itself so long as you do no melakhah. And someone who accepts ten minutes before should say that he is making the acceptance for the addition to Shabbat.

Practical summary

  • The Gemaras on sunset and nightfall are reconciled by Rabbeinu Tam (two sunsets) and by the Geonim (twilight starts at sunset). Everyone agrees twilight lasts 13 and a half minutes.
  • Although the Shulhan Arukh follows Rabbeinu Tam, Sephardi custom since early times follows the Geonim, so no melakhah from sunset.
  • Ashkenazi custom, following the Mishnah Berurah and the Yereim, is to stop 15 minutes before sunset (England), and 20 minutes satisfies all views.
  • A person running late should not drive on after the printed time. Only pressing need, and rarely, justifies a few extra minutes, and nothing may be done by oneself from sunset.
  • Accept Shabbat explicitly a few minutes before sunset. If you must pray Minhah first, accept Shabbat during or just after the Amidah repetition.
