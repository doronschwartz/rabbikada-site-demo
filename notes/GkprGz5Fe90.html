These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review: a mistake found at the end of an aliyah

The class opened with questions on the previous week. If a mistake is found in the Sefer Torah at the end of the third aliyah, the first three aliyot stand, and the fourth person is called from a new scroll. If the mistake was in a verse not yet read, those verses are read from the new scroll.

No skipping in the Torah (144:1)

It is forbidden, within one reading, to skip from one part of the Torah to another. On Mondays and Thursdays any ten verses may be read, but not five here and five there. It is disrespectful to the congregation and to the Torah, and people become confused.

The exception is when both passages are on the same topic. On Yom Kippur the Kohen Gadol read from Aharei Mot and then from Emor, because both speak of Yom Kippur. On a fast day the Kohen reads from the beginning of the passage in Ki Tisa and the next aliyah from its end, and "people don't realise" that the scroll is rolled forward two or three pages in between. This happens between aliyot, so no one is kept waiting.

Someone asked whether, on a Monday or Thursday, a reader who does not know that week's portion could read a portion he does know. The Rabbi said that arguably he could, since there is no requirement on those days to read the coming Shabbat's portion. The purpose is only that three days do not pass without reading. Skipping applies in either direction.

He added that whenever one reads from the Torah, every word must be read from the scroll. He has seen many bar misvah boys who remember the tune, lose their place, and carry on reciting. He trains his students to read from the text.

Skipping in the Prophets

In the Prophets one may skip within the same book, because they are narrative and rebuke, provided the congregation is not kept waiting. The Shulhan Arukh says one may not skip from one book of the Prophets to another, except within the Twelve Minor Prophets.

Yet we do this regularly:

  • When Shabbat Rosh Hodesh coincides with one of the four special Shabbatot, we read that Haftarah and add the first and last verses of the Rosh Hodesh Haftarah from Yeshayahu.
  • When there is a hatan, some verses are added from the Haftarah of Yeshayahu.

The Mishnah Berurah explains that the concern was the time spent rolling while people waited. Now the Prophets are printed, so there is nothing to wait for. "You see how the halacha adapts to changing times." He said the Rema records that Ashkenazim do not add the extra verses.

The word Haftarah

The Rabbi insisted on the pronunciation: Haftarah, not "Haftorah." The word comes from the same root as "ein maftirin ahar haPesah afikoman" in the Haggadah, meaning to conclude or release, like patur, exempt. The Haftarah is the conclusion of the reading. Someone suggested the name may have come later. "That's a good point."

Do not roll the Sefer Torah in public (144:3)

One may not roll the Sefer Torah in front of the congregation, mipnei kevod haSibur. If there is only one scroll and two portions must be read, as on Shabbat Rosh Hodesh, there is no choice.

The Mishnah Berurah allows rolling a second scroll while the first is being read, since no one is waiting. Still, it is better to prepare everything before the service, and Rav Ovadia Yosef writes the same. Someone, whether the hazan, the rabbi or the gabbai, should be responsible for checking the scrolls at the start of the service.

"It's never respect for the people just sitting and waiting doing nothing." Someone asked whether this applies on Shabbat, when no one is rushing to work. The Rabbi said kevod haSibur applies always: "You start on time, you finish on time, things are swift."

Calling one person to two scrolls (144:4)

Is there anything wrong with calling the same person for two aliyot? In principle, no. The issue is calling him to a second Sefer Torah after the first, because people will think there was a problem with the first scroll. The Mishnah Berurah brings a dispute about a person called last in the first scroll and first in the next. Our practice is not to call the same person to both scrolls at all. If someone was called in one synagogue and then again elsewhere the same morning, there is no concern.

Someone asked how this fits with the same Kohen being called for both Kohen and Levi when there is no Levi. The Rabbi answered that calling a different Kohen would suggest a problem with the first Kohen's lineage.

Taking out the wrong scroll

What if the wrong Sefer Torah is taken out? Returning it may cast a slur on that scroll. Rolling it costs the congregation's time. There are poskim on both sides. The usual practice is to roll it, but if someone did return it, "it's not the end of the world," and there is plenty to rely on.

Someone asked why the Torah cares so much about what people might say. The Rabbi answered that the Hakhamim went to great lengths only for fundamental things such as lineage, the honour of the Torah and Shabbat. Sometimes those safeguards look far-fetched, but when something is foundational one protects it.

He added that whoever opens the Ark should close it, and whoever carries the Sefer Torah out should carry it back, unless he needs to leave. Two people doing it jointly is fine.

The meturgeman (Siman 145)

In the times of the Gemara each verse was translated aloud into Aramaic. The reader read one verse at a time and waited for the translator, and the translator waited for the congregation to finish hearing the verse. Neither raised his voice above the other, and the reader did not prompt the translator, so people would not think the translation was written in the Torah.

A child may read with an adult translating, but an adult should not translate for a child. There was a lively discussion about whose honour this protects. The Rabbi's answer: the honour of the adult, who should not be explaining a child's words, "like a child giving a shiur and someone else has to explain it."

The Mishnah Berurah notes that we no longer translate, since people do not understand Aramaic, and the Targum had a special status. The Rabbi said there is still an argument for helping people understand. In Wembley and that year at Tiferet Eyal he introduced a short English synopsis of the parashah at the start of the reading. At a recent teen-led Shabbat, girls gave a synopsis before each aliyah.

Leaving while the Sefer Torah is open (146:1)

The class ended with the first paragraph of the next siman. It is forbidden to leave while the Sefer Torah is open. One may leave between aliyot. The Mishnah Berurah quotes the verse "those who forsake Hashem will perish," and applies it even to someone who has already heard the reading, and even when ten remain.

If you are desperate for the bathroom, of course go, but better in the break. Between aliyot leave only if there is a need, provided ten remain and you return. The Beur Halacha writes that once the scroll has been opened and shown to the congregation for reading, one should not leave. Someone asked about arriving late: "On the contrary, come in when you can, so you can hear the Sefer Torah."

Practical summary

  • Within one Torah reading do not skip from topic to topic, unless both passages are on the same subject and the skip is made between aliyot.
  • Skipping between books of the Prophets, as we do on Shabbat Rosh Hodesh, is permitted today because the texts are printed and no one waits.
  • Prepare the Sifrei Torah before the service so the congregation is never kept waiting while they are rolled.
  • Do not call the same person to two Sifrei Torah. If the wrong scroll comes out, rolling it is the norm, though returning it has support.
  • Do not leave while the Sefer Torah is open; if you must step out, do it between aliyot when ten remain.
