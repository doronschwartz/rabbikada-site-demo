Class Notes: Hunting and Killing on Shabbat

Review of Previous Principles

Principle 1: B'mino Nitzod (Commonly Trapped)

Tzayada (trapping/hunting) only applies at the Torah level (D'Oraita) if the creature is b'mino nitzod – its type is commonly trapped. If the creature is not commonly trapped, trapping it is only forbidden rabbinically (D'Rabbanan).

Principle 2: Accessibility in One Go

Even if a creature is b'mino nitzod, it must be trapped in a way that makes it accessible in one arm's breadth (one go). If additional actions are needed to access the trapped creature, the prohibition is only D'Rabbanan.

Example: Closing a box on a fly in a relatively large box is D'Rabbanan on two levels:

  1. A fly is not commonly trapped
  2. It is not accessible in one go

Additional Principles Covered Previously

  • Opening and closing windows (regarding trapping)
  • Using a dog to hunt
  • Safek psik reisha (doubt about inevitable consequences)
  • Trapping humans (no concept of tzayada for humans)
  • Shnayim she'asauhu (two people performing an action together)
  • Continuing to trap an already-trapped animal (permissible)
  • Special heter to trap an animal to prevent biting (even without pikuach nefesh)

Shochet (Killing) – Previous Discussion

  • The eight sheratzim (creatures)
  • Making a bruise/wound: forbidden either as a tolada of dash (extracting blood – Rambam's view) or as shechita (killing – other Rishonim's view), based on the principle "ki hadam hu hanefesh" (blood is the life-force)
  • Hurting someone out of anger: dispute whether this is D'Oraita (clear benefit of releasing anger) or not (not actually using the blood)

Se'if Tet: Fleas, Mosquitoes, and Lice

The Basic Halacha

A flea (parosh): You cannot trap it and you cannot kill it on Shabbat.

  • If you trap it = tzayada (D'Rabbanan – not commonly trapped)
  • If you kill it = shechita (D'Rabbanan – not using what you killed)
  • If you do both = two violations

Exception: On Your Flesh and Biting

Shulhan Arukh's strict interpretation: You may trap the flea/mosquito only if:

  1. It is on your flesh, AND
  2. It is in the process of biting you or about to bite you

Reasoning for the exception:

  • It is not commonly trapped (lav b'mino nitzod)
  • It is melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa (not trapping for use)
  • Combined with bodily discomfort, Hachamim permitted trapping

Other opinions: Even if the creature is on your clothes but in proximity to your flesh, you may trap it.

Trapping vs. Killing

Important distinction: Even when it's on you and biting you (assuming no sakana/danger), you may:

  • ✓ Trap it
  • ✓ Push it away
  • ✓ Put it under a cup
  • ✗ You may NOT kill it

Why not kill? Killing is melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa, which is always on a higher level. Bodily discomfort alone doesn't permit it.

Rama's Addition

Don't roll the flea in your hands because that may kill it. Instead, take it and trap it/throw it away.

The Special Case of Lice (Kinim)

Shulhan Arukh: Lice may be killed on Shabbat.

The Gemara's reasoning: The Mishkan's melakhot involved killing rams (for hides to cover the roof). Rams come about through procreation (peru u'rvu). Therefore, only creatures that reproduce through male-female cohabitation are included in the prohibition of shechita.

The Gemara believed lice arose through spontaneous generation – not through reproduction. Therefore, lice are not included in the prohibition of shechita.

The Modern Scientific Challenge

Pahad Yishak (Rabbi Yishak Lampronti, ~200-300 years ago): Modern science shows lice DO reproduce through male-female reproduction, not spontaneous generation. Should the halacha change?

Contemporary Poskim's Response

Rav Dessler and Rav Ovadia Yoseph: Killing lice is still permissible today.

Rationale: The Hachamim based their ruling on what is visible to the naked eye. Lice reproduction is not visible to the naked eye (unlike other creatures). Torah and Halacha follow "lo nitna Torah l'malachei hasharet" – Torah follows what humans naturally see, not what can be seen through a microscope.

Rav Elyashiv: Ruled strictly – one should not kill lice on Shabbat nowadays.

Majority opinion: It is permissible to kill lice on Shabbat, even today.

Practical application: Delousing a child on Shabbat is permitted. Lice solution may be used (provided there are no issues with pulling out hair).

Lice on Garments vs. Hair

On your head: You may kill lice (lice are common there).

On your garment: Don't kill – just flick them away. There may be fleas or mosquitoes mixed in (which you cannot kill).

Practical Applications: Infestations and Insects

Ant Infestation on Shabbat

Directly spraying ants: Assur (forbidden) – even though it's melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa (only D'Rabbanan), discomfort doesn't permit it.

Permitted methods:

  1. Spray around the ants=(not directly on them) – not a psik reisha because ants can escape
  2. Blow them away (with breath)
  3. Ask a non-Jew to kill them directly – this is shvut d'shvut b'makom hefsed/tza'ar gadol:
  • First shvut: telling a non-Jew to do melakhah
  • Second shvut: melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa

Flies in the House

Permitted method: Open a window and spray into the air. The flies have the option to escape, so it's not a psik reisha. You're not intending to kill them (lo mitkaven).

Spider in the Toilet

Question: May you flush if there's a spider that will inevitably die?

Answer: Yes, it is permitted.

Reasons (Minchat Yishak and others):

  1. Mitasek – the action is so removed from killing; you're doing an action of flushing, not killing
  2. B'shvil shelo yishchenu – similar to the heter for creatures that might bite
  3. Mius – the unpleasantness of leaving the toilet unflushed

Rav Wosner's suggestion: Try to flush lightly so you're not definitely going to kill it (though this may not always be practical).

Se'if Yud: Creatures That Can Kill

Creatures with the Ability to Kill

Halacha: All creatures that bite and can kill (have the ability to kill) may be killed on Shabbat, even if they are not pursuing you.

Example: Walking through a forest and seeing a dangerous snake on the side – you may go and kill it even though it's not coming after you.

Reason according to most opinions: Melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa (only D'Rabbanan), but because of the potential danger, Hachamim permitted it.

Reason according to Rambam: Since Rambam holds melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa is D'Oraita, he must understand this as a case of sakanat nefashot (danger to life) – even a creature not pursuing you has potential to kill someone.

Creatures That Cannot Kill But Can Cause Harm

Examples: Certain snakes, scorpions, crabs that cause pain but cannot kill.

Halacha:

  • If pursuing you: You may kill it
  • If not pursuing you: You may NOT kill it (can only avoid it)

Question on Rambam

According to Rambam (melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa is D'Oraita), why can you kill a creature that cannot kill you, just because it's pursuing you? Pain doesn't permit D'Oraita violations.

Answer: Rambam must understand that even these snakes and scorpions have some ability to kill (scorpion venom can be lethal). Shulhan Arukh's statement about creatures that "definitely cannot kill" would not be accepted by Rambam.

Trampling While Walking (Dores l'fi tumo)

Halacha: Even a creature that is not pursuing you, if it's in your path, you may trample on it as part of your regular walking.

Conditions:

  • You cannot go out of your way to kill it
  • It must be part of your natural path
  • You do it as part of walking, not as a separate action

Reason: Hachamim made a special dispensation for melakhah she'eina tzricha l'gufa when done incidentally.

Modern Application

Nowadays, most biting creatures (except mosquitoes, fleas, etc.) would be considered safek pikuach nefesh (possible life danger):

  • Unknown snake species could be venomous
  • Scorpions may have poison
  • Therefore, you could kill them based on danger

Exception (Mishnah Berurah): A daddy longlegs or similar large but harmless spider – not a sakana, cannot kill it, even if you're concerned it may crawl into your food (cover the food instead).

Se'if Yud-Aleph: Rubbing Phlegm into the Ground

The Halacha

On earth ground: You cannot rub phlegm/spittle into the ground with your foot.

Reason: May come to level the ground – a tolada of Choresh (plowing), since plowing prepares level ground for planting.

On carpet or regular flooring: No problem, you may rub it in.

Still applies today: In a park, on natural earth, you cannot rub in phlegm.

Walking Over It Naturally

Permitted: If you're walking past and the phlegm is in your path, you may step on it firmly as part of your walk and continue.

Not permitted:

  • Standing and rubbing it in
  • Walking toward it specifically to step on it

Important Principle: Memareiach (Smearing)

Mishnah Berurah's ruling on absorbent materials: You may rub phlegm into an absorbent material because:

  1. No leveling of ground
  2. No memareiach (smearing)

Key principle from Magen Avraham:

Memareiach (smearing) only applies when smearing something to stay smeared on the surface.

If you're smearing something to be absorbed within, that is NOT memareiach and is mutar.

Practical Application: Creams on Shabbat

Rav Ovadia Yoseph rules: You may rub Vaseline and other hard creams into your body on Shabbat.

Why it's permitted: The intention is for the cream to be absorbed into your skin, not to stay as a layer on top.

What to avoid: Don't apply so much that there's a visible layer on top of your skin – that would be smearing a layer, which is problematic.

Soft creams: Permitted in any case.

Se'if Yud-Bet: Domesticated Animals

Four Levels of Animals

Level 1: Naturally Domesticated Animals

Examples: Sheep, cows, horses

Halacha: No tzayada at all. You may trap them freely on Shabbat.

Everyone agrees on this level.

Level 2: Animals You Have Domesticated

Examples: A parrot, a pet bird

These are not naturally domesticated but you have trained them to stay in your vicinity.

Shulhan Arukh: You may trap them (e.g., close the parrot cage).

Rama: It is assur rabbinically to trap them.

Level 3: Domesticated Animals That Have Rebelled

Animals from Level 1 or 2 that have lost their obedience – they've gone through a "tantrum" and no longer respond to your authority.

Shulhan Arukh:

  • Cannot trap in a small place (accessible in one go = D'Oraita)
  • Can trap in a large place (e.g., close the room door)

Rama:

  • Cannot trap them at all – even in a big place
  • Rebelled animals that were domesticated by you = forbidden from the Torah

Level 4: Wild/Undomesticated Animals

These are subject to full Torah-level tzayada as discussed throughout this siman.

Cats and Dogs

Rama states: A cat is forbidden to trap (like other non-naturally domesticated animals).

Contemporary Poskim: Both cats and dogs that are domesticated:

  • According to Shulhan Arukh: Permitted to trap
  • Even according to Rama: Many say mutar for domesticated pets

For Sephardim: It is permissible to close doors/cages on domesticated cats, dogs, and other pets, unless the animal has lost its obedience (rebelled).

Summary and Key Takeaways

Tzayada (Trapping)

  1. Torah-level only if b'mino nitzod AND accessible in one go
  2. Creatures biting you: permitted to trap (two D'Rabbanans combined)
  3. Fleas/mosquitoes on you and biting: may trap
  4. Domesticated animals: depends on level of domestication and Ashkenazi/Sephardi custom

Shechita (Killing)

  1. Lice: may be killed (even today, according to majority)
  2. Fleas/mosquitoes: may NOT be killed, even when biting
  3. Creatures that can kill: may be killed even if not pursuing you
  4. Creatures that can harm but not kill: only if pursuing you
  5. Trampling naturally while walking: permitted for harmful creatures

Practical Modern Applications

  • Infestations: spray around (not on), ask non-Jew, blow away
  • Spider in toilet: may flush
  • Creams: may rub in if intent is absorption
  • Pets: generally permitted to close cages/doors (especially Sephardim)
