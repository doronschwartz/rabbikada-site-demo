Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 1, Seifim 10-12 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 26 September 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi recalled the first nine seifim of Siman 1: the misvah of peru urevu, the misvah of shevet, the misvah of not being alone, whether one may sell a Sefer Torah for these, the right time to marry, and Seif 9, which says a man may marry as many wives as he wishes. Hazal advised no more than four, but the Shulhan Arukh adds that where the custom is not to take a second wife, one may not. Today's class is mostly on Seif 10, the herem (ban) of Rabbenu Gershom. He said it is very relevant when dealing with both Ashkenazim and Sefaradim, and that Sefaradim generally take a more lenient approach.

How the Shulhan Arukh frames Seif 10

The Rabbi noted that the seif is only three or four lines. The Shulhan Arukh mentions Rabbenu Gershom in order to limit him, in three ways: by exceptions, by place (it did not spread everywhere) and by time (it was only until the end of the fifth millennium, about 800 years ago). The Rema, by contrast, adds many details and brings the later minhag. Seif 11 (the Shulhan Arukh still likes the idea of communities making their own takanot) was read briefly near the end.

The reasons for the takanah

The Rabbi gave three reasons from the Rishonim:

  • The Rashba: a safeguard against men who mistreat a wife, or neglect to feed and clothe her, because they have another wife.
  • The Rosh: "to give the woman power like the man" (lehashvot koah haishah leish), that is, equal footing in the marriage.
  • The Mordekhai: to prevent quarrels between the two wives.

He added that the Rishonim lived in a particular society. Rabbenu Gershom lived among Christians who did not take even one wife, and this may be why the takanah took hold in Ashkenazi lands and not in Spain, Egypt or the Middle East. He stressed that when learning Rishonim one should know where they came from, since each describes what he sees around him.

Did it spread? Two views

Source

View

The Rashba (Spain)

It did not spread, not even all of Provence. Many people married two wives.

The Maharam Alashkar (Spain)

Most communities did not accept it.

The Mahari Mintz (Germany)

By default it is accepted everywhere, until you come to a place where it is not.

Shulhan Arukh

Like the Rashba: it did not spread.

The Rema

Like the Mahari Mintz: it spread.

Each speaks from the world he knows, so each is right for his own place. The Rabbi's practical rule: unless you know otherwise, the default is that it was accepted.

How long does it last?

The Maharik held that it lasted only until the end of the fifth millennium. The Shulhan Arukh follows this, and the Rema agrees that this was the original intention. But the Rema says the minhag continued. The Pithei Teshuvah explains the underlying question: was this a siyag laTorah, a fence that Hazal may make forever, or a takanah for good citizenship and a decent society? If it was only the latter, there is no power to forbid something permitted by the Torah indefinitely, and so it had a time limit. A class discussion followed on whether protecting wives from abuse is itself a fence. The Rabbi's conclusion: whatever began as a takanah, "it has taken on a new face" as a minhag. Once the takanah expired it became a custom, and a custom is binding.

The exceptions

The Shulhan Arukh lists two, the Rema adds more. The Rabbi gave them as follows.

  1. Yibum. The Beit Yoseph says the herem was not decreed for yibum, since it comes from Heaven. The Mahari Mintz held that it applies even then. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Beit Yoseph, and the Rema records both views.
  2. Arusah (a betrothed woman). This is unclear. The Taz reads it simply: the herem applies from nisuin, so a man whose first wife has not yet been divorced may still do kiddushin with another woman. The Beit Shemuel rejects this and offers other readings: once you do erusin you must go through with the marriage but may divorce her, or a man may take two betrothed women, since the herem is about having two wives. The Pithei Teshuvah brings a further reading, that Rabbenu Gershom did not forbid divorcing an arusah against her will. The Rabbi noted that the Rema says nothing about it.
  3. Ten years without children. The Rema says that where the wife will not receive a get, he may marry another.
  4. A wife who is insane. She cannot be divorced, so he may marry a second wife.
  5. A wife who left the religion. The Rema says there is no need even for a get zikui, unless there is a custom to do it.

Heter Meah Rabbanim

When an exception applies, is permission of 100 rabbis still needed? The Beit Shemuel says the exceptions work even without it, but notes that "we always do" get the permission, as a custom. The Arukh HaShulhan was more lenient. The Rabbi found it hard to see a case today where it would be truly needed, outside these exceptions. The Beit Yoseph brings the Kolbo for the process: the rabbis should come from three lands. The Hatam Sofer explains that "100 men" need not be great scholars, only people who know basic psak, and that three lands can mean three states within one country, such as in America. They need not be spread equally.

Moving between places

An Ashkenazi who moves to a Sefaradi country is still bound, because the herem is on the person ("on the head of the man"). A Sefaradi who goes to an Ashkenazi place must follow the minhag of the place when he marries there. If someone arrives already with two wives, forcing a divorce is a different question, the subject of force, below.

Force

The Nimukei Yoseph says "we force". The Beit Yoseph explains that this means we force him not to marry, but once he is married there is no forcing. The Rema says that nowadays we do force him to divorce.

Which is worse?

If a man wants to divorce a wife who refuses a get and marry another, is it better to divorce her against her will or to marry a second wife? The Rabbi presented both sides. Divorcing is one single act, while living with two wives is a continuing violation, if one understands the herem as the Rema does. On the other hand, everyone agrees that the herem against divorcing against her will applies even beyond the fifth millennium, while the discussion of the time limit is only about taking a second wife. He said it is interesting to consider and left it as a question.

Practice today

  • Israel. Bigamy is outlawed by law, with a provision that the Beit Din HaGadol may permit it.
  • Elsewhere. Where a couple are divorced civilly and the wife will not receive a get, the question is practical. The Rabbi described the approach in cases involving Sefaradim: send her several written notices, about 30 days apart, asking her to receive a get. If she does not respond, write the get as best one can on the information given, and then allow the husband to remarry. For Ashkenazim it is much more serious.
  • Heter Meah Rabbanim today. The Rabbi called the requirement today "a big humrah", since there is a way out for men. In the class discussion it was noted that the requirement helps the perception of equality for women, but for situations like a wife in a coma, or one with advanced dementia who cannot receive a get, he leaned toward leniency and against making things harder unnecessarily, as long as civil law is respected.

Seifim 11 and 12

Seif 11: it is good to make communal takanot, with berakhot and bans, that a man will not take a second wife. This is quoting the Rosh, and applies even where Rabbenu Gershom was not accepted. Where the minhag is to marry one wife, you must follow it, whether the minhag comes from Rabbenu Gershom, from the law of the land or from the Jewish community. In America, bigamy is illegal, so even a Sefaradi should not take a second wife there. So it is not simply true that "Sefaradim marry many wives". The Rabbi mentioned that at Sefaradi weddings an undertaking is often added not to take another wife without the permission of Beit Din.

Seif 12: a man swore not to marry a second wife. If he then remained ten years without children, one can seek release from the oath.

Practical summary

  • Rabbenu Gershom's herem was meant to stop abuse of wives and to give the wife equal standing. It is observed in Ashkenazi communities as a binding minhag.
  • The Shulhan Arukh limits it by exceptions, place and time. The Rema says the minhag continued after the time limit.
  • Exceptions: yibum, arusah, ten years without children (where she will not accept a get), an insane wife, a wife who left the religion.
  • Even where an exception applies, the minhag is to ask for the permission of 100 rabbis from three lands. The Rabbi calls this a big humrah today.
  • An Ashkenazi remains bound when he moves to a Sefaradi place. A Sefaradi follows the minhag of the place he goes to, and civil law matters.
  • Divorcing against her will is forbidden by the herem even beyond the time limit, which is a factor when weighing the two alternatives.
