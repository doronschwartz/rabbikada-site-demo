Topic: Change and teshuvah, the story of Franz Rosenzweig's Yom Kippur, prayer from the heart, and forgiving others Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Tiferet Eyal, Tuesday 14 September 2021, the day before Yom Kippur

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Change is possible

We can fall into the trap of thinking that the way we are born is the way we will die. Many great cultures thought so. For the Greeks, character was destiny; Plato said some people are born gold, some silver, some bronze, some to rule and some to be ruled.

The whole message of Rosh Hashanah, the Ten Days and Yom Kippur is that change is possible. "If one doesn't believe that, one might as well give up now." That is why we read Yonah: the decree on Nineveh was overturned. Isaac Bashevis Singer said, "We have to be free; we have no choice." If we are just programmed by DNA and neurological impulses, life loses its beauty. Teshuvah is not only confession; it is the decision to learn from mistakes and become a different kind of person. Rav Soloveitchik said that our greatest creation is ourselves.

Moshe himself began by saying he could not change: "I am heavy of mouth." By Devarim he speaks for thirty-six days, one of the great orators of history.

The Rabbi distinguished a technical problem, where the doctor prescribes a pill, from an adaptive problem, where the doctor says no pill will help unless you change your lifestyle. Yom Kippur is about adapting.

Franz Rosenzweig's Neilah

Then he told a story he said had shaken him to the core. Franz Rosenzweig, born in Germany in 1886, became one of the most influential Jewish thinkers of the twentieth century. A close student of Rabbi Sacks told the Rabbi that the two greatest influences on Rabbi Sacks' thought were the Lubavitcher Rebbe and Rosenzweig.

His great-grandfather had come from Eastern Europe with semikhah. By his father's generation the family was nominally Jewish, attending the local Reform temple once a year on Yom Kippur. At university in Berlin, Rosenzweig met deeply committed Christians and envied them. Their faith spoke to them; his own seemed shallow and inconsistent. In 1913, at twenty-seven, encouraged by a cousin who had converted and a close Christian friend, he decided to convert to Christianity.

But he wanted to enter Christianity "as a Jew," so he resolved to attend one last Yom Kippur. Away from his parents, he went to a temple on the night of Yom Kippur and found it uninspiring as usual. For Neilah he came upon a small Eastern European, Yiddish-speaking shtiebel. When he came out, he was a changed man. He wrote to his cousin that it was no longer necessary, and no longer possible, for him to change his faith. He devoted himself to Torah study and became a central figure among Jewish thinkers.

What changed him? Not the text, since he barely knew Hebrew then. Not a sermon, since he had heard eloquent ones all his life. What he saw, he wrote, was a community of Jews who actually cared, people who were not an audience. His parents had said Judaism asked so little of him. That was exactly the problem. He wanted a religion that asked something of him.

Three lessons from the story

  1. Torah is lived, not only believed. "Taste and see that Hashem is good": first taste. Rosenzweig, a doctor of philosophy, conceded that ideas only become real when people live them. That is why Yom Kippur alone is not enough, and why halachah, from the word "to walk," gives a path through every moment of the year.
  2. A life can change direction in a day. No one changes their whole character in a day. But in a couple of hours one can change one's orientation. Teshuvah means return. People ask why they should go through it all again when they come back the same each year. The answer is that Yom Kippur gives a genuine chance to begin a new direction and glimpse a new horizon.
  3. We are responsible for each other. Rosenzweig was not saved by an argument, but by a congregation that made Judaism alive. It is easy to sit back and admire the hazzan, the sermon or the decor. "We never know who next to us is giving Torah their one last chance."

Prayer with wings

He told the story of the Baal Shem Tov, who refused to enter an empty synagogue because it was "too full": full of prayers that had no wings to rise. Whether or not every hasidic story happened, the lesson stands. Prayer is avodah shebalev, the service of the heart. It is avodah, work: you cannot sit back as in a cinema and expect a spiritual experience. And it is of the heart, not the lips. Yeshayahu says the people honour Hashem with their lips while their heart is far away. As a child, the Rabbi admitted, he spent much of Yom Kippur calculating how many pages were left per hour. Yom Kippur is not about finishing the book.

The Rishonim say prayer without kavanah is like a body without a soul. "Enthusiasm" comes from Greek words meaning "in God." Rabbi Sacks put it: prayer is the act of listening to God listening to us.

Forgive, and be forgiven

Where does the Torah first say that Hashem forgave? Not with Adam, Kayin or the flood, where there is punishment but no forgiveness. It comes after the golden calf, and again after the spies: "I have forgiven, as you asked." Where does a human first forgive another? A participant answered correctly: Yosef. After Yaakov's death his brothers, afraid, told him their father had asked him to forgive them. Yosef comforted them and said it was all from Hashem.

The Rabbi's point: in the Torah, people learn to forgive each other before Hashem forgives. Otherwise we might think we can repair our relationships simply by asking God. The Gemara reads "noseh avon veover al pesha" as "whom does He forgive? One who overlooks the wrongs done to him." Whoever lets go of the wrongs done to him has all his sins forgiven.

He explained this with two modes of divine conduct. In hanhagat hamishpat, Hashem has set up a system of consequences and, as it were, ties His own hands. In hanhagat hayihud, He sets the system aside. If we insist on strict justice with others, we may be right, and we may have that right, but then we should not expect mercy. If we live with mercy, Hashem does the same. That does not mean letting yourself be run over.

People travel the world for segulot before Yom Kippur. This is the greatest segulah of all, written by Hazal. Complete teshuvah, the Rambam says, is facing the same situation and not sinning, which is very hard. Prayer with real kavanah is hard; the Yerushalmi records sages who admitted that their heads bowed at Modim by themselves. But here is a simple way to have all sins forgiven. The Shulhan Arukh says not to be cruel when asked for forgiveness. We hold on because we imagine we can control others. "The only person which we can change is ourselves." Ironically, we think we cannot change, but everyone else can.

The thirteen attributes

We will say the thirteen attributes many times on Yom Kippur. The Gemara says Hashem told Moshe, "whenever Israel sin, let them do this order before Me," and promises that the thirteen attributes do not return empty. The commentators stress the word "do." Hashem does not need reminding that He is merciful. We need to ask: am I merciful? Slow to anger? Truthful in my dealings? When we emulate, the promise holds.

His final practical advice: we tend to ask forgiveness from people we get on with, just as at Purim we send mishloah manot to our friends. Use the time to approach the people where there really is friction, and say, "Let's start to refresh this year."

Summary

  • The core message of these days is that people can change; Yonah and Nineveh prove it.
  • Franz Rosenzweig, on the verge of converting, was changed by one Neilah among Jews who cared.
  • Torah must be lived, a life can change direction in a day, and our own passion affects others.
  • Prayer is work of the heart, not a recital to be finished.
  • Hashem forgives those who forgive; ask forgiveness from the people you really need to.
