These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two puzzles about Adar

The class met on the first day of Rosh Hodesh Adar, with Purim and Pesah coming up. The Rabbi began with two statements of Hazal.

The first is "mishenikhnas Adar marbin besimhah": when Adar begins, we increase our joy. What does that mean? It cannot mean drinking a little more every day of Adar. And it cannot be a mechanical rule, or we would have to be consistently sad all month when Av comes.

The second is the Gemara's advice that a Jew who has a court case with a non-Jew should avoid Av and try to have it heard in Adar, when his mazal is better. The Rabbi gave a modern example: if you have fourteen days to appeal a parking ticket, wait until Adar. But the Gemara in Shabbat also says "ein mazal leYisrael", Israel has no mazal. If so, what difference can the month make?

He suggested that the two statements complement each other, and set them aside to explain a deeper idea first.

Yihud Hashem in the Ramhal

Anyone who knows the thought of the Ramhal, in Derech Hashem and his other works, knows that its overarching principle is Yihud Hashem. We say Shema Yisrael several times every day, at a brit milah and at the end of life. Do we really need so many reminders that there are not two gods? "That's not the issue. That's not what Hashem Ehad means." On a simple level it means there is one God. On a deeper level it is, for the Ramhal, the foundation of the whole Torah and the reason the world exists: we are here to reveal the oneness of God.

The world runs with giluy and hester, revelation and concealment, and different periods of history have more of one or the other. Yihud Hashem means seeing everything that happens to us as part of God's plan. Every morning we bless God who forms light and creates darkness, and every evening we say He rolls away light before darkness. At the very moments when day and night change over, we declare that both come from one source.

The Rabbi took it further. If we truly lived with this, we would never get angry, never take revenge, never feel that something had gone wrong, and we would not need to flatter anyone, since flattery gives power to people. In practice we do all these things. "When I'm speaking now, I'm speaking to myself as well."

This is why, says the Ramhal, we cover our eyes for the Shema. The Rosh explains it as a way to concentrate. The Ramhal says it is because we believe in the unity of God but cannot yet see it. The world looks as if there are many powers, and human beings seem able to act against God's will. On Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur we pray for the day when evil will vanish like smoke and everyone will recognise that it, too, was only a tool in God's hands.

Every misvah, and every sin we are told to avoid, serves this purpose. Temptation presents itself as another power with its own pleasure. Someone who lives with Yihud Hashem sees that it is a smokescreen.

What mazal means

The word mazal comes from nozel, to flow. The Rabbi explained the Ramhal in Derech Hashem (Part 2, Chapter 7). God's infinite light is too intense for a world to exist directly, so it comes down through a chain of emanations. This, he said, is the simple idea behind the sefirot in Kabbalah. Everything physical has a spiritual force above it, and the last link in the chain, where the flow enters our physical universe, is the stars and planets, the mazalot.

That is where randomness enters. The Gemara in Shabbat says that someone born under Mars (Maadim) will be drawn to blood, so he should become a shohet or a mohel rather than a killer. What reaches a person may depend on which constellation the flow passes through. A good person may have bad luck and a bad person good luck. A person who lives without recognising the source of everything is subject to these forces.

Ein mazal leYisrael

The Ramhal explains, and the Rabbi said the Rambam says something along these lines too, that "ein mazal leYisrael" describes a person who lives with Yihud Hashem. Such a person has a direct relationship with God, and the blessing that comes to him need not be filtered through the randomness of the constellations. It does not mean every Jew is automatically free of mazal and every non-Jew subject to it. A Jew is more likely to live this way, but many do not.

He was blunt about charms and red threads. Maybe they work and maybe they don't, he said, but they declare that your life depends on wearing a necklace, which ties you into mazal and cuts off the direct relationship with God.

So when the Gemara calls Adar a month of good mazal, it is speaking to people who live within mazal. For them, Adar is a good time and Av needs care. For someone who truly lives with Yihud Hashem, Adar and Av make no difference. The Rabbi said this reading is supported by the Ibn Ezra.

Why then do we wish each other "mazal tov", literally a good flow? Because in practice we forget and do not always live on that level. Hazal often speak to the reality of human life, while "ein mazal" is the aspiration. He compared it to "Barukh shem kevod malkhuto", which we say quietly all year because God's kingship is hidden, and aloud on Yom Kippur, when we feel it.

A question from the class

Someone challenged this. Surely the halacha was written for everyone, including the greatest saddik. Would he really ignore the advice about Adar? The Rabbi answered that, on the Ibn Ezra's view, someone living fully with Yihud Hashem need not worry. But perhaps even a great saddik should take care, since he cannot be sure he has reached that level. He left it open: "When we get to that level, we'll see."

Purim: the festival of hester panim

The Rabbi then returned to Adar. Every festival in our calendar takes place in the Land of Israel or on the way there, except one. Purim is the lesson of exile, and in exile God's face is hidden, so His unity is harder to see.

The turning point of the Megillah, he suggested, is when Esther hesitates and Mordekhai answers that relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from somewhere else. He believes in Yihud Hashem: it will work out, and Esther can choose to be the channel. Esther takes this on, and that is the centre of the story. The whole Megillah looks like a series of coincidences, but when you look deeper, the hand of God is everywhere.

He pointed to the small aleph at the start of Vayikra. Without it, the word is vayiker: it happened, by chance, mazal. With it, vayikra: God called, with purpose.

That, he concluded, is the greatest simhah: to recognise that everything in our lives comes from God and that we are above the pull of mazal. Adar is the time to tune in to this again, to find the hand of God in the story of Esther and in our own lives despite all the concealment.

Summary

  • Hazal say we increase our joy in Adar, and also that a Jew's mazal is better in Adar, even though "ein mazal leYisrael".
  • For the Ramhal, the purpose of the world and of the misvot is to reveal Yihud Hashem, which is now hidden.
  • Mazal is the last link in the chain through which God's flow reaches the world; a person who lives with Yihud Hashem has a direct connection and is above it.
  • Adar's good mazal is for those who live within mazal; the aspiration is to rise above it.
  • Purim, the festival of exile and hidden coincidence, teaches us to find God's hand, and that recognition is the true simhah of Adar.
