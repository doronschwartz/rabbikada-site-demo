A new kind of condition

The class finished Siman 143 last time and now begins Siman 144, which is also about conditions in a get. The Rabbi explained the difference between the neighbouring simanim:

  • Siman 143: conditions that the husband places on the wife. He wants something from her, such as 200 zuz, serving his father or feeding his children.
  • Siman 144: conditions that keep the options open for the husband. He wants to divorce his wife, but there is a chance they may get back together, for example because he may return to town. So he says: "I divorce you on condition that I do not come back within twelve months." Here the responsibility is on him.
  • Siman 145: conditions about death, such as "if I die".

This class covers Seifim 1 and 2.

Seif 1: Does an unavoidable delay save the husband?

Suppose the husband says "if I do not come back within twelve months, you are divorced", and something happens that really prevents him from returning. Is the get valid? This is the question of an ones, an event beyond his control, in a get.

The source starts at the beginning of Ketubot, which discusses marrying within twelve months. The Gemara has two versions of what Rava said:

  • First version: ein ones begittin. An ones does not help. The get takes effect and she is divorced. Rava brings a proof from the case of a man who said that he would come within thirty days. On day thirty he is on the river bank, there is no ferry, and he shouts "I am here, I am here". It does not help.
  • Second version: there is an ones in gittin, but only for an ones that is not common.

The Gemara explains Rava's reason for the strict view. If an ones could invalidate the condition, then modest women would never dare to remarry, thinking that perhaps he was on his way and was held up. And on the other side, there would be women who would remarry anyway and end up with mamzerim. So Rava made an enactment, out of concern for both.

The Rishonim all rule like the first version. A later Gemara in Gittin says unequivocally that there is no ones in a get, in the case of a man who was busy preparing for his wedding and did not come in time.

Three levels of ones

This seems to clash with another Gemara, in Gittin, where a man says "if I die from this illness" and a house falls on him. There the get is not valid, because he did not die of the illness. The Tosafot, followed by the Ramban and others, resolve it with three levels:

  1. A common mishap, such as the ferries not running. Everyone agrees this is no excuse and the get takes effect.
  2. A completely strange event, such as a house falling on him, a snake bite or a lion. Everyone agrees this is a real ones, and the get does not take effect. The Rabbi's example: if he was bitten, lay in the jungle for months to recover and returned after eighteen months, that is an ones.
  3. The middle case, such as an illness, or a delay in preparing for the wedding. It is not common, but not so unusual either. This is where the two versions in the Gemara disagree, and the halacha follows the first version: it is not an excuse.

In the Rabbi's words, you cannot legislate for every bizarre case. The Gemara is concerned about the normal case, and Rava was also concerned that a woman might remarry and then have a mamzer. So an event that no one would have foreseen, like being carried off by an eagle, is treated as a real ones, but ordinary delays are not.

What the Shulhan Arukh says

The Shulhan Arukh gives the case of a man who said "if I do not come within thirty days", and he was on his way and fell ill, or the river stopped him, and he came after thirty days. The get takes effect, even if he stands on the other bank shouting that he is about to cross. He did not include "unless something happens to me" in the original condition. The Rabbi noted that the Shulhan Arukh treats illness and a river as the same level: both are common enough that he should have included an exception.

But a truly strange ones does cancel the get. If the husband died childless, she would need halisah in that case.

The Rema adds that captivity (shivyah) does not cancel the get. The Rabbi explained, following the Beit Shemuel, that this means captivity is in the middle level, and not a completely unusual event. In the army today, when a soldier goes out to the front and there is a real chance he will not return, he can include in his condition: "I divorce you if I do not return in twelve months, unless I am captured or prevented from returning." If he does not include it, we do not expect him to cover the most extreme cases. A student noted that captivity was common in earlier times, and today it may be far more unusual. The Rabbi agreed that the classification depends on the variables of the day.

A question of money

The Beit Shemuel adds a point. Suppose the husband says "you are divorced if you give me 200 pounds within twelve months". He saves the money, but on the last day a lion swallows it, or a burglar steals it. The get does not take effect. An ones can excuse a person from a penalty, but it cannot make it as if he had done the thing. The Rabbi's analogy: someone who missed a school exam through illness cannot claim the grade as if he had passed it.

The Beit Shemuel then raises the reverse case. A wife says "I will only accept the get if you pay me 200 pounds", and the husband gives the get with that condition, but an ones prevents him paying. The Beit Shemuel appears to say that here the get does not take effect either, for equality. Everyone asks about this. The husband can give the get without any condition, and the condition exists only to satisfy Rabbenu Gershom's herem against divorcing a wife without her consent. A student suggested that perhaps the husband never intended to violate the herem. The Rabbi found this interesting, but he said it would require us to assume that the herem included such an exception, which is hard to accept.

Seif 2: He dies before the twelve months are up

The husband says "if I do not come back within twelve months, this is a get". He dies after six months. Is the woman divorced, or does she need yibum or halisah?

The Mishnah makes a distinction. If he said "from now" (meahshav), then the get takes effect from the time it was given, and she is divorced without yibum or halisah. If he did not say it, she needs yibum or halisah.

Rabbi Yosei held that "the date on the document shows" (zeman shetar mekhaah). Even without "from now", the date on the get shows that he wants it to take effect from then. The question is whether the halacha follows him in gittin. This is also relevant because we always write "from today" in a get now, so is it even necessary? The Rishonim have four views:

  1. Tosafot and others: the halacha follows Rabbi Yosei, so she is divorced either way. The words "from today" in our gittin are there only to make the get nicer (shufra deshtara).
  2. The Tur, and the Shulhan Arukh follows him: we do not know. In money cases we are lenient and in gittin we must be strict, so she cannot marry anyone (it may have been a get) and she cannot do yibum (it may have been a get). She must do halisah only.
  3. The Rambam: Rabbi Yosei applies only in money law. In a get the date must be written anyway, because of an enactment, so the date proves nothing about his wishes. Without "from now" she needs yibum or halisah.
  4. The Rema: it depends. If the condition was written into the get, we do not say that the date shows his intent, because by writing "if" he showed that it should take effect only after twelve months. If the condition was verbal, the date works and she is divorced.

The Beit Shemuel's question

The Beit Shemuel asks a strong question. Today we always write "from today" in the get, so according to everyone the get should take effect from the start. The answer, from the Beit Meir, is that a condition stated verbally ("if I do not return in twelve months") overrides it, since he is saying that the get should take effect only after twelve months. One of the students suggested this too. The Rabbi agreed that it is not a clear contradiction, but it is reasonable to read it that way. He said this is similar to a get that takes effect after a week, which he said is a separate topic for a later siman.

Practical summary

  • In a condition like "if I do not return within twelve months", a common delay (travel problems, illness) does not rescue the husband, and the get takes effect.
  • A completely strange event, such as a house falling on him or an animal attack, is a real ones, and the get does not take effect.
  • Captivity is in the middle category, so it does not cancel the get. A soldier going to the front should include an exception in his condition.
  • A condition to pay money cannot be fulfilled by an ones: an excuse does not turn what he did not do into something done.
  • If the husband dies before the twelve months pass, the answer depends on the four views. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Tur: she may not remarry and may not do yibum, so she does halisah.
  • The same four views will return later in the siman, so they are worth knowing now.
