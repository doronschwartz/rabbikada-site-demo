Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 277 and 278, then an introduction to the categories of Mukseh before Siman 279 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 7 December 2023

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The previous week covered Siman 276: asking a non-Jew to do a melakhah, such as turning up the heating, and the separate question of benefiting from what a non-Jew has done. This short class, just before Hanukkah, finished Simanim 277 and 278 and then stopped to lay the foundations for Siman 279, on moving the candlesticks on Shabbat.

Siman 277: opening doors and windows

Siman 277 deals with old kinds of candles and wicks, and with being careful not to put them out when opening a door. The Rabbi said that in its details it is not relevant nowadays, but one principle remains. If it is not certain that opening a door or window will extinguish a flame, you may open it, even if in the end the flame does go out. The problem only arises when it is definite (pesik reisha). So as long as there is no strong, gusty wind, you can open windows and doors.

Siman 278: putting out a light for someone who is ill

Siman 278 allows extinguishing a candle for someone who is ill and in danger, so that he can sleep. The Rabbi asked what the hiddush is: we already know that anything may be done for someone in danger, including driving him to hospital.

His answer: normally we break Shabbat because of a specific medical need. Here we are told that sleep is always good for an ill person. So when someone is seriously ill there is no need to make a medical assessment first. You may put out the candles or switch off the lights straight away. Of course, if the candle can simply be taken out of the room, that is better, since extinguishing it is the more serious act.

Why there is Mukseh at all

Before Siman 279, which deals with moving candles and candlesticks, the Rabbi gave an introduction. Mukseh is rabbinic. Several reasons are given for it, among them that handling such things could lead a person to do melakhah, and that it makes Shabbat feel different from a weekday. "This is why it's so important to have categories and structure," he said. He added that when the Beit Din tests converts on Hilchot Shabbat, they are always asked to list the categories of Mukseh.

Two points about these categories. First, something only reaches category 3 if it is already category 2: an expensive item has to be a utensil used for something forbidden before its value "upgrades" it to the stricter category. Second, the reason for nolad shows the essence of Mukseh: when Shabbat came in you did not have it and you managed without it, so wait until Shabbat is over.

Food that cannot be eaten on Shabbat, such as flour or raw meat, is not a utensil at all. It is like a stone: on Shabbat it has no purpose, so it is category 1. The same is true of coins, which may not be moved at all.

The base (basis ledavar haassur)

A base takes on the status of the Mukseh on it. If when Shabbat came in, and through twilight, the only thing on a table was a phone or money, the table has the status of that Mukseh for the whole of Shabbat. Even if a child or a non-Jew later takes the phone away, the table still may not be moved.

The rules for moving Mukseh

  • Mukseh may be touched. The idea that it may not be touched is a mistake.
  • All Mukseh may be moved indirectly, in an unusual way. If your phone is on your bed, you may knock it off with your elbow.
  • Mukseh may not be picked up and moved normally, even because you need the space. You cannot pick up the phone to clear the bed; you have to push it off.
  • The exception is category 2, keli shemelakhto leissur. It may be moved because you need its place (a hammer on your bed may be picked up and put away) or to use it for a permitted purpose (you may crack nuts with a hammer, or use matches as counters in a game). An expensive gold pen on the bed, though, has been "upgraded" to category 3 and may only be knocked off.

Status is fixed at twilight

Mukseh is decided by the state of things at the start of Shabbat, during twilight. Normally, something that was Mukseh then stays Mukseh all Shabbat, and something that was not Mukseh then does not become Mukseh during Shabbat. Wet clothes hung up before Shabbat stay Mukseh even when they are dry the next morning. Someone asked whether wet clothes may be used if one has nothing else to wear; the Rabbi said there is an exception for wet clothes, to be learned later.

An empty pot is a keli shemelakhto leissur, because its main purpose is cooking. So if your cups are dirty you may drink water from it, but you may not just move it around. A pot that had food in it when Shabbat began is different: its use that Shabbat is holding food, so it does not become Mukseh even after it is emptied.

Questions from the class

Someone asked about a smartwatch. The Rabbi said it depends what kind it is. If it records all the time in the background, whatever you do, there is less of a problem, especially if you make no use of the information. If moving your hand switches something on, that is a problem.

The class ended with a quick exercise: name an object and say which category it is. Most things, he noted, fall into categories 1 and 2; category 3 covers valuable utensils, and the others apply to specific cases. "It's really helpful to train your brain to think like that."

Practical summary

  • You may open a door or window on Shabbat even if a flame might go out, as long as it is not certain to happen (no strong wind).
  • For someone who is seriously ill, you may put out a light so he can sleep without a medical assessment; removing the candle is better where possible.
  • Mukseh may be touched and moved indirectly, but not picked up, except a keli shemelakhto leissur for its place or for a permitted use.
  • Expensive utensils, coins, raw food and things born on Shabbat may not be moved at all.
  • A table or tray that held only Mukseh at the start of Shabbat takes its status for the whole of Shabbat.
