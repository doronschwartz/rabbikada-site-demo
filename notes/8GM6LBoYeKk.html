Last week covered how to use the first two sources safely: trust the senses but beware illusions such as reflections, and trust reason but beware dreams. Tonight is the third source.

Inference can create a whole science

Sometimes we infer one fact: smoke, so there is fire. Sometimes several: a creature eats and later excretes, so it must be able to draw in, retain, digest and expel food. Rav Saadiah's next example shows that sometimes what we see forces us to accept a whole science.

He takes the moon. We see it at different heights, sometimes in the south of the sky and sometimes in the north, taking different times to pass through the 28 lunar stations. If it had one simple motion, its path would never change. Since it changes, it must have several motions, and only geometry and astronomy can explain them. So we are forced to accept those sciences as true.

The Rabbi was frank that the technical details are beyond him, and said we do not need to follow every detail. The point is that the complexity of what we see forces us to a whole discipline. He compared the theory of relativity: a new body of science drawn from observation and experiments.

A question

Someone said they found the passage difficult, but understood that Rav Saadiah was showing how careful observation lets us build a theory of what we see. The Rabbi agreed, and added that Rav Saadiah is not claiming this as his own idea. He is pointing to science that everyone learns. The Rambam has a long chapter in the laws of Kiddush HaHodesh, the sanctification of the new moon, that takes the same background for granted and which most people find very hard.

Five traps in inference

Now Rav Saadiah warns when we have no right to infer. He says these five mistakes are the main reason people argue endlessly about inferential knowledge and trade proofs. If everyone kept to these points, there would be far fewer arguments.

Trap 1: there is another way to explain what I sensed

A person says: "I have to believe X, or else I would be denying what I saw." But first ask whether what he saw can be explained without X. If it can, the inference is not verified.

Rav Saadiah's example is the Milky Way. People saw that it is white, and concluded that the sun must once have rotated through that part of the sky and left its whiteness. But the whiteness could be explained otherwise: rising vapours, particles of fire, or many small shining stars. So the first conclusion cannot stand. (The Rabbi admitted this passage is hard, and said the point is the method.)

The rule: you may infer from your senses only if nothing else explains what you sensed. This is why a claim like "it must be true" is only as strong as the absence of alternatives. If we say "it is likely", we can have a discussion. If we say "it is a fact", then by Rav Saadiah's test we have to show nothing else could explain it. Speaking in terms of likelihood, the Rabbi said, would reduce a great deal of conflict between people.

Trap 2: there is another way to explain the reason

A person says: "I have to believe X, or I would deny a self-evident principle." But maybe the principle does not really force X, or can itself be questioned.

Aristotle reports that students of Pythagoras believed there are many earths, with their own creatures. Their reasoning: fire is the most precious element, and the most precious thing is always kept in the middle. The sun is fire, and our earth is far from it, so there must be other earths around the central fire.

The Rabbi asked the class how to rebut this. Several answers came up: is the sun really in the middle? Must the most important thing be in the middle? And who said fire is the most precious? You could just as well say that man is the most precious, that he lives on this earth, and so our earth is the centre. Their "self-evident" starting point was not self-evident at all.

The Rabbi asked for a modern example of this one, and no one had one to hand.

Trap 3: it contradicts another sense

If inferring Y from what I sense would conflict with something else I sense, I must decide which sense is stronger before I can infer anything.

The example is Thales of Miletus, who held that everything comes from water, since living things are full of moisture. But we also see that water always flows away and never stays still. How can something forever in motion be the independent root of everything else? One observation undermines the other.

Trap 4: it contradicts itself

An inference that ends up contradicting itself cannot stand. The example is the Epicureans, who defined good as whatever feels good. But take two enemies. When A kills B, A is pleased and calls it good. When B kills A, B is pleased and calls it good. The same event is both good and bad, which is impossible. So "good is whatever feels good" cannot be the definition.

Trap 5: it leads somewhere worse

Rav Saadiah gives two examples under this heading.

5a. Some argued that the world is eternal, with no beginning, because "we only believe what we sense, and we cannot sense a Creator." But the Rabbi pointed out that you cannot sense eternity either. Nobody has experienced a world with no beginning. So a person who says "I only trust my senses" is using a reason he does not himself accept.

5b. Some believed in Hashem but held that He cannot bring back yesterday. They reached this from some philosophy, which Rav Saadiah will discuss in the first essay. But it ends in an even more absurd place: that there is something Hashem cannot do. Whatever got you there must be rejected.

The seven things to watch

Rav Saadiah closes the chapter by gathering the warnings. There are seven: the five above, plus the two from last week, illusions (for the senses) and dreams (for reason). When we go through them patiently, and "he likes patience", we arrive at truth. This will matter later when the book tests whether prophets are true.

Check before you infer

1

Can something else explain what I sensed?

2

Can something else explain the reason?

3

Does it deny something else I sense?

4

Does it contradict itself?

5

Does it lead to something more absurd?

6

Am I fooled by an illusion?

7

Am I treating a dream as real?

If a claim passes all seven, you can say with confidence that you have inferred knowledge. If not, it may well be true, "but it is not inferred knowledge yet".

The Rabbi's challenge, and a closing comment

The Rabbi's challenge to the class: the next time someone says "it must be true", run through these seven checks.

One participant added a simple habit that goes with this. Whenever anyone says something must be true, ask why it must be true. The explanation may be hard, but it is the right question. The Rabbi agreed: once you have the reason, test it against the seven points, and do the same when the media tells you that "it must be this, because of that".

Summary

  1. Inference can give us one fact, several facts, or a whole science, as the moon shows.
  2. Five traps: another explanation for what I sensed; another explanation for the reason; conflict with another sense; self-contradiction; a conclusion more absurd than the problem.
  3. Seven checks in all, adding illusions and dreams.
  4. Likely is not the same as a fact. Use the word that matches the evidence.
  5. Ask "why must it be true?" and test the answer.
