Topic: How far we may read the Torah metaphorically, and where the limits lie Sources: Saadiah Gaon, Emunot VeDeot, Essay 7; the Rambam, Moreh Nevuchim II:25; the Netsiv; Pirkei Avot 3:11 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, Wednesday 12 July 2023, the opening class of a new series on Torah fundamentals

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where do we draw the line?

The Rabbi opened a new series on Torah fundamentals with a question that, he said, is widely misunderstood and goes to the heart of our faith: how much licence do we have to read the Torah, meaning the Five Books, as metaphor or allegory? Rabbinic statements, he noted, are a separate discussion.

He began with a set of questions. The Torah speaks of "the eyes of Hashem", of God's nostrils smoking with anger, of God "coming down" to see Sodom. Nobody in the room took those literally. What about Bilam's donkey speaking, read only a week or two before? The snake speaking to Havah? The splitting of the Sea, the revelation at Sinai, the binding of Yishak? Answers in the room varied widely, and someone raised a passage where the plain meaning is very hard for people today. He said that reinterpreting the Torah to fit modern sensibilities is a different question, which he would come back to at the end. He also mentioned that some modern writers have suggested that Avraham, Yishak and Yaakov are parables and not real people. "When have you overstepped? When is it appropriate? When is it wrong?"

He read from the well-known public debate between the late Rabbi Sacks and Richard Dawkins, where Dawkins presses again and again whether Avraham really put Yishak on the altar, and Rabbi Sacks answers that "these things happened, but they didn't happen as mere facts; they happened as morally instructive lessons". The Rabbi said he found the answer hard to pin down, and that this was exactly the question for the evening.

Torah has layers, and limits

Three ideas frame the question:

  • Torah is many-layered. The Gemara compares a verse to a hammer splitting a rock into many fragments. The Netsiv adds that even the plain level of the Torah is poetry, not prose, and poetry is never read purely at face value. In Hebrew the word for "word" and the word for "thing" are the same, davar: the Midrash says that when Adam named the animals, the names expressed what each creature is.
  • "A verse never departs from its plain meaning." Yet there are clear exceptions. "An eye for an eye" is explained by the Gemara as monetary compensation, and someone who preached it literally would be teaching something contrary to the Torah. "Do not put a stumbling block before the blind" forbids giving someone misleading advice; literally tripping a blind person is wrong, but falls under other prohibitions.
  • There are boundaries. Pirkei Avot warns that one who "reveals faces in the Torah not according to halacha" forfeits his share in the World to Come, even with Torah and good deeds. "It's a divine law in human hands, but there are limitations."

Saadiah Gaon's four reasons

The first to treat this systematically was Saadiah Gaon in tenth-century Babylonia, author of the first structured work of Jewish philosophy. (The Rabbi noted that the Talmud's own style is conversational, and that it was the Rambam, influenced by Greek thought, who brought order to law and philosophy.) Saadiah writes that every verse is literal unless one of four reasons applies:

  1. The senses refute it. Havah is called "the mother of all living", but she was obviously not the mother of lions and oxen.
  2. Reason refutes it. "The Lord your God is a consuming fire": fire is created and can be extinguished, so God cannot literally be fire.
  3. Another verse contradicts it. Bereshit 1 seems to have man and woman created together; Bereshit 2 has Havah made from Adam. One of them must be read non-literally.
  4. The tradition of the Hakhamim explains it otherwise. The Torah says forty lashes; the tradition is thirty-nine. "The fruit of a beautiful tree" is the etrog. "Between your eyes" means tefillin on the head, centred, not literally between the eyes.

There is no fifth reason. Saadiah cites people who read "do not kindle fire on Shabbat" as a ban on armed battle, or the ban on hames as a ban on adultery: none of the four applies, so these readings are simply wrong.

Reasons three and four are straightforward. But the first two are vague: whose senses, whose reason?

The Rambam's two principles

The Rambam (Moreh Nevuchim II:25) gives sharper criteria. Aristotle held the universe eternal; Plato held God shaped eternal matter. The Rambam writes that he does not reject Aristotle because of the verse "In the beginning God created": verses about creation could be reinterpreted more easily than all the verses describing God as having a body. He rejects it for two reasons:

  • Proof. God's incorporeality is proven by reason, so verses that say otherwise must be explained. The eternity of the universe was only an argument, never a proof, and an argument is not enough to abandon the plain meaning. (Science, the Rabbi noted, later confirmed that the universe had a beginning.)
  • The foundations of the faith. God having no body contradicts nothing in the Torah. But Aristotle's eternal, unchanging universe would rule out miracles and prophecy and "pull down the foundations of our religion". Plato's view, by contrast, would not.

So a verse may be reinterpreted only when there is real proof against its plain meaning and the reinterpretation does not undermine a foundation of the faith. The Rambam does not list the foundations, and the Rabbi said we must use common sense.

Applying the framework

  • Sinai: God speaking to the whole people is foundational. You cannot allegorise it, and nobody can prove a negative anyway.
  • The snake and Bilam's donkey: not foundational, and you may read them as inner experiences. The Rambam himself holds that the donkey never spoke.
  • The Avot: to say they never existed undermines a foundation, and he mentioned the controversy over the Rambam's writings, when some in Provence allegorised misvot and the Avot.
  • Angels: the Rambam holds the three visitors to Avraham and the one who wrestled Yaakov appeared in a vision; Yaakov's limp is like waking from a nightmare short of breath. The Ramban strongly objects that this contradicts the Torah.

"You're never going to get a clear black and white answer. I think we have a framework at least in which to work." The next class would be on rabbinic authority.

He closed, in the Three Weeks, by noting that among the things we mourn is the loss of the Sanhedrin. Without it there is no body with authority to reinterpret or to change long-standing practice, so we remain bound by the rulings and customs that have come down to us.

Summary

  • The Torah is many-layered, yet every verse keeps its plain meaning unless there is a valid reason to depart from it.
  • Saadiah Gaon allows a non-literal reading only when the senses, reason, another verse or the tradition require it.
  • The Rambam adds two tests: real proof against the plain meaning, and no harm to a foundation of the faith.
  • Sinai and the Avot cannot be allegorised; a speaking donkey can.
