PART 1 — Chapter 8: The Term מקום (Makom / Place)

The Two Meanings of Makom

Meaning 1 — Physical Space (the literal meaning)

ועיקר קריאת מונח זה היא למקום מיוחד או כללי The primary meaning is a specific or general physical space.

  • Meyuchad (specific): "He's sitting in this house, on this chair, in this room"
  • Klali (general): "He's in this neighborhood / this city"

Either way — the literal makom is the physical space something occupies.

Meaning 2 — Status / Rank (a borrowed, conceptual sense)

ולאחר מכן הורחבה משמעותו... והפך למונח לדרגת האדם ומעלתו It was broadened to denote a person's status and level — his standing in a particular subject.

  • "פלוני במקום פלוני בנושא פלוני" — X is at place X in subject X (e.g., "which place are you up to in the Gemara?" — a conceptual place, not geographical)
  • The Gemara: ממלא מקום אבותיו — one who "fills the place of his fathers" — i.e., follows them בחכמה וביראה (in wisdom and fear), not a geographical spot
  • The Mishnah: ועדיין המחלוקת במקומה עומדת — "the debate still stands in its place" — i.e., in its status (במעמדה), not a physical location

This conceptual usage exists in other languages too: in English, "I'm not in a good place today" — not a geographical statement, but about one's mental/emotional status. The same borrowing happens in Hebrew.

Applying It — "Baruch Kevod Hashem MiMekomo"

ברוך כבוד ה' ממקומו (Yechezkel) — said four times a day (before Shema, in Kedusha, in Uva Letzion, and at Mincha)

This does NOT mean "blessed is Hashem from the [physical] place where He is" — Hashem is not in a specific place (that would imply limitation/corporeality).

כלומר לפי דרגתו וגודל חלקו במציאות It means: according to His level and supreme standing in existence — His conceptual "place," which is incomparable / sui generis (אין שווה לו ולא דומה לו).

Word by word: Baruch (blessed) · Kevod Hashem (God's glory — either God Himself, or those who recognize His glory) · mimkomo (from His "place" = His unique conceptual standing in all of reality).

The whole point of the chapter: so you don't fall into thinking Hashem occupies a specific place — which would imply corporeality and limitation. His "place" is a unique conceptual standing, because nothing is comparable to Him.

A Crucial Aside — This Book Is NOT a Lexicon

דע שכל מונח שאנו מבארים לך בחיבור זה... אין מטרתנו בכך להאיר את מה שנזכר בו בלבד, אלא אנחנו פותחים בכך שער

Rambam's purpose isn't merely to define words. Each word's explanation is a key (מפתח) — opening gates to understand all the places that term appears across Tanakh and Chazal, for our philosophical purpose, not for linguistic interest.

ואתה תתבונן בספרי הנבואה... ותבין כל מונח רב משמעי במשמעות המתאימה לו Once you learn this book, go back and study Tanakh again — now reading each multi-meaning term in its proper context. Our words are a key to this work and others.

Applying It Again — "Hinei Makom Iti" (Ki Tisa)

After the golden calf, Moshe asks הראני נא את כבודך ("show me Your glory"). Hashem responds:

הנה מקום אתי ונצבת על הצור — "Behold, there is a place with Me, and you shall stand on the rock"

דרגת עיון והתבוננות השכל — this means a level of intellectual contemplation — not a physical eye/place. זאת בנוסף למקום בהר... שבו הייתה התבודדות — this is in addition to the simple meaning that there was a physical spot on the mountain.

The deeper meaning: Makom here = a rank. Hashem tells Moshe: "You asked to see My glory; I cannot give you the whole, but I will raise you to a new intellectual place (conceptual understanding of Me) you've never reached before."

A Key Methodological Principle (will recur often)

Rambam does NOT remove the literal meaning unless there is a need to do so. Here the verse still literally means a physical place on the mountain — and also carries the deeper meaning of an intellectual ascent.

Rashi on This Verse

הנה מקום אתי — peshat: "there is a place prepared for your need where you'll stand and behold what you behold" (sheltered so you won't be harmed). Midrash: המקום אתי ואינו אומר אני במקום — Hashem says "the place is with Me," not "I am in a place" — because הקדוש ברוך הוא מקומו של עולם ואין עולמו מקומו — God is the "place" of the world; the world is not His place.

The Gemara (Berachot 7a)

At this moment, Moshe also asked Hashem to explain צדיק ורע לו, רשע וטוב לו — why the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper. He didn't receive a full answer — but it shows this was an intellectual encounter and an opening for philosophical inquiry, confirming makom here is a conceptual place.

Developing the Concept — Makom in Other Places

1. "Ayeh Mekom Kevodo" (Keter / Musaf)

כבודו מלא עולם, ומשרתיו שואלים: איה מקום כבודו להעריצו "His glory fills the world, and His ministers ask: where is the place of His glory, to revere Him?"

Not "where geographically is God?" — rather: where is the intellectual space to grasp Him? How can any creation — even angels — comprehend God's glory and presence?

The drawn-out "Ayeh" (Sephardi / Arizal custom): the old Iraqi and Syrian custom of extending ayeh (sometimes for a full minute — the highlight of Shabbat morning). The likely reason: as we say ayeh, we are straining to attain an intellectual conception of Hakadosh Baruch Hu — where, how, can we reach this?

The Arizal on the Yamim Nora'im: there are four times we say ayeh (Rosh Hashanah Day 1 Musaf, Day 2 Musaf, Yom Kippur Musaf, Ne'ilah). At each, pray for one of three things only: (1) Ruach Hakodesh/prophecy, (2) parnasah, (3) righteous children. (One custom: choose differently for the first three; the fourth — what you choose — defines what you most want in life.) A beautiful link: as we conceptualize Hashem (ayeh), we draw near to prophecy, to becoming good role models (→ righteous children), and to recognizing all comes from Hashem (→ parnasah).

2. "HaMakom" as a Name of Hashem

ברוך המקום ברוך הוא (Pesach Haggadah); המקום ינחם אתכם בתוך שאר אבלי ציון וירושלים (Ashkenazi consolation)

Why is Hashem called "HaMakom"? Simply: because He is the "place" of the entire universe — His presence sustains all existence (as Rashi said). (Per Rambam's chapter, one could add: because His standing is sui generis — incomparable — He is "the Place.")

The Gematria (attributed to the Vilna Gaon): the square of the Name יקוק = 186 (10² + 5² + 6² + 5² = 100 + 25 + 36 + 25 = 186). And המקום = 40 + 100 + 6 + 40 = 186 (with the kollel). The two are equal.

Why this matters (via the Ramchal, Da'at Tevunot) — why Ashkenazim say "HaMakom yenachem" at a shiva: יקוק is Hashem's Name of mercy — but its mercy is not always recognized in life (we see much that seems to contradict it). The square represents the Name fully manifested / drawn out. When does a person most question Hashem's mercy? When losing a loved one (when middat hadin is most apparent). So we bless them: HaMakom yenachem — may the Name of mercy (יקוק) be fully squared, manifested, and felt in your life — that you not only know theoretically that יקוק is mercy, but actually experience it, and so be comforted.

3. Makom in the Akeidah

Four times in a short span:

וילך אל המקום אשר אמר לו האלהים · וירא את המקום מרחוק · ויבאו אל המקום · ויקרא אברהם שם המקום ההוא ה' יראה

Abarbanel (500 years ago) cites those who argued — based on this very Rambam — that because makom can mean a conceptual place and is stressed four times, the Akeidah never physically happened (only in a vision).

Abarbanel demolishes this. The Akeidah physically happened — two real people, Avraham and Yitzchak, saddled a donkey and traveled. As Rambam himself taught (on hinei makom iti), one removes the literal meaning only when there is a necessity — a logical or scientific problem. Here there is none. The emphasis on makom teaches that through this process they ascended to new intellectual levels — it's both a physical place and a conceptual ascent.

4. The Makom of Gan Eden (to be developed later)

Was Hashem placing Adam in Gan Eden a physical/geographical space — or a heightened intellectual space to understand God, from which he was banished after the sin? (Raised here as something to think about; to be revisited later in the book.)

Chapter 8 Summary

Makom means (1) a specific/general physical space, or (2) a rank/conceptual standing. Hence: Baruch kevod Hashem mimekomo = according to His incomparable conceptual standing; hinei makom iti = an intellectual ascent (while still literally a spot on the mountain). Applied further: ayeh mekom kevodo (where is the intellectual space to grasp Him), HaMakom as a Name (He is the place of the world), the Akeidah (physical and an ascent), and Gan Eden (to come).

PART 2 — Chapter 9: The Term כסא (Kisei / Throne)

Meaning 1 — A Chair / Throne (the literal meaning)

קביעותו העיקרית בלשון היא שמו של הכיסא The primary meaning is a seat.

Crucial linguistic point: In Hebrew there is no separate word for "chair" vs. "throne" — kisei is both. Why? Because a chair was once a luxury — ordinary people sat on the ground; only the great had seats. (Modern Hebrew now needs both because everyone has chairs.)

וכיון שעל הכיסא היו יושבים רק בעלי הרוממות והגדולה כמו המלכים... והכיסא נעשה לדבר המורה גדולתו של מי שראוי לו Since only the eminent (kings, dignitaries) sat on a seat, the seat became an indicator of the importance of the one who sits on it.

Meaning 2 — Something of Importance/Majesty (borrowed sense)

Because the seat denotes importance, kisei is borrowed for important things:

The Temple Is Called "Kisei"

נקרא המקדש כיסא... כיון שהוא מורה על גדולתו של מי שהתגלה בו והשרה את אורו The Mikdash is called a throne because it indicates the greatness of the One whose holiness rests there. כסא כבוד מרום מראשון מקום מקדשנו (Yirmiyahu) — "Throne of glory, exalted from the beginning, the place of our Temple."

The Heavens Are Called "Kisei"

נקרא השמים כיסא... משום שהם מורים... גדולתו של מי שיצרם ומנהיגם The heavens (the entire celestial order — galaxies, celestial bodies) are called a throne because, to one who understands them, they display the greatness of their Creator. השמים כסאי והארץ הדום רגלי (Yeshayahu — Haftarah of Shabbat Rosh Chodesh) — "The heavens are My throne, the earth My footstool." I.e., they indicate My existence (מציאותי), My greatness, and My power to sustain them — just as a throne indicates the importance of the one fit for it.

Rambam highly valued astronomy and astrophysics (the paths and composition of celestial bodies) as genuine chochmah through which one apprehends God — while rejecting astrology (the claim that celestial bodies influence humans), as he makes clear in his letters.

A supporting proof (Rabbi Kada — surprised Rambam didn't cite it): Pharaoh to Yosef — רק הכסא אגדל ממך ("only by the throne will I be greater than you," Miketz). This is not a bigger physical chair — it means "only in importance will I exceed you." Kisei = importance, not merely a physical seat.

The Principle Stated

לא שיש גוף שהאלוה שוכן עליו... כי עוד יתבאר לך שהוא יתעלה אינו גוף NOT that there is a body upon which God sits — for He has no body; so how could He have a place (Ch. 8) or a throne? Rather, any place Hashem glorified and singled out with His light — like the Mikdash or the heavens — is called kisei.

This runs against the Midrash (Bereishit Rabbah 1:4) that the Kisei HaKavod was one of the things preceding creation — which Rambam must either reinterpret or not accept.

A Stylistic Observation (Rabbi Kada) — How Rambam Teaches

Most people give a drasha by: quote the verse → raise a question → give the answer → "from here we learn." Rambam consistently does the opposite. He first lays the foundations ("what does makom/kisei mean generally?"), and only then brings the verse — so you are led naturally to the conclusion rather than starting "on the back foot" with a problem. A more elegant mode of delivery: build the lesson, then discover it in the Torah.

Meaning 3 — Kisei as God Himself (the verse in Beshalach)

After Amalek:

ויבן משה מזבח ויקרא שמו ה' נסי, ויאמר: כי יד על כס יה, מלחמה לה' בעמלק מדור דור "...and he said: for there is a hand upon the throne (kes) of God — Hashem has war with Amalek in every generation."

Why neither Meaning 1 nor 2 fits: It can't be a physical throne (God has no body); and "something important" doesn't fit the sentence either. So what does kes Yah mean here?

מה שהלשון הרחיב בו... הרי הוא תיאור גדולתו ורוממותו... שאין ראוי לדמיין שום דבר מחוץ לעצמותו ולא נברא מנבראיו It is a description of God's own greatness — and one must not imagine anything outside His essence.

כך שיתעלה נמצא בלי כסא ונמצא עם כסא — זו כפירה בלי ספק To imagine God sometimes with a throne and sometimes without — is outright heresy.

כבר נאמר במפורש: אתה ה' לעולם תשב, כסאך לדור ודור (Eicha) — "You, Hashem, dwell forever; Your throne is for all generations" — מה שמורה שהוא דבר שלא נדמהו ממנו — showing the throne is not distinct from God.

ולפיכך הכוונה בכסא... תפארתו וגדולתו שאינו חוץ מעצמותו Therefore "kisei" here means God's own splendor and greatness — not something outside His essence.

The Underlying Principle — The Unity of God

God's absolute unity forbids speaking of an attribute as separate from Him. With a human we say "X is wise, handsome, generous" — three things X has and could lose. This cannot apply to God: His unity demands everything is included in the One. So one cannot speak of "the throne of Hashem" as though there were Hashem and His throne — even conceptually — because that would imply sometimes a throne, sometimes not. Rejected on philosophical grounds.

So the Verse Means:

Moshe swears by raising his hand — kisei Yah = Hashem Himself. Raising the hand always signals an oath (cf. Eliezer: וישם העבד את ידו תחת ירך אברהם... וישבע). So כי יד על כס יה = Moshe lifts his hand in an oath upon Hashem Himself. (This is unlike Onkelos, who reads it differently — for another time.)

Chapter 9 Summary — The Three Meanings of Kisei

  1. A physical seat/throne (literal)
  2. Something majestic/important — the Mikdash, the heavens (which display God's greatness; cf. rak hakisei egdal mimcha)
  3. God Himself (in kes Yah) — because the unity of God forbids treating "His throne" as anything distinct from His essence

A doorway to later study: this prepares us to understand the visions of Yechezkel and Yeshayahu "seeing Hashem on an elevated throne" — i.e., a deep intellectual apprehension of God's majesty, not a literal seated figure.
