Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 15, Seifim 27-31 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 2 April 2025

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The Rabbi began from the end of Siman 15, Seifim 27 to 31. So far the siman went through all the forbidden relationships, those from the Torah and those from the Rabbis, the second-degree relatives (sheniyot) and the disagreements about how many generations. One of these is the Torah prohibition of marrying your wife's sister: it applies even if you divorce your wife, but if your wife dies, you may marry her sister. Today the question is what happens when a mistake is made, with this prohibition and others.

The Mishnah in Yevamot: two cases

Case 1. A man's wife went abroad. He was told she died, so he married her sister. Then the wife came back. This relationship with the sister was wrong, and it carried karet, but it was beshogeg (by mistake). The Mishnah rules:

  • He continues living with his first wife.
  • He may marry the relatives of his sister-in-law (her mother, daughter and so on). Had his wife really died and he married her sister, those relatives would be forbidden. But since the wife came back, he was never really married to the sister-in-law (the kiddushin does not take effect where there is karet), so the relationship was only zenut, and a man may marry the relatives of a woman he had zenut with.
  • If his wife later dies, he may go back and marry the sister-in-law, since the prohibition is gone and nothing remains from what he did by mistake.

Case 2. He was told his wife died and married her sister, and had children. Then it turned out the wife died only later than they thought. A child born while the wife was still alive is a mamzer (because one who is liable to karet creates a mamzer, as we learned in Siman 4). Children born after her death are kosher.

The Gemara: the brother-in-law case, and Rabbi Akiva

A broader case. Suppose both the man's wife and her sister's husband (his brother-in-law) were abroad, and testimony came that both had died. He married his sister-in-law, and then both came back. The sister-in-law and her husband cannot stay together. Even though it was by mistake (beones), a woman who had relations with another man while married is forbidden to her husband, and in any event Hazal made a penalty (kenas) in Yevamot for such a mistaken case. The Tanna Kamma says that the man himself may still stay with his own wife. The other view in the Gemara says no: since his act caused a forbidden relationship, his own relationship is also forbidden.

Rabbi Akiva and the arusah. Where a marriage does not take effect because of karet, the kiddushin is void, yet a get is given because of what people will think (marit ayin). Rabbi Akiva adds the case of the wife's sister: here too a get is needed. The Gemara asks: if so, in our case the man would have to give a get to his sister-in-law, and then his first wife would be forbidden to him, and the Mishnah says he can stay with her. The answer is that Rabbi Akiva was speaking about a different case: a man who only betrothed (kiddesh) the first woman. She went abroad, he heard she died, and he married her sister. People would say that the first kiddushin was al tenai (conditional) and failed, so he was free to marry the second woman, so the second marriage was real. Therefore he must give her a get, and then he cannot take back the first woman, because she is now forbidden as the sister of a woman he divorced. Everyone agrees with this. The Rabbi explained that this is more than marit ayin: it is a decree that people will say a married woman may go out without a get.

So two rulings are agreed by everyone (muskam lekhulei alma):

  1. If he was married to the first woman, he may stay with her, no get is needed for the second.
  2. If he only betrothed her, he must give a get to the second woman, and he is forbidden to both.

Seif 27

The Shulhan Arukh begins with the betrothal case. A man betrothed a woman, she went abroad, testimony came that she died, he married her sister, and then she came back. He is forbidden to live with either of them in the future, and both need a get. All the laws of Yevamot and the penalties apply. He is forbidden to both forever, but if one of them dies, he can marry the other.

The second case: he married her, she traveled, and she did not die. He needs no get for the second woman, stays with his wife, may marry the second woman's relatives, and if his wife dies he may marry the woman he was with. The reason for a get in the arusah case is that people may suspect the first kiddushin failed and the second marriage was real. When he was married, people would not think the marriage fell apart. Tosafot adds that even if that happens, it is very uncommon, so the decree still applies.

The Rema. He adds: if a man did zenut with a woman who is forbidden to him, this does not affect his relationship with his wife, and he may marry the relatives of that woman, because it was only zenut. He gave the example of a man's wife's mother or her daughter from another marriage: if he had zenut with the daughter, that does not affect his wife, and he may marry the daughter's relatives who are not connected to his wife. Then the Rema adds a limit: if the woman is ragil (regularly around his house), it becomes a problem and the Rema requires a divorce. The Rabbi said "regular" still needs to be defined.

A question on the Rema. In Seif 13 we learned about bat anusato: according to the Torah you may marry the relatives of a woman you raped, but the Rabbis forbade it during her lifetime, in case he ends up marrying the first woman. The Helkat Mehokek and the Beit Shemuel point out that this applies here as well: the Rema's permission to marry the relatives can only be after the death of one of them. Otherwise the rabbinic decree applies.

Why is this different in the Shulhan Arukh's case? In the main case, the man was told his wife died, and then she came back, and still he may marry the relatives of the second woman. Why does the rabbinic decree not apply? The Taz, and the Beit Shemuel too, say the decree was made for someone who did zenut deliberately, since he is willing to cross boundaries. Here the first act was a mistake, so the decree does not apply. The Bah disagrees and says that the Shulhan Arukh meant it only after one of them dies.

Seif 28: the brother-in-law case

This is the case of the Gemara: a man whose wife and brother-in-law both went away and both returned. The Rif and the Rosh rule, and the Shulhan Arukh too, that the sister-in-law needs a get and is forbidden to both men, and even the man's own wife is forbidden to him. This is true whether she was married or only arusah. The Gemara says that we do not say mitokh (because she is forbidden to one, she is also forbidden to the other), but Rabbi Akiva does say it, and for some reason the halakhah follows Rabbi Akiva.

The Rabbi summed up three rulings from the sugya: with only a betrothal and a mistake, he cannot stay with either woman unless one dies; with a marriage and a mistake, he stays with his wife and may marry the second woman's relatives (the Taz says so even for the relatives, since it was all a mistake); and if the sister-in-law's husband also came back, both men must divorce.

A hakirah. The Rabbi raised a further question. A man did zenut with his sister-in-law, who was married to another man. So he did two things at once: eshet ish and ahot ishto. She cannot stay with her husband. Now both innocent parties, his wife and her husband, die. Can the two who did zenut marry? The principle is keshem sheasurah labaal kakh asurah laboel (a woman forbidden to her husband is also forbidden to the man she was with). That principle never needed to apply at the time, since she was forbidden anyway. So we could say the principle never took effect and she is now permitted, or that it applies now that she is free of her husband. The Rabbi did not give a conclusion. In discussion it was agreed that the question arises whether the husband died or divorced her.

The Hida. The Hida adds a practical point. If you only had a shiddukh (what we call engagement today, not halakhic kiddushin) with a girl and it was broken off, you may marry her sister. But the Hida says it will be awkward and cause enmity, and he advises against it.

Seif 29

The mamzer law from the Mishnah. If the wife died later than he thought, a child born during her lifetime is a mamzer, and a child born afterwards is not. The Rabbi briefly noted that a similar case with an eshet ish whose husband returns can also create a mamzer, but he did not develop it.

Seifim 30-31

Seif 30: a concubine (pilegesh). If a man had a pilegesh and there were no witnesses to kiddushin, he may marry her relatives. If she says he betrothed her and there are witnesses to her statement, she is forbidden to all his relatives, but he can still marry hers: her statement alone is not taken seriously against him.

Seif 31: a daughter married off by her father. A man married a woman whose father had given her in marriage thinking she was a naarah, but she was actually a bogeret. The father had no authority, so there was no kiddushin. He may marry her relatives. But as we learned in many places, if he gave a get, the get makes him forbidden to her relatives, even though no get was needed.

That is the end of Siman 15.

Practical summary

  • A man who was told his wife died and married her sister, and then his wife returned, stays with his wife, needs no get for the sister, and may marry the sister's relatives.
  • If he had only betrothed the first woman, he must give a get to the second, and he is forbidden to both until one of them dies.
  • If his own wife and the sister's husband both returned, the sister cannot stay with her husband, and the man cannot stay with his wife, so both men divorce.
  • Zenut with a forbidden woman does not affect the marriage or the relatives, but the Helkat Mehokek and Beit Shemuel say that during her lifetime the rabbinic decree still applies (the Taz and Beit Shemuel exclude a mistaken case).
  • Children born while the first wife was alive are mamzerim, and those born after she died are not.
  • A get given in a case where none was needed still forbids the relatives.
