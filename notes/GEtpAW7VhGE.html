Topic: Yishuv Eres Yisrael: whether living in the Land is a misvah, when one may leave, and the misvah to love the Land Sources: The Ramban's additions to the Rambam's Sefer HaMisvot, Positive Misvah 4; Ketubot 110b-111a; Iggerot Moshe, Even HaEzer 1:102 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom on the evening of Yom HaZikaron, Tuesday 3 May 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The occasion

The Rabbi opened a new series of classes leading up to Shavuot. That evening was Yom HaZikaron, when we remember those who fell defending the State of Israel, "may their memory be for a blessing", and the following night was Yom HaAsmaut, seventy-four years since the founding of the State. So he devoted the class to three halachic questions about the Land: Is there a misvah to live in Eres Yisrael? Once there, may one leave? And is there a misvah to love the Land even if we do not live in it?

The Ramban: a misvah in its own right

The Gemara gives the number 613 but not the list. The Rambam counted the misvot systematically in his Sefer HaMisvot, with fourteen principles, and the Ramban wrote glosses listing misvot he believed the Rambam had missed.

The fourth positive misvah the Ramban adds is to conquer and settle the land promised to our forefathers, and not to leave it to other nations or to desolation. His source is the verse in Parashat Masei: "You shall take possession of the land and settle in it, for I have given you the land to possess it" (Bamidbar 33:53). He brings support from the Gemara in Ketubot, that one who lives outside the Land is "as if he serves idolatry", and from the Sifre, where sages leaving the Land wept and recited this verse.

There is a further proof. The Gemara rules that if one spouse wishes to move to Eres Yisrael and the other refuses, the one who wishes to go can compel the other, and if necessary the marriage is ended, with the ketubah paid or forfeited according to who is refusing. The Rambam himself codifies this in the Mishneh Torah. So why did he not count settling the Land as a misvah?

The Megillat Esther and the three oaths

Rabbi Yishak de Leon, author of the Megillat Esther, wrote to defend the Rambam against the Ramban. He suggests that the Rambam saw settling the Land as a misvah only for the time of conquest and the Temple, and does not count misvot that are not for all generations.

He points to two passages in Ketubot. One says that whoever goes up from Bavel to Eres Yisrael transgresses a positive command, because of a verse in Yirmiyahu. The other expounds the oaths in Shir HaShirim: Hashem made Israel swear not to go up to the Land en masse and by force, and not to rebel against the nations; and He made the nations swear not to oppress Israel too greatly. The Rabbi explained that these "three oaths" are the main source of the anti-Zionist position held by some Hasidic groups.

He then raised three difficulties with this approach: - The Rambam's own ruling on compelling a spouse shows that he gave living in the Land halachic weight. - Aggadah is not halacha. As a general principle we do not build halacha from the homiletic parts of the Talmud, and the oaths are a homily on Shir HaShirim. - A broken oath. In Hilchot Nedarim, when two sides swear to each other and one side breaks the oath, the other is released. The nations swore not to oppress Israel too greatly. After the history of persecution, it is argued, our side of the oath no longer binds.

He also mentioned the argument that the oath applies only when the nations object, and that it lapsed once the nations recognised the Jewish claim to the Land, but noted that this is debatable.

Two better explanations of the Rambam

The Sifre, on the verse in the Shema about exile, says Hashem tells Israel to keep the misvot even in exile, "so that they will not be new to you when you return", like a king who sends his queen to her parents' home but asks her to keep dressing as a queen. The Ramban, quoting this, writes that the main fulfilment of all the misvot is for those who dwell in the Land, and that settling the Land is equal to all the misvot of the Torah.

The Rambam's fourth principle is not to count commands that encompass the whole Torah, such as "You shall be holy". Rav Kook suggests that settling the Land is one of these, the setting in which all the misvot are fully kept, so the Rambam agrees with the Ramban but does not list it.

The Avnei Nezer suggests that where one misvah enables another, the Rambam counts only one, as he counts building the Mishkan but not housing the Aron in it; settling the Land is included in conquering it.

The Rabbi said he himself inclines to the view that the Rambam did hold there is a misvah, since this is what the Mishneh Torah implies.

What kind of misvah?

Some misvot are obligatory, like eating massah on the night of Pesah. Others are fulfilled when done but not required, like voluntary sedakah. Which is this?

Rav Moshe Feinstein (Iggerot Moshe, Even HaEzer 1:102) writes that most authorities follow the Ramban that there is a misvah to live in the Land, but it is not an obligation that would forbid living in the diaspora. One who settles there fulfils a misvah. He adds that a person considering the move should first think about whether he will be able to keep all the misvot there properly.

Rav Eliezer Melamed argues that it is obligatory today. In the past, he says, Jews were prevented from fulfilling it; now that there is a state and no compulsion, the obligation applies. He cites the Kuzari, where the king asks why the Jews have not gone to live in their land, and the rabbi answers, "You have shamed me." Our prayers about Sion, says the Kuzari, are like the chirping of birds if we do not act on them. Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi acted on this: he gave up his life in Spain and set out for the Land.

The Rabbi's conclusion: given how Jewish communities have flourished across the world for two thousand years, he finds it hard to call it an obligation, and Rav Moshe Feinstein's view, a voluntary misvah, is the more relatable one.

May one leave the Land?

The Gemara and the Rambam permit leaving Eres Yisrael for only three reasons: to learn Torah, to marry, or for business. Business includes even someone comfortable who wishes to increase his trade.

So what about a holiday abroad? Rav Ovadia Yosef, in Yehaveh Daat, rules that it is forbidden to leave for a pleasure trip, even if one intends to return, since it falls under none of the three reasons. Rav Shmuel Wosner, in Shevet HaLevi, argued that the prohibition is on leaving to live abroad, and a short trip is permitted. The Rabbi added that in the time of the Hakhamim, travel took months or years, and someone who left might not come back. With a return ticket and a trip of a few days, there are grounds to permit it. But, he said, one should not leave without a purpose.

Loving the Land

"You shall eat and be satisfied, and bless Hashem your God for the good land He has given you" (Devarim 8:10). The Gemara tells that Rabbi Abba would kiss the stones of Akko, and Rabbi Hanina would repair the roads. The Rambam brings this in the Mishneh Torah: the great sages would kiss the borders of the Land, kiss its stones and roll in its dust. Since the Rambam codifies halacha, not inspiration, love of the Land is a halachic duty, expressed in whatever way suits each person.

The sin of the spies was speaking ill of the Land, rejecting "the desirable land" (Tehillim 106). Rashi explains that Rabbi Hanina mended the roads so that no traveller whose wagon broke would speak badly of the Land. So there is a misvah to speak well of it.

A question for those abroad

Most of the class lived outside Israel, and the Rabbi said that while preparing he found himself asking what we are doing here. There can be genuine reasons, such as work or family, and it is most likely not a transgression. But it is a misvah, and the Land is the main place of the misvot. We should keep a real aspiration to live there one day, and at the very least support the Land and its people in whatever way we can. He added that the misvah is about living in the Land and spending time there, not about any political position, and there is value even in visiting.

Practical summary

  • Most authorities follow the Ramban that living in Eres Yisrael is a misvah; the Rabbi inclines to the view that the Rambam agrees.
  • Following Rav Moshe Feinstein, he regards it as a voluntary misvah rather than an obligation, though some, such as Rav Eliezer Melamed, hold it obligatory today.
  • One may leave the Land to learn Torah, to marry or for business. Rav Ovadia Yosef forbids leaving for a holiday; Rav Wosner permits a short trip, and the Rabbi sees grounds for this with a return ticket, but not leaving for no purpose.
  • Loving the Land and speaking well of it is a misvah, which the Rambam codifies from the practice of the sages.
  • Those living abroad should keep a real aspiration to live there and support the Land and its people.
