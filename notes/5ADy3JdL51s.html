Topic: Why Rosh Hashanah comes before Yom Kippur, why its prayers contain no sins and no requests, and Yihud Hashem, the unity of God Sources: The Rosh Hashanah Amidah; Sukkah 52a; the Rambam's Guide for the Perplexed Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada to a small group before Rosh Hashanah, Thursday 19 September 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A peculiar title

The Rabbi called the shiur "What not to pray for on Rosh Hashanah." We are usually told what to pray for, so the title sounds strange. He asked the group what they thought, and one answer came close: not your own personal needs, but the common good.

He then raised one of the most famous questions of the season. Rosh Hashanah is Yom HaDin, the day of judgment, and Yom Kippur is the day of atonement. Why does Hashem set it up so that we come to judgment before we have had the chance to apologise? Surely it would make more sense to come clean on Yom Kippur first, with a fresh slate, and only then stand in judgment.

No sins and no requests

Two observations make the question sharper. - No mention of sin. Although it is a day of judgment, nowhere in the Rosh Hashanah prayers is there an acknowledgement of our sins. "If you do find it, it wasn't part of the original prayer, it was added in." There are a couple of very small references, added much later. - No requests. In the additions for Rosh Hashanah that the Hakhamim instituted in Shaharit and Musaf, nothing is asked for. Zokhrenu LeHayim and the requests to be inscribed in the Book of Life are later additions, from about a thousand years ago. The regular text of the Amidah stays, with its usual requests, just as on Shabbat.

This seems odd. In court you plead for everything and give it your best. Understanding why, he said, makes Rosh Hashanah "far less stressful" and much more meaningful.

Yihud Hashem

The principle behind all of this is Yihud Hashem, the unity of God. The struggle today is not believing in one God rather than two. Early thinkers, and the Gnostics, found it easier to imagine a god of light and a god of darkness, because one God makes the problem of evil so hard. Judaism insists there is one God. But the deeper question is whether we really see every aspect of reality through the lens of HaKadosh Barukh Hu, and believe that nothing can happen to anyone without Hashem willing it.

That is easier said than believed. We cover our eyes for Shema because we cannot yet see it: the world seems full of different powers, much good and much evil. We say Barukh shem kevod malkhuto quietly because only one day will His presence be revealed for all to recognise. And we say Shema so often, morning, evening and before sleep, not to be reminded that there are not two gods, but that there is nothing else: everything emanates from Him.

Sin as an illusion

If we truly believed this, he said, we would never sin. When I sin knowingly, I am telling myself that something against God's will is worth pursuing. But if everything comes from Hashem, that good must be an illusion.

He told a Hasidic story of a man who came to the marketplace offering to sell the secret of life, held in his closed hand. The whole town chased him, and when they finally forced his hand open, there was nothing there. And the Gemara in Sukkah describes how, in the future, the righteous will see the evil inclination as a great mountain and wonder how they conquered it, while the wicked will see it as a thread of hair and wonder how they failed to. To the saddik it looks enormous, but there is nothing inside.

Knowing God through what He is not

The purpose of our entire religion is to reveal the oneness of God. Since God is infinite and we are finite, we cannot grasp any of His positive attributes. When we say God is merciful, we only see mercy through our own finite lens. This is negative theology, which Saadiah held before the Rambam and the Rambam developed in the Guide for the Perplexed. The one thing we can fully grasp is a negative: He is one, not two.

So when we look at everything, including evil, and say it is not outside the reality of God, but a test, a temptation, an illusion, we affirm His oneness. Every time a person withstands temptation, it is an affirmation of Yihud Hashem.

He asked everyone, "for myself as well," to think before Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur how often each day we make a conscious decision to do right. Much of what we do is habit. A conscious choice is deciding to be kind, not to speak lashon hara, to give charity or time beyond our means, to make the call we know is right.

The Amidah and the CEO

Read the English of the Rosh Hashanah Amidah, he urged. It speaks only of the sovereignty of God: that everyone will recognise Him as one and in charge. "All wickedness will vanish like smoke": smoke looks like something, but you touch it and there is nothing there. That is the whole idea of Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot: making Hashem King.

He gave a parable. In a huge company you get five minutes a year with the CEO. One employee asks for a pay rise, another for a new laptop. A third says: I see your vision for this company; how can I contribute to it? That person will be given whatever he needs. When Yihud Hashem is revealed there is no evil and everything is perfect. "That's essentially what we mean by Mashiah": everyone recognises the sovereignty of God.

"Rosh Hashanah is not about you." If it becomes give me this, give me that, we have missed the point. The Zohar compares those who pray on Rosh Hashanah only for children, life and sustenance to dogs barking at their master.

Why Rosh Hashanah comes first

Now the opening question is answered. Rosh Hashanah is a blank canvas on which we set a vision: how can I be part of Hashem's world? Yom Kippur is the details, the long confession of everything we did wrong, and the Ten Days of Teshuvah flesh the vision out. Big ideas without details never happen, but details without a vision are lost. The Rosh Hashanah prayers are universal, speaking of all nations and all humanity.

Approached like this, Rosh Hashanah is no longer an anxious day of worrying whether we prayed hard enough. When we are part of the vision, the rest follows.

He then offered what he called his own theory. The added requests for life and livelihood, some only three or four hundred years old, go against the character of the original prayer. He suggested they were added because these ideas are hard for many people, who come to synagogue mainly hoping things stay as they are. Like an appetiser at the start of a class, the additions keep their attention.

Questions from the group

Can we ever be free of self-interest? Someone asked how to want to be part of Hashem's vision while still hoping for money, health and good relationships. The Rabbi called it a strong question: can a human being ever lose their ego? The Hasidic masters believed complete self-negation was possible. Even Moshe, the humblest of men, sometimes grew angry. "All Hashem wants is your best." The discussion also touched on the idea, taken very far in Chabad thought, that Hashem is in everything, which raises the question of how we have any existence of our own.

What about fear of God? Someone suggested that knowing everything can be taken away humbles the ego. Serving out of fear for oneself is a low level, the Rabbi said, though we must always live with that recognition: "this too shall pass." When life is good, do not become haughty; when it is hard, do not despair. One reason we read the haftarah of Hannah and Peninah is to show how fickle life is. Fear is the first step to love, and the highest level is fear that comes from love: loving someone so deeply that you are afraid to put a foot wrong. Accepting the Torah at Sinai was not enough; on Purim we accepted it again, from love.

Summary

  • Rosh Hashanah's own prayers mention no sins and ask for nothing; such passages were added later.
  • Yihud Hashem means seeing that everything comes from Hashem, so the pull of sin is an illusion.
  • Each conscious choice to do right affirms Hashem's oneness.
  • Rosh Hashanah is not about our needs but about how we can be part of Hashem's vision.
  • That is why it comes before Yom Kippur: first the vision, then the details.
