Review of Part 1 so far

Part 1 is called Yesodot HaMesiut, the fundamentals of reality. Chapter 1 gave the principles of faith. Chapter 2 gave the purpose of creation: Hashem, the ultimate good, bestows the ultimate good by letting us cleave to Him, and gives us choice so that the good becomes our own. As Pirkei Avot says, do not serve for the sake of a prize: the misvah itself refines us and so brings us closer. Chapter 3 described body and soul, and the two consequences of Adam's sin: we must die and be resurrected, and the world must be destroyed and renewed. Chapter 4 showed how, living in a material world, we can raise it by using it rightly.

Questions

Will the world really be destroyed? The Gemara in Sanhedrin says the world will exist for 6,000 years and then lie desolate for a thousand. In the Ramhal's thinking this fits: just as the body must die before it is renewed, so must the world, since the refined bodies of the future would not fit the world as it is. The Rambam takes a different approach, in which eternity is for the soul. Most of the Rishonim and later rabbis, starting with the Ramban, follow the approach the Ramhal presents.

Is Gan Eden the same as Olam HaBa? No, that is a common error. What people call Gan Eden is usually Olam HaNeshamot, the world of souls, where the soul waits while the body is in the grave. The real reward is Olam HaBa, body and soul together after the resurrection. Your place in the world of souls matches your place in Olam HaBa, and it is "me'ein", a taste of it, as Shabbat is a sixtieth of the world to come.

How things come down: Hishtalshelut

Hishtalshelut means a chain. Things do not come straight from Hashem: it is not possible for Hashem to make a world directly from Himself, because it would be too intense. His energy is diluted, and it comes down to this world through the links of a chain. That is the theme of Chapter 5. The Ramhal never uses the word Kabbalah, but this chapter is the basis for understanding it. As always, before explaining, he first gives us the whole picture.

Paragraph 1: the parts of existence

Broadly, all that exists is either physical (gashmi) or spiritual (ruhani).

The physical is whatever can be sensed. It divides into the heavenly, the sun, moon, planets and stars, and the earthly: land, water, air and everything we touch.

Animals. The Rabbi asked whether an animal's consciousness is physical or spiritual. A participant answered that an animal has drives but cannot reason about why it acts, and the Rabbi said this is exactly what the Ramhal says. We have a nefesh, which all creatures share: primal instincts, eating, drinking, passing on our genes. Human beings also have a neshamah. Section 3 will explain that the soul has five levels: nefesh, ruah, neshamah, hayah and yehidah.

The spiritual is whatever is free of physicality and cannot be sensed. It divides into two:

  • Souls (neshamot): spiritual creations made to enter bodies and be bound to them closely.
  • Separate beings (nivdalim): spiritual creations that never enter bodies. They are of two kinds, forces (kohot) and angels (malakhim). There are many kinds of angels, just as there are countless kinds of fish or plants, but we call them one group.

So existence has five parts: the heavenly and the earthly; souls; forces and angels.

Exceptions. Hashem is not part of this scheme at all: He is beyond existence. Within creation there are two exceptions. One is the shedim, demons, which are not sensed and so are in a sense spiritual, but belong to this world and so are in a sense physical. The Ramhal gives them only a few lines and says they are an exception, without explaining further. The other exception is man.

Paragraph 2: man is unique

Man alone is made of two entirely different creations: a lofty soul, from the spiritual world, and a lowly body, from the physical world. "Be careful here," the Rabbi said: do not think animals are the same because they have feelings. The Ramhal says the soul of an animal is physical, the finest part of the physical world. We share that nefesh with animals, and in that we are very like them; but we also have a neshamah, a separate creation bound to the body. That is what makes the human being unique.

The next class turns to the forces and the angels, and how influence passes up and down between the spiritual and physical worlds. The Rambam, too, writes that there are ten levels of angels. The Rabbi called this paragraph "the Ramhal at his best": before telling us anything about forces or angels, he gives us the whole framework.

Summary

  • Chapter 5 explains how Hashem's influence comes down to the world through a chain of links, Hishtalshelut.
  • All existence is physical (heavenly and earthly) or spiritual (souls, and the separate beings: forces and angels).
  • An animal's soul is the finest part of the physical world; man alone joins a spiritual neshamah to a physical body.
  • Gan Eden, the world of souls, is a waiting room; the real reward is Olam HaBa after the resurrection.
