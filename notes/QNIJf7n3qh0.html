Topic: The life and bold responsa of Rav Yoseph Messas (1892-1974) of Meknes, Tlemcen and Haifa Sources: Rav Yoseph Messas, Mayim Hayim and Nahalat Avot Shiur: The third class in Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday series on the lives and works of our Hakhamim, given in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 18 July 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

From Jerusalem and London to Morocco

After Rav Uziel and Hakham Gagin of London, Manchester and Ramsgate, the series moved to Morocco. Someone had said this would be the best part of the series, and the Rabbi agreed: "We're going to see some incredible things." He read the responsa inside, since they are not well known.

His life

Rav Yoseph Messas was born in 1892 in Meknes, in northern Morocco. Morocco was not one tradition, the Rabbi stressed. The north was closer to Europe and more philosophical; the south was where the kabbalists flourished. Unlike Rav Uziel's, his parents were ordinary people. He studied in Meknes, including in a yeshiva set up by Ashkenazi rabbis concerned about French influence, and with Rav Berdugo.

  • At 16 he married Simhah, the same name as his mother. The Rabbi noted the Ashkenazi custom, from the will of Rabbi Yehudah HaHasid, not to marry a woman with one's mother's name; Sepharadim were never particular about this.
  • After 14 years without children he married a second wife, Rahel, without divorcing the first.
  • At 31 he became rabbi of Tlemcen in northern Algeria, where he served for 17 years.
  • He returned to Meknes as a dayan.
  • In 1964 he moved to Haifa, became its Chief Rabbi, and served until his death in 1974. In Israel he was known for visiting prisons and hospitals.

He should not be confused with his cousin, Rav Shalom Messas, Chief Rabbi of Jerusalem.

His main works are Mayim Hayim, his responsa, and Nahalat Avot, on Pirkei Avot. He wrote with a flowery, poetic style "not really paralleled by his contemporaries", wrote poems and even drew.

A posek of staggering confidence

"I haven't come across a posek in the last 200 years who has the self-confidence of this person." Many poskim end a lenient ruling with "only if three others agree with me". He did not. Yet he was a great talmid hakham, Chief Rabbi in Meknes, Tlemcen and Haifa, and respected even by those who disagreed strongly, such as Rav Ovadia Yoseph. "That's how Sepharadi hakhamim disagreed with each other."

Behind it all, the Rabbi said, was "total love and inclusivity". Rabbis like this worked to keep everyone together.

The butchers of Tlemcen

The most extreme example. "All of our butchers publicly desecrate Shabbat," he wrote. For years he spoke to the communal board and went to the butchers at night, pleading with words of love, and finally persuaded them all to close on Shabbat, on condition that no one opened. One stubborn butcher swore with every oath, "on the life of his children, on his wife", to close, and Rav Messas went home overjoyed. The next morning the man withdrew, the others gave up, and the board would not help.

Why does it matter? A Jew who publicly breaks Shabbat loses his trustworthiness (neemanut), so his meat cannot be relied on. But rumours were spreading that the community was eating forbidden meat, and he would not accept that. So he set out to show it was permitted, while still working to make them keep Shabbat:

  • He relied on the view that one loses trust only by publicly breaking a Torah prohibition that carries the death penalty, given the weakness of the generation.
  • He went through everything they did on Shabbat: skinning (done by non-Jews), cutting meat, breaking bones, sharpening knives, weighing on scales. Each, he argued, was rabbinic or not a capital offence.
  • They wrote accounts on Shabbat, in French. He relied on the Or Zarua, brought by the Rema, that writing in a language other than Hebrew is not a capital offence. "Nobody took this on," the Rabbi said.
  • Where almost everyone breaks Shabbat, doing so no longer shows contempt, so it does not cost a person his trustworthiness. Rav Yaakov Ettlinger, author of the Arukh LaNer and the Binyan Sion, had made a similar point about calling people up to the Torah; Rav Messas applied it to trusting their meat.
  • The butchers worked in twos and threes, sold the few non-kosher animals to Arab customers, and "hate each other", watching for any slip.

His conclusion: "Anyone who buys meat from them, your heart can be secure... eat with happiness." "This is absolutely incredible," the Rabbi said. "Literally somersaults in halacha."

Asked how a rabbi can be paid for working on Shabbat, the Rabbi said that is a separate discussion: teaching and leading prayer are not forbidden labour.

Carrying and hair covering

Carrying. In his town everyone carried on Shabbat without an eruv. He argued that there is almost no public domain from the Torah today, so the rabbinic decree against carrying in a semi-public area no longer applies there. Asked whether a decree stays even when its reason has gone, the Rabbi said that is the usual rule; Rav Messas answered that it stays only where the reason could return.

Hair covering. Asked what the wives of shohetim and dayanim relied on in going with their hair uncovered, he argued that hair is "nakedness" only where it is customary to cover it, as with unmarried girls. Since the custom everywhere had changed, uncovered hair was now their beauty and nothing immodest. "Shocking, no?" the Rabbi said. Rav Ovadia Yoseph wrote that one cannot generally rely on Rav Messas's rulings because they were written for a particular context. The Rabbi noted that such contexts still exist in many communities.

Wine, and Purim costumes

Wine of non-Jews. He quoted a letter his predecessor had written to the rabbis of France in 5630 (1870), defending a rabbi in France whose colleagues would not sit with him because he drank unsupervised wine. The prohibition was made because of intermarriage and assimilation, which he argued no longer applied, and the wine came from Muslims, who are not idolaters: treating them as such is itself wrong.

Purim costumes. He was not always lenient. He opposed dressing up on Purim, which he traced, after asking experts, to the Christian carnival and its licentiousness, and which had led to immodesty. The Rabbi noted that Spanish and Portuguese communities do not have the custom.

What we learn

"I'm not saying we should learn his books and be posek according to what he's saying." The point of the series is to show how great hakhamim approached the people in front of them. He was doing "somersaults" to judge his community favourably, and whether every argument is right, "maybe not". But we live in a difficult generation; many Jews have had no chance to learn. "Try do somersaults in your head not to judge them." Sepharadi communities have always been "a broad church", the most and least religious praying together, and we must not lose that.

Summary

  • Rav Yoseph Messas of Meknes, Tlemcen and Haifa was a great and respected posek with remarkable self-confidence.
  • His rulings on the Shabbat-breaking butchers, carrying, hair covering and wine of non-Jews went far beyond what others would say.
  • Rav Ovadia Yoseph disagreed strongly, and the Rabbi did not present these rulings as practice.
  • The lesson is his love for every Jew and his drive to keep the whole community together.
