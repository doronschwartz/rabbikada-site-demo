Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 3, Seifim 1-9 Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 29 November 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

Siman 3 deals with the laws of Kehunah (priesthood). The Rabbi said Seif 1 is the heart of the siman, because the other seifim mostly fall away once it is clear. In Siman 2 we saw that a man who says "I am Jewish" is believed. Kehunah is much stricter. The plan was to finish the siman, say something about Kohanim nowadays, and then begin Siman 4, which is mainly about mamzerut.

Seif 1: a man who says "I am a Kohen"

The Gemara in Ketubot, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say that a man who says he is a Kohen is not believed. He may not be called up first, may not do Birkat Kohanim and may not eat terumah. The Rabbi first distinguished two kinds of Kohen:

  • Kohen meyuhas: established by two full witnesses, and eats terumah by Torah law.
  • Kohen hazakah: treated as a Kohen by presumption. This siman is about him.

Why is a Kohen different from a Jew? The Rosh says there is a financial gain (revah mamon): the man and his descendants will eat terumah. Where there is financial gain we do not believe a person's own words. The Rabbi said this reasoning is central to Kohanim nowadays.

Ranking the three things. Eating terumah and Birkat Kohanim are the more serious, since a non-Kohen doing Birkat Kohanim breaks a prohibition. Being called up first is the least serious, since any Jew may have an aliyah.

The Remakh, the Maggid Mishneh and the Ran. The Remakh allowed calling him up first on his own word, since the worst result is that he relies on it to eat terumah, and Hazal made no decree about that. The Maggid Mishneh and the Ran gave a different reason: outside Israel there is no terumah by Torah law nowadays. The Shulhan Arukh follows the Gemara and the Rambam. The Rema brings the Remakh and allows an aliyah.

The Bah. The Rema also allows Birkat Kohanim, which those Rishonim never mentioned. The Bah answers that their only worry was that people would conclude he may eat terumah and gain money. Birkat Kohanim brings no gain. And outside Israel there is no terumah of the rabbis, so no problem. The Bah added that in Israel there would be a problem.

Place

Someone says "I am a Kohen"

Outside Israel

We call him up and allow Birkat Kohanim. The Rabbi said Sefaradim rely on this too.

Israel

A real concern, because of terumah. We should want confirmation from his previous community.

Pidyon HaBen. Here there is real money, five coins. The Hatam Sofer said this is rare, and the Kohen is usually someone the family knows. The Yaaves held that a Kohen nowadays must return the money. The Rabbi said this is why the custom is to give the coins back as a gift. If a shul pays Kohanim a fixed fee, a man's word alone is not enough (Ashkenazim who paid Kohanim to come on festivals found that some people lied).

The Hazon Ish. In Israel too, the Hazon Ish says there is no real gain today, since terumah is burned. The Rabbi noted the answers that it can still be used as lamp oil or fed to animals, but nobody lies about this today, so we rely on it also in Israel. The main rule stays: where there is real gain, we do not believe him.

Two further results. First, the man binds himself (shavya nafsheih hatikha deisura). If he marries a divorcee or touches a dead body he receives malkut. Second, the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh say he makes the divorcee a halalah. The Rabbi called this a big hiddush: other Rishonim, such as the Ran, hold that his self-binding affects only himself. His practical point for a dayan: a man who is permitted to marry a woman forbidden to Kohanim, because his Kehunah is in doubt, must himself accept that he is no longer a Kohen. "You can't have it both ways." He cannot be called up as a Kohen and marry her. A brother is not affected, since each person is judged alone.

Part three: a casual statement. If he mentioned it incidentally, and told a story (his father took him out of school, bathed him in the mikveh, and he ate terumah that evening), he is believed. The details matter because a Kohen's slave also eats terumah, but slaves did not go to school. So the story proves he was a Kohen who was allowed to eat.

Seifim 2-4: witnesses and documents

  • Seif 2: One witness is enough for a Kohen hazakah, even his own father, since the father could simply have given him terumah (migu). A document naming him a Kohen and signed by witnesses works, and some say even his own signature. The leniency works only one way: a man held to be a Kohen cannot remove the status by his own word or one witness.
  • Seif 3: The Rashbas lived at the time of the Spanish Inquisition. If a witness says a man was a Kohen before it, we do not worry that his father married a non-Jewish woman under pressure. The Rabbi noted that this extra strictness appears in Siman 3 and not in Siman 2, because of the money involved with Kehunah.
  • Seif 4: Two men may each testify that the other is a Kohen, and we do not suspect collusion. An adult is believed when he remembers seeing someone bathed in the mikveh as a child to eat terumah, a hiddush, since we do not normally rely on a childhood memory. The Rabbi said he did not fully understand the Bah's long discussion here and would not go into it.

Seifim 5-7: a witness about the father

The Rambam says the single witness must testify about the man himself. "I know his father was a Kohen" is not enough, because the father may have married a woman forbidden to him and produced a halal.

The contradiction with Seif 3. The Rashbas did not worry that the father married a non-Jewess. The Helkat Mehokek and the Bah answer that the Rashbas dealt only with calling a man up to the Torah. The Rabbi called this a weak answer, since the problem was never the aliyah itself. Perhaps the Rashbas follows other Rishonim who worry only where there is a rumor (kol). He asked the class to remember the tension, since it returns in Siman 4.

A rumor against a Kohen's father. If a man's father is known as a Kohen and people say the father married a divorcee, the rumor is enough to raise a doubt. Two witnesses against the rumor settle it. With one witness for and one against, the father's hazakah wins over the rumor. A student asked why the rumor cannot return once the witnesses cancel out. The Rabbi: a hazakah together with witnesses is strong enough to defeat both witnesses and rumor, but a hazakah alone can be weakened by a rumor. He said the commentary he checked did not fully resolve it.

Seifim 8-9: mixed-up births and Kohanim

Seif 8 (Gemara in Yevamot). A divorcee does not wait the 92 days and gives birth at nine months. The child may be from the first husband (born at seven months) or the second. If one was a Kohen and the other an Israelite, the child is a doubt (safek). He takes every stringency: he marries only women a Kohen may marry, does not become impure for the dead, and does not serve. But he gets no malkut, since it is only a doubt, so there is no shavya nafsheih as in Seif 1.

Seif 9: two Kohanim. If two Kohanim's babies were mixed up, or the woman married two Kohanim within three months, the child is certainly a Kohen. He must treat both men as his father and mourn for both, but cannot become impure for either, since one is not his father. Even if both die together, he does not.

If it was not marriage. If the child came from zenut (relations outside marriage), the Gemara says the "mixed up" Kohen loses his privileges: no terumah, no service. The Gemara first quotes "zero meyuhas aharav", which suggests he must have clear lineage, then reconciles it with "lezarakha aharekha". It concludes this is a rabbinic rule, and applies only to zenut, because zenut is by nature secret and unclear. The Rema applies this where a woman had relations with a Kohen within three months and then married another.

The Helkat Mehokek's question. When a married woman has an affair, the child is fully valid, since we say most relations are with the husband (rov biot aharei baal). Why not say the same here? The Rabbi said the time frames differ, since after divorce or death she is no longer with a husband. He left the point for Siman 4.

The Rema's last point. If a single woman and a Kohen agree the child is his, the child is a Kohen, and we do not worry about others.

Question from the class

How does DNA testing affect these laws? The Rabbi said we do rely on DNA for questions like these, including who is a Kohen, as far as he remembered the poskim. But we never bring DNA into mamzerut. If we did, much of Siman 4 would collapse, since many of its laws rest on rules DNA would contradict. It would need to be clear DNA. He was not sure what happens if someone already tested for other reasons, but thought it would be ignored for mamzerut.

Practical summary

  • A man who says he is a Kohen is not believed, because of financial gain. In practice we rely on him for an aliyah and Birkat Kohanim, outside Israel and (following the Hazon Ish) in Israel too.
  • Where there is a real payment, such as a shul paying a Kohen, his word alone is not enough.
  • A man who wants to marry a woman a Kohen may not marry must accept that he is no longer treated as a Kohen.
  • One witness, the man's father, or a signed document can establish a Kohen hazakah. A witness who speaks only about his father is not enough.
  • A child of a mixed-up birth in marriages treats both men as fathers. If it came from zenut, he loses the privileges of Kehunah.
  • DNA is accepted for such questions, but not for mamzerut.
