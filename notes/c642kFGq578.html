Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 315, seifim 3-5 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, late 2025

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Where we are

The class continued Siman 315, making an ohel (tent) on Shabbat, with the aim of finishing the topic so that a new one could begin the following week. The Rabbi first recalled the principles from last time:

  • Making an ohel keva, a permanent tent, is forbidden from the Torah, and so is adding to one.
  • An ohel arai, a temporary tent, is forbidden by the Hakhamim, but adding to it is permitted.
  • A partition that permits something (such as a sukkah wall) is forbidden; one that does not (such as a parokhet) is permitted.
  • So the hood of a buggy may be opened and closed only if a tefah was already open from before Shabbat.

Seif 3: beds, tables and how many walls

When assembling a bed, one should not first set up the legs and then put the bed board on top. Rather, hold the board in the air, put the feet under it, and only then lower the board onto them. This change from the normal way avoids making even a temporary ohel. That is the Gemara. Tosafot, the Rosh and the Rishonim qualified it: it applies only when the legs are boards joined like walls. Plain legs, as on a table, are permitted in every way, which is why people set a table top on its legs all the time. The Shulhan Arukh rules like Tosafot.

There is a discussion in the poskim about how many walls are needed. The Rabbi gave a simple way to remember it:

  • Something made for protection (shelter from sun or rain): even with no walls at all it is an ohel. This is why one may not put up a huppah on poles on Shabbat.
  • Something like a bed, not made to shelter what is underneath but whose space is often used (shoes and things stored under it): it is a problem only with at least two walls, and then it must be assembled the other way round.
  • Something like a table, whose space underneath is not really used: it needs four walls to be an issue, which is rare.

"You see how far the Hakhamim extended this": even a bed or a table can raise a question of ohel when done temporarily.

Seif 4: spreading a sheet

A bed strung with ropes: if there are three tefahim between the ropes, one may not spread a sheet on it, since that makes an ohel over the space underneath, and one may not take the sheet off, since that takes the ohel down. Based on the previous seif, this applies only to a bed whose sides come down like walls. If the gaps are less than three tefahim, there is no problem either way, because of lavud: the gap is treated as closed. And if a mat or sheet was already spread a tefah before Shabbat, it may be spread out completely on Shabbat. That is the principle of adding to an ohel arai.

Seif 5: folding chairs, and the umbrella question

"This is a very important seif." A chair made of parts, which opens with its seat and legs together when you want to sit and folds up when you finish, may be opened on Shabbat. This is the Gemara's kisei traskal. Even if it has sides that come down like walls, it is permitted, because the ohel is already made: you are not setting up poles and putting a board on them; it opens and closes by itself. So all folding chairs and folding tables may be opened and closed on Shabbat, even where seifim 3 and 4 would otherwise have been a problem.

The Mishnah Berurah, quoting the Magen Avraham, adds that a folding huppah that simply opens and closes may also be opened and closed. The Rabbi stressed why this matters: a huppah covers the people under it, so it seems that even an ohel made for protection is permitted if it is a folding one. On that basis, he said, if we stopped here, an umbrella should be permitted too: the ohel is already there, and you press a button and it opens itself.

Yet the poskim of the last few hundred years forbid it. The main reasons:

  1. The Noda BiYehudah (Rav Yehezkel Landau of Prague), who set the tone of the whole discussion, says the leniency of kisei traskal applies only to something not made for protection, like a bed or chair. An umbrella exists to protect, so it is forbidden. As for the huppah, he answers that it is not made for protection at all (we put one up even under a ceiling); it is made for honour and to mark the ceremony. Even if the umbrella was opened before Shabbat, he forbids lifting it up, because people will not know it was opened before Shabbat.
  2. The Biur Halacha brings poskim who forbid it, and explains that it is not like kisei traskal because the umbrellas of the time had to be fastened or tied after opening, which is an extra action. He adds that there is also an issue of carrying.
  3. The Hazon Ish disagrees with the Noda BiYehudah about the huppah, so he gives a new reason: tikkun mana, "fixing an article", a rabbinic form of Makeh BePatish. The tents permitted to be opened and closed are used in their place. An umbrella is a new kind of ohel that can be taken anywhere, and that is significant. Even if it was opened before Shabbat, he invokes marit ayin or uvdin dehol (a weekday activity).

The Rabbi's analysis: the Noda BiYehudah's distinction is his own hiddush, not made by the Gemara or the Rishonim, and many do not accept it. The tying reason was historically real, but does not apply to umbrellas today. Carrying is a new gezerah, and does not explain a place with an eruv. Rav Ovadia Yosef brings the Noda BiYehudah and rules, on the strength of the other reasons and marit ayin, that one should forbid it; the Rabbi said he finds that hard to square with Rav Ovadia's own principle elsewhere that we do not create new marit ayin after the close of the Talmud.

His conclusion was careful: "I'm not suggesting it's mutar. I'm still saying that it's asur." But he finds each reason weak, especially where the umbrella was opened before Shabbat. In his view the prohibition began because early umbrellas needed human input, and once forbidden, it stayed forbidden.

A parallel: electricity, and questions from the class

He compared this to electricity on Shabbat. At first most poskim thought fire was involved and forbade it. When it became clear there is no burning, new reasons were offered. Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach has a teshuvah that goes through every reason and concludes, in the Rabbi's words, that he does not know the reason why electricity is forbidden, but it is forbidden; perhaps because filament bulbs were common. Rav Asher Weiss builds on the Yerushalmi that Rabbi Yohanan spent two and a half years categorising the melakhot, and anything felt to be a melakhah without an av was placed under Makeh BePatish. He argues that switching on electricity is so significant an act that it belongs there. The Rabbi called it a huge hiddush, but a very interesting idea. Could the same be said of an umbrella? "I don't think there's something significant in opening an umbrella."

Questions:

  • A parasol in the garden. Far easier to be lenient, since the Hazon Ish's reason (a portable tent) falls away; the Noda BiYehudah would still object, since it is made for protection. The Rabbi said he thinks there are grounds to permit it, and the same for the umbrella clipped onto a buggy. No tefah needs to be left open beforehand, because a kisei traskal opens by itself.
  • A manual awning. This is not a kisei traskal. Unrolling it is actively spreading out a new ohel, so a tefah must be left open before Shabbat, as with seif 2. An electric awning may not be operated.
  • The buggy hood. Same as the awning: you are pulling the ohel open, so the tefah rule from last week applies. Asked whether a hood that sprang open at the press of a button would be different, he said perhaps it would.
  • Folding chair versus awning. Opening a chair is one action and the ohel comes with it; pulling out an awning or hood is making the ohel. It was put to him that one wants a chair but wants shade from a parasol; he answered that this is the Noda BiYehudah's distinction, which nobody made before him.
  • Asking a non-Jew. Rav Ovadia Yosef permits a non-Jew to open and carry the umbrella for you, and this is not marit ayin since everyone can see he is carrying it. So if it is pouring and you cannot otherwise get to synagogue, that is an option.
  • Is uvdin dehol codified? No. It is mentioned a few times in the Gemara and later extended; it is a very subjective concept.

On practice he was clear: where there is no eruv, carrying an umbrella is forbidden (at least rabbinically, if our streets are a karmelit), and if you are able to explain this to someone, you should. In a place with an eruv, as in many Jewish areas today, it is still forbidden, "we mustn't do it", but in his own opinion one does not need to protest strongly against someone who comes to synagogue with one.

Practical summary

  • A bed with walled sides is assembled board first, legs after; plain legs and tables are fine.
  • Folding chairs, folding tables and anything that opens by itself into an ohel may be opened and closed on Shabbat.
  • Do not open or carry an umbrella on Shabbat; the poskim forbid it even with an eruv. A non-Jew may carry one for you.
  • A garden parasol or a clip-on buggy umbrella that opens by itself: the Rabbi sees grounds to permit.
  • A manual awning or buggy hood: leave a tefah open before Shabbat, then it may be adjusted.
