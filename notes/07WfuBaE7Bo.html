The Sanhedrin in the Three Weeks

This class was given in the Three Weeks, with Rosh Hodesh Av that Friday and Tisha BeAv a week and a half away. A central part of our mourning is the Beit HaMikdash. But the Rabbi pointed to something else we lost: the Sanhedrin, "a focal point of our collective identity as a nation". The class asked what the Sanhedrin was, how it was lost, and whether it can ever be re-established.

Seventy elders and the chain of semikhah

The Sanhedrin begins in Parashat Behaalotekha. Moshe tells Hashem he cannot carry the people alone, and Hashem says: "Gather for Me seventy men of the elders of Israel... and they will bear the burden of the people with you" (Bamidbar 11:16-17). "Elders" means the wise of the nation, not the old. Seventy with Moshe makes seventy-one, an odd number, so the court can never be split.

Its real name is the Beit Din HaGadol, the great court. "Sanhedrin" is a Greek word, meaning a gathering. The Bartenura, on the Mishnah in Sotah, plays on the word: they are called Sanhedrin because they hate showing favour in judgment to the powerful or the wealthy.

The Rambam rules that every Beit Din, even a court of three, needs at least one judge ordained by someone ordained, in an unbroken chain back to Moshe (Hilchot Sanhedrin 4:1-2). Moshe laid his hands on Yehoshua, and the elders ordained the next generation. That is the origin of the word semikhah, to rest one's hands, although the hands were not actually required.

The Zugot of Pirkei Avot were the leaders of this court: in each pair, the first was the Nasi and the second the Av Beit Din. The Anshei Keneset HaGedolah, the Men of the Great Assembly, were a kind of "super Sanhedrin" of 120, convened after the Babylonian exile to bring every faction together. Their enactments, including our prayers, are still with us.

A slow decline

The Mishnah in Hagigah 16a records a dispute about leaning on a korban on Yom Tov, and Rashi calls it "the first mahloket among the Hakhamim of Israel". There had always been arguments, the Rabbi explained, but until then they could all be settled, if necessary by the Beit Din HaGadol, like a Supreme Court. In the Second Temple period people stopped listening. "The Sanhedrin governs with the consent of the people," he said.

Forty years before the destruction, the Sanhedrin left the Lishkat HaGazit in the Temple and sat in the shops, and could no longer judge capital cases (Shabbat 15a). Rosh Hashanah 31a-b lists its exiles: to Yerushalayim, Yavneh, Usha, Shefaram, Beit Shearim, Sippori and finally Tiberias. Its last remnants lasted until about the fourth century. The Gemara says it will one day be restored, starting from Tiberias, reading the verse "Hitnaari meafar kumi", shake off the dust and arise.

This is why the Amoraim in Bavel are called Rav, not Rabbi like the Tannaim: full semikhah could only be given in the Land of Israel. Today's rabbis and dayanim do not have it either. The Rema writes that semikhah nowadays only tells people that this person may teach, with his teacher's permission (Yoreh Deah 242:14). So no court today can try capital cases.

The Rambam: renewing semikhah

How could the Sanhedrin ever return, if the chain is broken? One classic answer is to wait for Mashiah. The Rambam did not accept that. In his Commentary on the Mishnah he writes that if all the sages agreed to place one person at their head, in the Land of Israel, he would be ordained and could ordain others. Otherwise the Sanhedrin could never return, yet we have been promised that it will. It is the sages of the Land of Israel who count, "and we pay no attention to those outside the Land" (on Bekhorot 4:3). Once there is one samukh, the chain can begin again.

In Sefer HaMisvot (positive commandment 176) he counts appointing judges as a misvah for all generations. And in a later edition of his commentary, the Rabbi noted, he writes that the Sanhedrin will return before Mashiah, since Mashiah himself must be ordained.

The key text is Hilchot Sanhedrin 4:11. The Rambam writes "it seems to me", a phrase he uses when saying something not stated in the Talmud: if all the sages in the Land of Israel agreed to appoint and ordain judges, they would be ordained. Why then did the sages grieve over the loss of semikhah? Because Israel was scattered and could not all agree. Then he ends with three words: "vehadavar sarikh hekhrea", the matter needs a decision. As far as the Rabbi knows, they appear nowhere else in the Mishneh Torah, and the simple reading is "I am not sure". The Meiri writes the same idea.

Much of Lekha Dodi, he pointed out, is about this reawakening; "Hitnaari meafar kumi" is the same verse. "Without our legislative body we're paralysed," he said, and this too is part of the sadness of Tisha BeAv. Someone asked about the rule that a court cannot undo another's enactment unless it is greater in wisdom and number. That, he answered, applies to overturning existing takanot; a Sanhedrin could also address new questions and make new takanot.

The great semikhah controversy

The Rambam's idea drew little attention until the 16th century. After the expulsions from Spain and Portugal, many Jews reached the Ottoman Empire, and after 1517 large communities grew up in Safed and Tiberias. Many had lived for years as Christians and wanted complete teshuvah for sins punishable by karet. For that they felt they needed malkot, lashes, which only an ordained court can give.

In 1538 Rav Yaakov Beirav, himself a refugee from Spain, gathered twenty-five rabbis in Safed, and they conferred semikhah on him. He then ordained four others, among them Rav Yoseph Karo, author of the Shulhan Arukh, and the Mabit, Rav Moshe di Trani. Rav Yoseph Karo later ordained Rav Moshe Alshikh, who ordained Rav Hayim Vital.

Rav Yaakov Beirav offered semikhah to Rav Levi ibn Habib, the Ralbah, the leading rabbi of the smaller Jerusalem community. The Ralbah refused: the rabbis of Jerusalem had not been consulted, so even on the Rambam's terms there was no consensus. The question went to the Radbaz, Rav David ibn Zimra in Egypt, who rejected the semikhah for several reasons:

- The Rambam requires all the sages of the Land, and Jerusalem was left out.

- The Rambam himself was unsure. The Safed side read "the matter needs a decision" as referring to something narrower; the Radbaz read it as covering the whole idea.

- The sages must agree together in one gathering.

- Real semikhah requires knowing the whole Torah, and he doubted anyone in his generation did.

- He himself held that Mashiah will be ordained miraculously and will then ordain others.

- The ten lost tribes may still have an unbroken chain.

The project faded. Rav Yaakov Beirav had to leave the Land, Ottoman persecution hampered it, and within a few generations it was forgotten. Even the Shulhan Arukh writes that "nowadays there are no ordained judges" (Even HaEzer 177:2), although Rav Yoseph Karo had received this semikhah.

Later attempts and our own time

In the 19th century Rav Yisrael of Shklov, a student of the Vilna Gaon and author of Peat HaShulhan, tried to renew semikhah and even sent an emissary to look for the ten tribes. Rav Kook wrote that a central purpose of returning to the Land was to restore the Sanhedrin; some twenty years later he wrote that our generation was not yet worthy. Around 2005 a group of rabbis gathered in Tiberias to try again, but it never really gained traction.

Until there is a Sanhedrin, the Rabbi said, we live with different minhagim, nusahim and rulings, Ashkenazim one way and Sepharadim another. In theory the Rambam's way could still work, but every community would have to agree on one person, and he does not see that happening now.

Summary

  • The Beit Din HaGadol began with Moshe and the seventy elders, and rested on an unbroken chain of semikhah and the consent of the people.
  • It declined through the Second Temple period, was exiled step by step to Tiberias, and ended by about the fourth century; no rabbi today has the original semikhah.
  • The Rambam held that if all the sages of the Land of Israel agreed on one person, semikhah could be renewed, though he ended "the matter needs a decision".
  • In 1538 Rav Yaakov Beirav renewed semikhah in Safed; the Ralbah and the Radbaz rejected it, and it faded, as did later attempts.
  • In the Three Weeks we mourn the loss of the Sanhedrin as well as the Beit HaMikdash, and pray that we all come together again.
