These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Being stricter during the Ten Days (Siman 603)

With only one class before Yom Kippur and Sukkot the week after, the Rabbi covered a lot of ground quickly.

Siman 603 has one paragraph: even someone who is not careful during the year about pat palter, kosher bread baked by a non-Jewish bakery, should be careful during the Ten Days of Teshuvah. Buying such bread during the year is a real leniency and "absolutely fine": the supermarket brands are cheaper, and the restriction was never total. Yet the halacha asks for more during these days, even from someone who knows he will go back to it after Yom Kippur.

Why? Because during these days "we're laying down our spiritual genes" for the year ahead.

Someone once asked him whether this applies to milk: should someone who relies on Rav Moshe Feinstein's ruling about ordinary supermarket milk drink only halav Yisrael during the Ten Days? The Rabbi thought it is different. Bread from a non-Jewish baker is knowingly relying on a leniency. Milk in countries with strict government regulation, such as the UK, the EU and America, is considered as if a Jew had watched the milking, so it is not a leniency in the same sense. Anyone may of course take on extra stringencies.

Make time to reflect

The Rema says a person should search his deeds during these days, and doubtful sins need more teshuvah than certain ones, just as the asham taluy for a doubtful sin was more costly than a regular sin offering. The Rabbi quoted Rabbi Sacks on the difference between the urgent and the important: we spend our lives on urgent things, and the important ones (family, thanking Hashem, learning, reflecting on life) get forgotten. Shabbat makes time for the important things each week. The Ten Days do the same for the year. "We're asking Hashem for a whole year of life ahead. The question is, what did you do with last year?" Set aside ten minutes or half an hour each day to think about the past year and the coming one.

Eating on Erev Yom Kippur (Siman 604)

It is a misvah to eat a great deal on Erev Yom Kippur. The Torah says "you shall afflict yourselves on the ninth of the month in the evening", and the Gemara teaches: "Whoever eats and drinks on the ninth is considered as if he fasted on the ninth and the tenth."

  • The misvah is by day, not the night before, and includes drinking.
  • It is not a Yom Tov meal requiring meat and wine. The Rabbi advised against heavy meat before the fast; chicken or fish is better. The Shulhan Arukh also says not to come into Yom Kippur overfull and complacent.
  • The Gemara says the study hall closed on Erev Yom Kippur and Erev Pesah. One may learn a little, but it is not the day for classes and havrutot.
  • Someone who had a bad dream may not fast on Erev Yom Kippur.
  • Tahanun is not said on Erev Yom Kippur, since it has something of a Yom Tov about it. That year, with Yom Kippur on Tuesday night, Tahanun was said on Monday afternoon but not on Tuesday. The pre-dawn Selihot, even if they run past daybreak, are said in full, since they belong to the night before.

Kaparot (Siman 605)

The Shulhan Arukh writes plainly that the custom of slaughtering a chicken for each male on Erev Yom Kippur and saying verses over it "should be stopped". The earliest source is in the Geonim. The Ramban and the Rashba, both from Catalonia and both inclined to Kabbalah, said it was forbidden as darkhei haEmori, a pagan practice.

The Rema replies that the Geonim and many later authorities wrote of it, all Ashkenazim do it, and an old custom should not be changed. Many Sefaradim do it too: Iraqis, Syrians, Jerusalemites, Tunisians and Turkish communities. It spread through the Arizal, whose student Rav Hayim Vital recommended it. "If anything the Shulhan Arukh says not to do and we do it, 99.9% of the time it's from the Arizal."

How to do it properly

If we continue our fathers' custom, it must be done in a way that is clearly not pagan. "There's no hocus pocus, there's no magic, there's no easy way out in Torah." The Rema explains that a person should think that what is done to the bird should really have been done to him, and that through teshuvah Hashem removes the decree from him. This is like the Ramban's explanation of sacrifices, and like the eglah arufah, which shocks people into responsibility. The prophets constantly said sacrifices mean nothing without what comes with them. Done as a symbol to awaken teshuvah, the Rabbi did not think the Shulhan Arukh would object. Someone asked whether there are better ways to awaken teshuvah. "If you're able to do it without, then don't do it," the Rabbi agreed, adding that a large part of it is the charity.

The details

  • A cockerel for a male, a hen for a female, and for a pregnant woman two hens and a cockerel (unless the baby's sex is known). White birds are preferred: "your sins shall become white".
  • The Mishnah Berurah says it is better to give the poor its value than the chicken itself, since a poor person may be embarrassed to be handed a bird "with your sins on it". If he prefers food, give the food. Either way, be sensitive.
  • If you eat the chicken yourself and give its value away, there is nothing wrong with eating it. Feeling uneasy about it "is buying into the whole problem".
  • From this grew today's custom of using money, waved in the same way. The Rabbi suggested around eight or nine pounds a person that year, given inflation.
  • If funds are limited, give at least for the adults, those over bar or bat misvah. If you are particular to give exactly ten percent as maaser, do not use maaser money for this.

Asking forgiveness (Siman 606)

Yom Kippur does not atone for sins against other people until you have appeased them, even for words alone. If he refuses, go a second and third time, each time with three people. After three times you need not continue. The Rambam's wording suggests four approaches: once alone, then three times with others. Asking in front of others makes it public, which matters especially when you embarrassed someone publicly.

Do not leave it to Erev Yom Kippur

The general request for forgiveness on Erev Yom Kippur is for the small, forgotten hurts between people who see each other regularly, like the general annulment of vows. If you know you hurt someone and he bears a grudge, do not wait. Those who wait often "are not doing it because they really care about the other person's feelings", and their request comes as an instruction: "Forgive me." Each time you return, try a new way to reconcile, and be specific about what you did.

Should you tell someone what you said about him? The Hafes Hayim holds that you must say what you did. Rav Yehudah Zev Segal of Manchester held that if the person does not know, telling him may make it worse. The Rabbi suspected there is much truth in that, and that one should be tactful: "I may have said something about you that I regret; please accept my apology."

What Yom Kippur does and does not do

  • Missed positive misvot do not need Yom Kippur; you make them up where possible.
  • Sins carrying karet or the death penalty need teshuvah, Yom Kippur and suffering. Hillul Hashem needs even more.
  • Yom Kippur atones fully for ordinary negative prohibitions, such as non-kosher food or lashon hara towards Hashem.
  • Every sin against a person is also a sin against Hashem. In the Rabbi's view, the Godly side is only forgiven once the human side has been addressed.

The one who is asked

Do not be cruel, and do not use the moment for revenge. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul writes that withholding forgiveness "to teach him a lesson" is only for a teacher with his student or a parent with a child: "The only people we teach lessons to is ourselves." The exception: someone who spread a false and damaging rumour about you need not be forgiven, since the damage cannot be undone. The Rabbi warned how quickly things now spread online. He added that exposing real wrongdoing, with evidence, where people are being harmed, is a duty and not slander.

If the person has died

You go to his grave with ten people and ask forgiveness. The Pri Megadim explains the ten: one says "I have sinned against the God of Israel", which suits a minyan. The general request said at a parent's grave covers ordinary friction; something specific that a parent died upset about needs to be asked for properly.

He closed: do not speak badly of the dead. Immersing in a mikveh on Erev Yom Kippur is a custom, so no berakhah is said, and the Mishnah Berurah discusses a shower for one who cannot go.

Practical summary

  • During the Ten Days avoid bread from non-Jewish bakeries even if you buy it during the year; in the Rabbi's view this does not extend to ordinary supermarket milk.
  • Eat and drink well on Erev Yom Kippur by day, but not heavy meat and not to excess before the fast.
  • Kaparot, if done, must be a symbol to awaken teshuvah, never a magical act. Giving the value to the poor is better than giving the bird.
  • Ask forgiveness of those you know you hurt well before Yom Kippur, specifically and tactfully, up to three times with three people.
  • Do not withhold forgiveness to teach a lesson, except a parent or teacher with a child or student; there is no duty to forgive someone who spread a false rumour about you.
