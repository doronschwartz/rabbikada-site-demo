Topic: Voice-activated devices such as Alexa and Siri on Shabbat: leaving them on, speaking to them, and programming them in advance Source: Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach, Minhat Shlomo; the Hazon Ish; Yerushalmi Shabbat 7; Bava Mesia; Daat Zekenim on the man Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada for Torah Way's "Start Your Day with Torah", North West London, Thursday 12 September 2019 (12 Elul)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A new question

Technology moves faster than we can imagine, and halacha has to keep up. Voice assistants like Alexa and Siri listen constantly for a "wake word" and then answer. Can you leave one on over Shabbat? Can you ask it, on a winter Shabbat morning, whether you need a coat?

Why is electricity forbidden on Shabbat?

The Rabbi first summarised the views, following Rav Shlomo Zalman Auerbach's Minhat Shlomo. He was speaking only of electricity without heat, such as LEDs and phones. An incandescent bulb is fire, and is forbidden by Torah law according to most.

  • Molid (creating something new). The Beit Yishak compares closing a circuit to creating a new scent in a garment, a rabbinic form of makeh bepatish. Rav Shlomo Zalman was not convinced: molid is limited to specific cases, and flicking a switch involves no effort.
  • Boneh (building). The Hazon Ish holds that completing a circuit is building. Many poskim disagree. Rav Shlomo Zalman's example: closing your front door is not building, because a door is made to open and close; so too a circuit.
  • Rav Shlomo Zalman himself admits he cannot pin down the exact prohibition, but the consensus of the poskim since the teshuvot appeared is that it is forbidden, and using it would lead people to use incandescent bulbs.

Rav Asher Weiss adds a powerful idea from the Yerushalmi. Rabbi Yohanan and Resh Lakish spent three and a half years on the laws of Shabbat. Wherever something felt like a melakhah but they could find no source, they classed it under makeh bepatish. If so, says Rav Asher Weiss, what greater melakhah is there today than electricity, which runs our whole lives? He therefore treats it as Torah law, though in certain cases he relies on the view that it is rabbinic. Rav Shlomo Zalman and Rav Ovadia Yoseph hold it is rabbinic.

Leaving the device on

These devices record all the time, waiting for the wake word, but nothing is kept. Is it a problem to leave one on while you talk normally at home?

If electricity is rabbinic, the Shulhan Arukh permits a pesik reisha (an inevitable side effect) of a rabbinic prohibition that the person has no interest in. You are speaking, the device inevitably picks up the sound, and you do not want it to. And even if it were Torah law, something so insignificant may not count as a melakhah at all, like the few drops of water left in a cup before pouring in hot soup.

So, seemingly, one may leave it on in the background: certainly for Sefaradim, and perhaps for Ashkenazim too.

What if your wife is called Alexa? Better to switch the device off before Shabbat. If you did not, it seems still permitted: calling your wife is an act of speaking to her, and the device coming on is even further removed than a pesik reisha, like a light that comes on as you walk past.

Talking to it directly

The real question is whether speech counts as an action. Two sources suggest it does:

  • Bava Mesia: if you keep diverting an ox from eating while it threshes, by voice and not with a muzzle, you have still transgressed "do not muzzle". Speech has the status of action.
  • Daat Zekenim on the man: if the man tasted of whatever one thought of, why did it have to be prepared before Shabbat? Because you had to say what you wanted, and saying it was like cooking.

Two sources point the other way. One may whisper an incantation to stop a dangerous creature on Shabbat without it counting as trapping, and the letters on the Kohen Gadol's breastplate lit up in answer to speech without it counting as writing. But the Mishnah Berurah explains that trapping by incantation is not the normal way of trapping, just as glowing letters are not the normal way of writing. Speaking to Alexa or Siri is exactly the normal way of using them.

So in practice it would seem to be a problem: speaking to the device has the status of switching it on yourself. It may also be ordinary weekday talk, which is out of place on Shabbat.

Programming it before Shabbat

Finally, may one set the device before Shabbat to give information on Shabbat? For Ashkenazim there is a concern of avsha milta, making a noise that draws attention. But the Shulhan Arukh holds there is no such problem, just as Sefaradim may set a washing machine before Shabbat to run on Shabbat. So, strictly speaking, for Sefaradim it would be fine.

Practical summary

  • Electricity without heat is forbidden on Shabbat; most hold rabbinically, and Rav Asher Weiss as Torah law.
  • Leaving a voice assistant on in the background seems permitted, certainly for Sefaradim and perhaps for Ashkenazim.
  • Speaking to it directly seems forbidden, because speech that operates a device in its normal way counts as an action.
  • Setting it before Shabbat to give information on Shabbat is, strictly speaking, fine for Sefaradim.
