The question of the evening

Is there a contradiction between being well versed in philosophy and science and being a pious, believing Jew? Does immersion in general culture take away from dedication to the Torah, or does it increase it? That question, the Rabbi said, runs through every line of the teshuvah studied that evening: the Meiri's letter of late 1305. It is a teshuvah that "sets you in the context of ancient Sefarad," of how our Sefaradi Hakhamim approached the world, and he believes we should be proud of that tradition.

The Meiri, author of the famous Beit HaBehirah, was born in 1249. His name was Menahem ben Shlomo; he was called HaMeiri after an ancestor named Meir. To understand his letter, the Rabbi said, you must understand its whole background.

From Andalusia to Languedoc

As early as the tenth century, Jews in Islamic lands met Greek philosophy as developed by the Arabs. Rav Saadiah Gaon drew on the Kalam in Emunot VeDeot; Ibn Ezra, Avraham ibn Daud and later the Rambam followed. Not everyone took that path: Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi in the Kuzari and Rabbenu Bahya in Hovot HaLevavot had a different vision.

In 1147, persecution drove the Jews out of Andalusia. Many settled in Languedoc, in what is now southern France: Montpellier, Narbonne, Béziers, Perpignan. The Hakhamim there welcomed them and commissioned translations, first of the Jewish classics and then of mathematics, astronomy, medicine and physics. Five generations of the ibn Tibbon family worked on this; Shmuel ibn Tibbon translated the Moreh Nevuchim in 1204. By 1300 Languedoc was a hub of philosophical and scientific inquiry, while nearby Catalonia was turning to Kabbalah. That contrast, he said, is the key to everything that follows.

The first controversy, around 1232

After the Moreh spread, Rabbi Shlomo of Montpellier and Rabbenu Yonah of Gerona pressed for a complete ban on the Rambam's works. Their three charges were that the philosophers deny miracles, allegorise the Torah away from its plain meaning, and are lax in misvot, with tefillin mentioned again and again. The Radak defended the Rambam. It ended badly, with the burning of the Rambam's books, though some of those involved later apologised.

The second controversy, 1303 to 1305

Seventy years later nobody spoke of banning philosophy altogether. The question was who may study it, especially the young, and the target was extreme allegory, such as treating Avraham and Sarah as symbols of matter and form.

Abba Mari of Lunel turned to the Rashba in Barcelona. The Rabbi stressed how surprising this alliance was. Abba Mari was a moderate Maimonidean who saw the Moreh as the authoritative statement of the Torah's inner meaning, and held, like the Rambam, that Maaseh Bereshit and Maaseh Merkavah are science and theology. For the Rashba, Maaseh Merkavah is Kabbalah. When Abba Mari complained that preachers were revealing the secrets of the Torah in public, the Rashba's reply was in effect: philosophy is not the secrets of the Torah at all.

After a first letter in 1303 was ignored, in July 1305 the Rashba and thirty-three Hakhamim of Barcelona banned the study of Greek philosophy and science before the age of 25. Montpellier answered with a counter-ban, put Abba Mari himself in herem, and he responded with a counter-counter-ban. At this point the Meiri, the leading halachic authority of Languedoc, wrote.

Part one of the letter: you went to the wrong address

The Meiri opens with lavish praise. When you want to criticise someone, the Rabbi noted, you start by praising him. He fully agrees that preachers who allegorise the Torah before the uninitiated must be stopped.

Then he turns. Why did you take this outside our region to the Rashba? He honours the Rashba as "a father for us all," but the Hakhamim of Catalonia "have chosen to go after the Kabbalah," and for them most philosophy is demonic. "Are there no doctors here?" A whole land renowned for its learning has been given a bad name. And you told him about the minor issue, young people studying science, and missed the real problem, the preachers.

The Meiri then praises philosophy itself. Where something seems to contradict the Torah, he attributes it to his own lack of understanding and picks "the rose from the thorns." It is an honour for a nation to have philosophers in it; without them the nations will call us foolish. If one or two people stray, deal with them, but if that is a reason to close philosophy, we should also close Kabbalah, since of the four who entered the Pardes only one left in peace. He names great Talmudists of the region who mastered every discipline, and he points out that parts of the Gemara, like the geometry of Masekhet Sukkah, and the Rambam's laws of sanctifying the new moon cannot be understood without science.

Part two: why age 25 cannot work

"This may be correct according to his place," the Meiri writes of the Rashba's ban, "but with all honour to him, we cannot accept it." His reasons: - People are different, and one age limit cannot fit everyone. - By 25 a man has a wife and a living to earn, and will never find the time. - A teacher available at 20 may be gone at 25. - How will anyone understand the 25 introductions to the second part of the Moreh, which are built on physics and philosophy? - There is no point banning what people will not keep. It only makes a mockery of the Hakhamim, as with the first ban: everyone now learns the Rambam.

Finally the Meiri tells Abba Mari he should never have issued his counter-ban. When one camp bans another and the other bans back, the only result for ordinary people is mockery of the Torah.

Lessons for today

The Rabbi drew three lessons: - Know the context. A rabbi from outside a community, foreign to its culture, should be careful before intervening in its disputes. "Hakhamim, be careful with your words." - Torah and wisdom together. For both the Meiri and Abba Mari, philosophy and science were part of Torah; they disagreed only about age. - A forgotten camp. Today a rabbi who is also a scientist seems strange. For most of the Hakhamim of Andalusia and Languedoc, it was simply part of a life committed to Torah.

The story ended in 1306, when Philip IV expelled the Jews from France and both camps "lived to fight another day."

Questions from the class

Someone asked why new ideas spread so slowly today, despite instant information. People do not like change, the Rabbi answered, and the further we move from the Talmud the more fossilised things become; we also live in echo chambers. Another asked about Elisha ben Avuyah. The Meiri, he said, uses exactly that case: no one ever suggested banning Kabbalah because one person went astray. A third asked whether the Mishneh Torah was also controversial. In the first controversy, yes, and in his lifetime; but the Rambam won out, and "today there isn't a yeshiva in the world which won't study the Rambam."

Summary

  • The 1305 dispute was not about banning philosophy outright but about who may study it and at what age.
  • The Meiri objected to bringing an outside authority from a different school into Languedoc's affairs.
  • He held philosophy and science to be an honour to the nation and essential to understanding parts of the Gemara and the Moreh.
  • Bans that people will not keep, and bans answered by counter-bans, only bring the Torah into disrepute.
