Topic: What makes the Pesah night misvah of telling the story unique, and why it has no berakhah

Sources: Torah ("Vehigadta levinkha"); the third paragraph of the Shema; the Gemara; the Rambam; the Haggadah; the Rishonim; the Rashba

Daily Halacha: Pesah, Wednesday 29 March 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • Only two Torah misvot are unique to Pesah night: eating massah and Sippur Yesiat Misrayim, from "Vehigadta levinkha".
  • Remembering the Exodus is a misvah every night, fulfilled in the third paragraph of the Shema. On Pesah night it differs in two ways: we relive it ("in every generation a person must see himself as if he left Egypt"), and we discuss it rather than merely mention it.
  • The night is built to encourage questions. The Gemara, codified by the Rambam, says that even one alone asks Mah Nishtanah, really one question in four parts, answered by "we were slaves".
  • Why ask oneself? Egypt was both physical slavery, which we mark with massah, four cups and reclining, and intellectual slavery. When Moshe asked Pharaoh for a three-day break, Pharaoh increased the work so they would have no time to think.
  • A dictatorship curtails free speech above all. Asking questions shows freedom to investigate and analyse, so on Pesah night we relive our mental freedom through question, answer and discussion. The more one does, the greater the misvah.
  • Why no berakhah on this Torah misvah? One answer in the Rishonim: it is covered by Kiddush, which mentions Zekher LiYesiat Misrayim. The Rashba answers that a misvah with no fixed limit, lasting the whole night, has no berakhah.

Sources quoted

  • "Vehigadta levinkha": the misvah of telling the story.
  • The Gemara and the Rambam: even one alone asks Mah Nishtanah.
  • The Rashba: no berakhah on a misvah without a limit.

In one line

Pesah night relives not only physical freedom but intellectual freedom, expressed by asking, answering and discussing the Exodus.
