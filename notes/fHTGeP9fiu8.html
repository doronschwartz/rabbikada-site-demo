Where we are

Emunot VeDeot has ten essays, and before them an Introduction of eight chapters. Chapter 1 simply thanks Hashem for the ability to write the book. Last week the class began Chapter 2, where Rav Saadiah explains why people make mistakes in their search for truth. There are two reasons. Some people do not know what they are looking for: you may be a brilliant detective, but if you have never seen Reuven, you cannot find him. Others know the goal but not the method of getting there. Often it is a mixture of both.

He also described four kinds of believers: those who have found the truth and are confident in it; those who have found it but are unsure; those who are confident but wrong; and those who flip-flop from one belief to the next. That last group is the one he wrote the book for. He saw people "sinking in doubts" and felt he had to help, even though he admits he does not know everything.

The end of Chapter 2: an oath

Rav Saadiah closes the chapter by asking the reader to take an oath, in Hashem's name, about how to read the book.

  • If you find a mistake, tell me. Do not say, "It is his book, it is his problem." Wise people care about wisdom, and those who fight for wisdom share a camaraderie. Foolish people cling to their foolishness; the wise should not.
  • Come without prejudice. Come to the book wanting to clear your doubts, not to defend what you already think. With that attitude, and with Hashem's help, "I am the Lord who teaches you to understand," the book will work.

Rav Saadiah then lists what follows if teacher and students all come this way: those who doubt will be settled, and those who until now believed only because "this is what my father told me" will begin to believe through their own understanding. The Rabbi asked how many of us can say our beliefs are really our own, and not just what we inherited.

He goes on: the righteous will rejoice, you will feel good inside, and you will pray better. There is a sense in which it is better to hold a wrong belief with conviction than to flip-flop endlessly, because living with doubt is very hard. Our Sages say there is no happiness like the removal of doubts.

Two further results caught the Rabbi's attention. You will be more honest in business, and you will be less jealous. What is the connection to belief? His answer: dishonesty and jealousy come from not having a pure sense of Hashem's unity. If you really believe that everything comes from Hashem, you know you cannot gain by cheating someone, and you know that what Hashem gave you is right for you. That is hard to achieve, but it is what we aim for.

The chapter ends with Habakkuk's image: knowledge of Hashem will cover the world as water covers the sea. Rav Saadiah is confident in his book. "If you still have doubts at the end, go and complain to Saadiah."

A question

Someone asked whether anyone ever corrected Rav Saadiah in his lifetime. The Rabbi said he did not know. Rav Saadiah died not long after writing it, and the Rabbi has never seen such a correction recorded. "Let's see if you can find something to send him."

Chapter 3: why did Hashem leave us in doubt?

Chapter 2 explained why people err. Chapter 3 asks the bigger question: why would Hashem allow it? Why not create a world where everyone knows the truth clearly?

The answer is simple, though Rav Saadiah gives it at great length, and it shows his method: we are creatures, and creatures live in processes. Nothing in creation is instant. Farming, building and weaving all take time, and you cannot stop halfway and expect a finished product. Knowledge is the same. Every belief begins in confusion, and the intellect has to work through it until the doubts are cleared.

The Rabbi gave a small picture of this. With 2 plus 2, the answer is instant, because we have been through that process so many times that it is stored away. With 31 times 63 you have to work it out. Our settled conclusions are things we once processed; the open questions are those we have not yet finished with.

Rav Saadiah's picture is ten doubts. You think, and you are left with nine, then eight, then seven. If you stop at five, you are less doubtful than before, but you have not arrived. You must go all the way.

The long example: finding a proof

To show how this works, Rav Saadiah gives an extended example. Imagine you are looking for a spoken statement that will prove something. Look at how much you have to rule out first:

  1. Sounds of lifeless things: stones banging, rain, thunder.
  2. Sounds of living creatures that carry no meaning: the neighing of a horse, a cat purring.
  3. Human noises that are not speech: coughing, breathing, a baby's cry.
  4. Single letters: "A, B, C, D" are articulate, but prove nothing.
  5. Single words: "heaven" or "star" are words, but a word alone proves nothing.
  6. Phrases: "a shining star" is better, but still only describes.
  7. Full statements with context: "The sun has risen. It started to rain."

At this point we have a real statement, but not every statement is true. There are three kinds:

Kind of statement

Meaning

Example

Necessary

It has to be true

Fire is hot

Impossible

It cannot be true

Fire is cold

Possible

It may or may not be true

Reuven is in Germany; the sun has risen

We set aside the first two, which need no investigation. We investigate the third.

The Rabbi's illustration: you and a friend are locked in a windowless cell, arguing about whether the sun has risen. A stranger walks in and says it has. Have you won the argument? Not yet. His claim is only "possible". If he walks in wearing sunglasses, it becomes more likely; but he might be tricking you. Only a clear proof turns the statement from "possible" into "necessary", and then all the earlier doubts are gone.

He pointed out that we do all this in a split second. If you hear a dog barking outside the cell, your mind quietly drops that as irrelevant. If the stranger announces lunch, that is a fine statement, but it does not help. The process is happening all the time; Rav Saadiah is just slowing it down so we can see it.

Why this matters: do not stop halfway

If you stop after step five, you have removed some doubts but still face the rest. And, the Rabbi stressed, "this is an important line": if you stop, you often have to start again from the beginning.

Rav Saadiah says this is why many people rejected philosophy. Some did not know the process at all. Others began it and did not finish, and so were lost in their own wisdom. He quotes the Gemara: arguments in Torah arose when students did not fully analyse what their teachers taught them. Had they done so, the tradition would have stayed clear. The Rabbi added that one of the Geonim explains this by the persecutions of the Hadrianic period, which made it hard for students to study properly.

Our example was simple, with ten doubts. A deep question about Hashem or the Torah may have twenty, or a hundred.

The conclusion: do not blame Hashem for your laziness

Rav Saadiah turns sharply to the reader. The Rabbi called it "the most important line of tonight": do not blame Hashem for your foolishness. Your doubts are because of your laziness.

You cannot remove all your doubts in a single moment. People sometimes say, "Rabbi, I will come to you, and you will answer all my questions." It does not work like that. To expect it is to step outside the laws of creation, and in fact to ask to be like Hashem. Only He knows things without a process. We have to go through it.

Rav Saadiah ends with four images of those who wait for the process to finish:

  • the silver refiner, who removes the dross before the vessel can be made ("remove the dross from the silver");
  • the milker, who presses the milk until the cream comes out;
  • the farmer, who does not just plant but waits to harvest;
  • the one who tends what he invested in until it ripens: "it is a tree of life to those who hold fast to it."

The Rabbi said Rav Saadiah could not have spoken more wisely for our own generation, which wants everything instantly, "at the click of a button". He suggested that this is part of why so much in society is falling apart.

Summary

  1. Chapter 2 ends with an oath: tell me if you find a mistake, and read with an open mind. Done properly, the book brings conviction, happiness, honesty and freedom from jealousy.
  2. Why does Hashem allow doubt? Because we are creatures, and everything created develops through a process, including understanding.
  3. The sun example: to prove even a simple statement, we rule out one possibility after another, until only the necessary remains.
  4. Do not stop halfway: if you stop, you often have to start again.
  5. Do not blame Hashem: wanting instant certainty is wanting to be like Him. Our doubts come from impatience, and the answer is patience.
