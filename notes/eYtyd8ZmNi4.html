The core of Yom Kippur

The recording begins as the Rabbi introduces his theme: Yom Kippur in our relationship with Hashem and with other people, and what we are doing in the tefillot.

Yom Kippur has five prayers, one at night and four by day. Each is an Amidah, with a repetition in the daytime services. In Musaf the repetition includes the Avodah, the account of the Temple service, which is already mentioned in the Talmud. The selihot that now fill the day were added later. He pointed out that the Mishnah describes young men and women going out to the vineyards on Yom Kippur: they did not spend the whole day in synagogue. El Melekh, which we say 26 times, comes from the period of the Geonim. Over time the hakhamim of each generation felt people should spend the whole day in synagogue, and the selihot were added after each prayer.

The one part of the selihot that is essential is the viduy, the confession. So the core of Yom Kippur is the five tefillot, the Avodah in Musaf, and the viduy.

Teshuvah is not the misvah

The Rambam is the only one to write a section called Hilchot Teshuvah. Laws usually go with a ritual: laws of sukkah, laws of shofar. Teshuvah seems to be a personal journey back to Hashem, so how can it have laws?

The Rambam holds that teshuvah is not one of the 613 commandments. The Ramban, on the verse in Nisavim "and you shall return to Hashem your God," counts it as a misvah. The Rambam's logic is that if I am speaking lashon hara, the Torah already forbids it; I do not need another misvah telling me to stop.

What the Rambam does count is viduy. His opening words: for every misvah in the Torah, positive or negative, deliberate or accidental, "when a person does teshuvah... he is obligated to confess before God." Two lessons follow: - Teshuvah is your choice: "when he does teshuvah." - Only once you have done teshuvah does the obligation to confess begin. Confession without teshuvah is not only unnecessary, it can be harmful.

The Ramban counts both teshuvah and viduy as separate misvot. Either way, the Rabbi said, what is often taught in school, that teshuvah means stopping, regretting and confessing, is wrong. Viduy is not part of the teshuvah process. It comes after it.

The three parts of teshuvah

The Rambam lists three elements: leaving the sin, regret for the past, and commitment for the future. Complete teshuvah is higher still: when Hashem can testify that this person will never return to the sin, or when a person meets the same situation with the same ability and does not sin. If you were sincere when you committed and fell a week later, it was still teshuvah.

Why these three? Because a person's identity is made of past, present and future. - Present: stop the sin now. - Past: our memories are part of who we are. Dementia is so terrible because without a past you hardly have a present. If I stop sinning but relish the memory of what I did, it is still part of me. - Future: a person with nothing to look forward to cannot really live in the moment. Without a commitment for the future, the sin remains part of my identity.

Even when all this is done, you have not yet done a misvah. Not sinning was always expected. Only now does the new misvah of viduy begin.

Why confession must be spoken

Teshuvah can happen in your head in a few moments. Viduy has to be said aloud, standing.

Do not ask forgiveness too early. Viduy is saying sorry. If I cheated a friend in business, or made a joke at his expense that everyone laughed at, and I am not sure I would not do it again, "then don't ask sorry." Such an apology is insulting, a way of asking forgiveness just to feel clean. He called it emotional blackmail. Where money was taken, restitution is also part of teshuvah.

Viduy and todah. The root of viduy (vav, dalet, he) is the same as todah, thanks. When someone does something for me, I want to restore the balance by giving back. When I cannot, I say thank you, which is a confession that I owe a debt of gratitude. Without that, there is no relationship.

The same applies to wrongdoing. When someone wrongs me, the relationship is unbalanced. That is why revenge is "sweet": I pull you down to my level. The other way to restore balance is for the one who did wrong to bring himself down by saying sorry. "Although it's just a word, that power of saying sorry is so strong." It can stop the greatest revenge.

With Hashem it is the same, and our relationship with Hashem mirrors our relationships with people. Verbalising our sins to Hashem trains us to acknowledge mistakes to people. He told a well known story of a man who prayed with great intensity at the end of the Amidah, "may my soul be like dust to all," then stepped back, bumped into someone and berated him. Begging Hashem is easier than asking people.

Mikveh and viduy

The Rambam's analogy: viduy without teshuvah is like immersing in a mikveh while holding something impure. You come out impure. Yom Kippur is a mikveh with the power to restore everything, but, as the Mishnah says, it atones for those who return.

How to say viduy

  • The long lists are generic, many items not relevant to each of us. We say them all because we are one people. Their purpose is to trigger our memory of what we need to work on. Rabbenu Nissim's viduy and the Al Het are the most specific.
  • We say viduy eleven times on Yom Kippur, counting Minhah on Erev Yom Kippur, which we say, the Gemara explains, in case one chokes at the final meal.
  • The Rambam says a proper viduy names what you did, takes responsibility and says to whom: "I was rude to you," not "forgive me if I did anything."
  • Should you tell someone about lashon hara he never knew you spoke? This is a famous disagreement between the Hafes Hayim and Rav Yisrael Salanter. Based on the Rambam, you should, since naming the act is essential; the other view is that telling may only make things worse, and it is better to say, "I know I have wronged you this year and I am sorry." Use common sense in each case. What does not work is the vague phone call an hour before Kol Nidre.
  • Viduy is said, but not publicly. You must not publicise your sins. The Spanish and Portuguese say each viduy once aloud and once silently, which gives a chance to say your own.

"The greatest viduy will be if you replace the viduy in the siddur with your own viduy." The Rambam says the more one confesses, the more praiseworthy. We have added so much to the day, he said, that there is little time left for introspection; it is better to say part of the viduy properly than all of it without thought. He even suggested that it can be more valuable to step out for an hour to think quietly than to say every word in the book.

Asked whether "modeh," to admit, is the same root, he said yes: admitting and thanking are the same idea.

Summary

  • The essence of Yom Kippur is the five tefillot, the Avodah and the viduy; the selihot were added later to fill the day.
  • For the Rambam, teshuvah (leaving the sin, regret, commitment) is a precondition, and the misvah is the viduy that follows it.
  • Viduy, like todah, restores the balance in a relationship, with people and with Hashem.
  • A real apology names what you did and to whom, and only comes after real teshuvah.
  • Use these days to prepare, so that the viduy on Yom Kippur is purposeful.
