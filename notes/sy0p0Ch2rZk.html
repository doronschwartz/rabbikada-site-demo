Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 282, seifim 1-3 Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 4 January 2024

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Where we are

Hilchot Shabbat has two sides, Zakhor and Shamor, the positive and the negative. The prohibitions begin at Siman 301. Since Siman 242 the class has been going through the positive laws in the order of Shabbat: preparing on Friday, candles, Kiddush, the meal, and before the break the start of Shabbat morning prayers. Next on Shabbat morning comes the reading of the Torah, Siman 282.

Seif 1: seven aliyot, and adding more

On Shabbat we take out the Sefer Torah and call up seven people, and more may be added. The number of aliyot shows the holiness of the day:

  • 7: Shabbat
  • 6: Yom Kippur
  • 5: Yom Tov and Rosh Hashanah
  • 4: Rosh Hodesh and Hol HaMoed. Rosh Hodesh is holier than Hanukkah and Purim, even though people get more excited about those.
  • 3: fast days, Hanukkah, Purim, Mondays and Thursdays

Adding. The Shulhan Arukh allows adding without distinguishing between days, so for Sefaradim one may add on Shabbat, Yom Kippur and Yom Tov. The Rema says the Ashkenazi custom is not to add on Yom Tov. Strictly they may (for a Brit Milah, say), but ideally they do not.

How it began. The Mishnah Berurah explains that originally only the first and last of those called up made berakhot; the ones in between fulfilled theirs by listening. The Rabbi linked this to a Spanish and Portuguese custom: someone who arrives after the Sefer Torah is taken out is not given an aliyah, since he missed the first berakhah. "I like this minhag very much." His own practice is to prefer giving aliyot to people who were there before the Amidah, though he bends this for a simhah. During Covid some communities had one person read the whole portion with one set of berakhot, based on this original practice. Already in the time of the Gemara, for the honour of the Torah, every person called up says his own berakhot.

Not too many. The Mishnah Berurah says not to add too many, because it burdens the congregation, unless it is needed to avoid an argument. There is no problem of a wasted berakhah. The Rabbi said that if it were up to him he would abolish calling everyone up on Simhat Torah: people walk around and chat outside, which is no honour to the Torah.

Weekdays. The Gemara does not add on weekdays because people need to get to work. Rav Ovadia Yosef was asked about twin bar misvah boys on a Monday or Thursday. He ruled that one may add a fourth aliyah when there is a real need: a hatan in his week of Sheva Berakhot, the father at a Brit Milah, a bar misvah boy, or twins. The weekday limit is only a custom, not a prohibition. The Rabbi stressed that wanting an aliyah to say Kaddish is not such a need. Asking a Kohen to leave is not always practical either, especially in a shul with many Kohanim.

The Spanish and Portuguese only add in odd numbers, to avoid pairs. The Rabbi said this goes back to a concern in the Gemara that we no longer keep, and he is not a fan of it.

Seif 2: repeating the reading

The Shulhan Arukh says that when adding aliyot, one may repeat verses already read. The Rema says some forbid this and the custom is to repeat only when there is no choice, which means even the Rema agrees that strictly it is allowed. The Kaf HaHayim and the Ben Ish Hai write clearly that repeating is fine.

The Rabbi's own practice is to split the portion so each person reads something new, at least three verses each, because hearing the same verses over and over gets boring and people start talking. When he calls up nine on Shabbat, he usually divides the first aliyah along the weekday divisions and, if necessary, breaks up a later one, so that every aliyah has new verses. But repeating is perfectly acceptable, as we do on Simhat Torah.

The Mishnah Berurah adds an important point: an aliyah that repeats earlier verses does not count towards the seven. That is why some communities give someone who publicly does not keep Shabbat only an extra, repeated aliyah. The Rabbi said this has to be weighed against offending people, and he personally is not particular about it.

Seif 3: who counts towards the seven

Everyone counts towards the seven, including a woman and a child who understands what the berakhot mean, from about six or seven. But the Hakhamim said a woman should not read in public, because of the honour of the congregation (kevod hasibur).

Some background. Originally each person called up read his own portion, as the Yemenite communities still do today: "That's the way it should be." The custom of a single reader began later, in medieval Europe, because many people could no longer read. A woman reading for the congregation implied that the men could not, and that is the slight to the congregation.

The Rabbi stressed that this is not like other cases where something is permitted by the Torah and forbidden by the Hakhamim, such as carrying money on Shabbat. Here the Gemara says plainly that she counts ("everyone counts towards the seven") and in the same breath says not to do it.

Why say it counts, if it is not done? The Yaavets explained, and Rav Ovadia Yosef also discusses it: imagine a community of ten men who cannot read or say the berakhot properly, and one woman who can. Strictly, she counts.

One posek ventures, with some hesitation, that in a family minyan, for example at a Shabbat Hatan in a hotel with only one family present, the grandmother of the family might be called up, since in that setting it is an honour to everyone there. The Rabbi mentioned that some today argue that since women are now as literate as men, the original concern no longer applies. He said he hears the argument but does not think we are ready for that.

A tallit for an aliyah? There is no halacha to wear one. On Shabbat we wear the tallit because it is on for Shahrit and Musaf. Sefaradim do not put one on for an aliyah at Minhah.

Practical summary

  • On Shabbat seven are called; more may be added, but not too many.
  • Sefaradim may add on Yom Tov and Yom Kippur too; Ashkenazim ideally do not add on Yom Tov.
  • On a weekday a fourth may be added for a hatan, a Brit Milah or a bar misvah, not just for someone to say Kaddish.
  • Repeating verses for extra aliyot is fine, but a repeated aliyah does not count towards the seven.
  • Women and children count towards the seven in principle, but women are not called up because of kevod hasibur.
