Topic: A step-by-step guide to the year when Erev Pesah falls on Shabbat: the firstborns' fast, the search, disposing of and selling hames, and the Shabbat meals Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, the laws of Erev Pesah that falls on Shabbat Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Holland Park and on Zoom, Sunday 30 March 2025 (Rosh Hodesh Nisan)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

An unusual year

Less than two weeks before Pesah, the Rabbi took the class through everything unique to a year in which Erev Pesah falls on Shabbat, which last happened in 2021. He went in the order things happen: Thursday, Friday, Shabbat morning, Shabbat afternoon and Saturday night.

The fast of the firstborns

The firstborns' fast is not in the Babylonian Talmud, the Rif or the Rambam. The Yerushalmi mentions it and says it is not the halacha. Its source is Masekhet Soferim, from the period of the Geonim, and the Rishonim accepted it as practice, to recall that the Jewish firstborns were spared.

  • Some Rishonim say female firstborns fast too. The Shulhan Arukh brings this but does not require it, and Sefaradim do not do it, though someone noted that Syrian women from Aleppo do.
  • The Rema records that a father fasts for a firstborn son under bar misvah. That is the Ashkenazi custom, not the Sefaradi one.
  • Because it falls on a busy day and is a custom, the practice developed of attending a siyum instead: listening to the completion of a tractate and eating from the meal exempts one from fasting.

He placed it in context. The fasts from the Torah and the Prophets are Yom Kippur and the four rabbinic fasts. The Fast of Esther is a universal custom, and the firstborns' fast has the same status.

This year it cannot be on Shabbat, and fasts are not normally brought forward. The Shulhan Arukh brings two opinions: fast on Thursday, or do not fast. For Sefaradim the main view is that firstborns do not need to fast this year. Fasting on Thursday, or at least joining a siyum, even on Zoom, is good practice. Whoever fasts, fasts the whole day.

Thursday night: the search for hames

The search cannot be on Friday night, since a flame cannot be used on Shabbat, so it moves to Thursday night, the 13th of Nisan, with the full berakhah. Ideally begin after nightfall (about 8.40pm); if that is hard, one may start from sunset (about 7.50pm), not earlier. Once the time for the search arrives one may not sit down to a meal or study Torah before searching, though a snack is fine.

  • Talk only about the search, since the berakhah covers the whole house.
  • A torch is fine, and probably less distracting. The candle was needed to see into corners in the dark; the light is what counts. (In passing he noted that Havdalah needs a real flame.)
  • Afterwards, set aside the hames you will eat on Friday and on Shabbat morning, and say Kol Hamira only for hames you do not know about. You cannot nullify what you still intend to eat.

If the search was missed it is done the next day, then on Friday night, then on Shabbat without a light, then during Pesah, and even after Pesah. Hames owned over Pesah is forbidden forever, so it must be found and destroyed.

Friday: disposing of and selling hames

The usual times. From midday on Erev Pesah, hames is forbidden by the Torah. The Hakhamim added a margin, since people told the time by the sun: no eating two hours before midday, and no ownership one hour before. This year that is about 10.45am for eating and about 11.50am for ownership, both on Shabbat morning.

This year you may own and eat hames all of Friday. The Shulhan Arukh says it is good practice to keep the usual routine: get rid of all the hames you will not need by about 11.50 on Friday. If you were at work, do it any time before Shabbat. No Kol Hamira is said on Friday.

How to dispose of it. Sefaradim need not burn it. Any complete destruction works: burning, throwing into the sea, crumbling it into the wind, or flushing it down the toilet. Your own bin is still your property, so putting hames in it is not enough, unless it is collected on Friday or put out on the street.

Selling hames. For expensive hames, such as bottles of whisky, one may sell to a non-Jew. "It's a proper sale." This year it is arranged so that hames placed in the sold cupboards until Shabbat begins is included. Someone asked about hames already thrown in the bin; the Rabbi explained that you cannot sell what you are destroying.

Work and haircuts, normally restricted from midday on Erev Pesah, are permitted all day Friday this year, since it is only the 13th.

Lettuce for maror must be washed and checked on Friday, since it cannot be rinsed of insects on Shabbat.

The Friday night meal

There are plenty of options:

  • Hallot, kept carefully so that crumbs do not spread.
  • Ordinary massah. Massah may not be eaten on Erev Pesah, but that means from the morning. On Friday night one may use two massot with hamosi (not al akhilat massah).
  • Massah ashirah or cooked massah, below.

"A really important point which people neglect": it is Shabbat. Do not eat in a corner of the house. Lay a proper table and enjoy Shabbat.

Shabbat morning: a race against 10.45

Shabbat requires a bread meal in the morning, and all hames must be finished by 10.45. At Holland Park, Shaharit would start at 7.30 and finish by 9.30, with no Kiddush in shul, so that everyone could be home by 10 and eat before 10.45. Massah ashirah (egg or "rich" massah)

Made with pure fruit juice, not water, so it is not hames and not valid for the Seder. But it is mezonot: you need about 160 grams for hamosi, three or four pieces.

Cooked massah

Regular massah briefly fried in oil or dipped in hot soup in a net before Shabbat. It can no longer be used for the misvah, so it may be eaten, and it is still hamosi: a kezayit of about 30 grams.

Shabbat afternoon and Saturday night

Seudah shelishit should ideally include bread. Hallot and regular massah are no longer allowed, so use massah ashirah or cooked massah, as early in the afternoon as possible, to keep an appetite for the Seder. Ashkenazim, who do not rely on these, use fruit or a potato-flour cake. A cake made from massah meal is not allowed, since massah meal is real massah.

  • Pesah massot are mukseh on Shabbat, since they cannot be eaten that day.
  • One may sleep on Shabbat afternoon, since resting is part of Shabbat, but do not say "I'm sleeping so I'll have energy for tonight", which is preparing on Shabbat for Yom Tov.
  • The Seder table can be laid on Friday, or from sunset (about 7.52pm), since a rabbinic restriction gives way for a misvah. No melakhah until nightfall (about 8.41pm).
  • Light a 24-hour candle before Shabbat, to light the Yom Tov candles and for Havdalah.
  • Kiddush that night includes Havdalah, in the order borei peri hagefen, Kiddush, the berakhah over the flame, Havdalah, Shehiyanu. "Follow the book."

Questions at the end. Someone using disposable plates on Shabbat asked about washing hames dishes once the sink is kashered: wash them in cold water. A dishwasher needs one empty cycle on the hottest setting. Someone asked whether one may buy hames during Pesah to give to homeless non-Jews. "Absolutely not. Grave prohibition."

Practical summary

  • Sefaradi firstborns need not fast this year; fasting on Thursday or attending a siyum is good practice.
  • Search for hames on Thursday night with a berakhah, and nullify only unknown hames.
  • On Friday, ideally get rid of all hames you will not eat by about 11.50am; the sale covers what is put away until Shabbat.
  • Friday night: hallot, ordinary massah, massah ashirah or cooked massah. Lay a proper Shabbat table.
  • Shabbat morning: daven early and eat by 10.45, using hallot, about 160g of massah ashirah, or a kezayit of massah fried or boiled before Shabbat; nullify all hames by 11.50.
  • Seudah shelishit early with massah ashirah or cooked massah; prepare nothing for the Seder before sunset.
