Topic: Rabbi Sacks' book Not in God's Name: hard texts, dualism, sibling rivalry and the chosen people Shiur: A conversation between Dayan Daniel Kada and Rabbi Joseph Dweck on Zoom, Tuesday 9 February 2021, the second evening in their series on the works of Rabbi Sacks

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Hard texts and the need to interpret

The evening opened with a powerful line from Rabbi Sacks: taking our Torah and applying it without interpretation is itself an act of violence. The Rabbi praised Rabbi Sacks for his honesty in this chapter. He admits that there are hard texts, passages that make any thinking person uncomfortable and that seem far from the world we live in. "But that's what Torah is about. Torah is about interpretation."

Rabbi Sacks quotes the Gemara in Kiddushin: one who translates a verse word for word, with no context, is a liar. The Torah has a simple meaning, but it also has a whole context, and you cannot take a single sentence and apply it as it stands. Rabbi Sacks calls this fundamentalism: going straight from revelation to application without interpretation. It is an impatience, a refusal to sit down, to study, to see how the verses speak to one another.

That is when, in the words of Pascal that Rabbi Sacks quotes, "men never do evil so completely and cheerfully as when they do it from religious conviction."

Why the war texts are no longer operable

Rabbi Sacks goes to great lengths to show that many of these texts no longer apply. The underlying message of the Torah is shalom, as Yeshayahu prophesied: nation will not lift up sword against nation. The Rabbi brought the discussion in the Gemara about carrying a sword in the public domain on Shabbat. The Hakhamim hold that a sword is a burden, not a garment. Sometimes we need weapons, but they are not part of us, not part of our essence.

He also noted the view of the Rambam that even with Amalek there is a command first to offer peace, and that war is only permitted when peace is refused. And the Gemara in Berakhot teaches that once Sanheriv mixed up the nations, we no longer know who is from the seed of Amalek.

Yet even after all this, he admitted, a difficulty remains. "Wiping out a nation, that's a difficult thing to stomach." For himself, the way he grasps these passages is through the idea that the Torah speaks to people in a particular setting. The Rambam writes that sacrifices were a concession to human psychology, so that people would not end up serving idols. The Rabbi suggested that slavery, in that week's Parashat Mishpatim, works the same way: outlawing it completely 3,300 years ago was too much to ask, so the Torah regulated it so heavily that the Gemara says one who acquires a slave acquires a master for himself. The laws of war, he suggested, should be read in the same light.

Rabbi Dweck added that the book is not about war, which is part of the natural world and will not disappear soon, but about attacking others because they are not the right religion or not God-fearing enough. He stressed that a text is given to a people to be read within a particular mindset, and that reducing that broad framework to basic fundamentalist thinking "kills the text."

Dualism and the oneness of God

Rabbi Sacks argues that extremism grows from dualism: everything framed as good against evil, us against them, children of light against children of darkness. Rabbi Dweck was clear that good and evil are very much Jewish ideas. The problem Rabbi Sacks identifies is seeing two sources of reality, a good power and an evil power, because it is so hard to hold together one God and the evil we see.

The Rabbi tied this to the Ramhal, for whom the whole purpose of creation is to recognise the yihud, the oneness of HaShem. All the positive attributes of God are beyond our understanding. The one thing we can fully grasp is that there is only one, that there are not two. That is why we say Shema twice a day. We do not need reminding that there is one God; we need reminding that there is one power, one source, and that everything comes from it. It is far easier, he said, to choose a side than to live with complexity.

Role reversal and the stranger

The Rabbi spoke about the chapter he found most powerful: role reversal, which Rabbi Sacks calls the most profound moralising experience. He told the story Rabbi Sacks brings of a rising Hungarian politician in an antisemitic party who discovered in 2012 that his own grandmother had survived Auschwitz. His whole life changed, and he later became an observant Jew.

Morality, Rabbi Sacks says, binds and blinds. It binds us to our community and blinds us to those outside it. The cure is to imagine yourself as the other. Only by seeing someone as less than human can a person commit "altruistic evil."

The Rabbi gave two examples: - David and Natan: David is furious at the rich man who took the poor man's one lamb. When Natan says "you are the man," David answers with one word, hatati, "I have sinned." The role reversal shows him his own wrong. - Yoseph and his brothers: Yoseph places Binyamin in the very position he had been in, to see whether the brothers have changed. Only when Yehudah offers himself in Binyamin's place does Yoseph reveal himself.

This is why the Torah warns us so many times not to oppress the stranger, "for you were strangers in the land of Egypt." In Rabbi Sacks' reading, one purpose of Egypt was to teach a new nation what it means to be the other.

The chosen people

Rabbi Dweck explained chosenness as an expression of real love, which is always love of a particular individual, not generic love of everything. The Rabbi added the story of the philosopher who, asked which soup he wanted, answered simply "soup." Love must be directed to someone. There is God's covenant with Noah and all humanity, and a special covenant of love with Israel.

Questions from the audience

Someone asked how God could call us chosen after seeing what Yaakov's favouritism did to his sons. The Rabbi answered that chosenness is "not a right, it's a responsibility." We are called God's firstborn, a light to the nations. God tends to choose the one who is not naturally stronger, Yishak over Yishmael, Yaakov over Esav. And when God chooses, He does not reject. That, he said, was Yaakov's mistake with Leah: it is natural to love one person more, but you must make clear you are not rejecting the other.

Asked whether a person from any nation can have a full relationship with God without becoming Jewish, Rabbi Dweck read the Rambam at the end of Hilchot Shemittah VeYovel: any person whose spirit moves him to stand before God and serve Him becomes holy of holies. It does not say he has to convert.

Someone asked about halachot that seem to say we should show no mercy to non-Jews. The Rabbi called it a hard text that needs interpretation, and brought the Meiri, who holds that these laws refer only to idol worshippers who reject God, which by his view excludes Islam and, according to many, Christianity too, which greatly narrows them. Rabbi Dweck added that such texts are about real enemies and real danger, not a licence to be "bloodthirsty and self-righteous."

Summary

  • Going straight from revelation to application without interpretation is, in Rabbi Sacks' words, fundamentalism.
  • The war texts are hedged by the command to seek peace and by the loss of Amalek's identity, though the Rabbi admitted a difficulty remains.
  • Dualism is easier than living with one God and a complex world; the Shema reminds us twice daily that there is only one source.
  • Role reversal, imagining ourselves as the other, is the strongest cure for hatred, as David and Yoseph's brothers learned.
  • Chosenness is a responsibility, and choosing one does not mean rejecting another.
