These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording. Much of the evening was sung, and the melodies cannot be captured here.

Music from the beginning of the Torah

Before introducing his guest, the Rabbi spent a few minutes on the place of music in Judaism. "It's really part and parcel." His method, he said, is always to trace an idea back to the first time it appears in the Torah.

  • Bereshit 4. Yuval is "the father of all who play the harp and the flute". Music appears at the very beginning of the Torah.
  • Bereshit 31. Lavan complains to Yaakov: had you told me, I would have sent you away "with joy and with songs, with drum and with harp". Simhah and song go together. Someone added the Midrash that Serah bat Asher sang the news to Yaakov that Yoseph was alive.
  • Miriam led the women with drums at the sea, and Tehillim 150, said every morning, praises Hashem with every kind of instrument.

Music, prophecy and the soul

The prophets reached their level through music. When Shaul was overcome by a bad spirit, his servants brought someone who could play the harp, and the spirit left him. The Rambam, in his Eight Chapters, advises a person suffering from melancholy to listen to music.

The Yerushalmi explains why the celebration on Sukkot was called Simhat Beit HaShoevah: from there they would "draw" Ruah HaKodesh, because the Shekhinah rests only where there is joy, as the verse says of Elisha: "and as the musician played, the hand of Hashem came upon him." The Leviim sang a song each day of the week, and the Mishnah in Arakhin records which instruments played on which days.

He added a note on the word hazzan. In the Talmud, the hazzan was not a singer at all. He was the synagogue official who oversaw the community, collected money and ran the services. Perhaps the word comes from "seeing", in the sense of oversight. Because this person usually also led the prayers, the title passed to the one who sings them.

How the guest began

The guest Hazzan, whom the Rabbi described as world class and as the hazzan they are fortunate to hear every week, explained that he grew up without music. At about eighteen, praying in a synagogue in Jerusalem, he heard a hazzan sing a maqam for the first time "and I went crazy". He began to ask what it was and where it came from, and at nineteen he started lessons with Hazzan Moshe Habusha, whom he called one of the greatest hazzanim of all time. "Quite late," the Rabbi said. "Better late than never."

What is a maqam?

A maqam is a musical mode. In Western terms, Maqam Ajam is the major scale and Maqam Nahawand the minor. The names come from Persian, Turkish and Syrian places and words. Change a note or the starting point and the maqam takes a new name, so there are many, but about ten are in regular use. Some are too narrow for a whole service and are used only "to spice it up". The word itself means "place", like makom: the place on the ladder of notes where the melody rests.

The guest traced the first written mentions to two Arab writers of the fourteenth century. In the Jewish world, the first was Rabbi Yisrael Najara, author of Yah Ribbon, in Safed in the time of the Arizal, who marked the maqam above each of his songs.

The main families

  • Ajam. Major and happy. Used for weddings, and for Shabbatot when the parashah tells a happy event, such as Yaakov's marriage.
  • Rast. Similar to Ajam, but with quarter tones, which Western music does not use; it sounds sharper and more Arabic. In the Aleppo and Jerusalem traditions it is used every Shabbat afternoon for Minhah, and it is called the head of the maqamat.
  • Bayat. For joyful songs, and especially for the birth of a daughter, a "bat", since the name sounds alike.
  • Hijaz. The sad maqam. The Syrians use it only on certain Shabbatot, such as the parashah of Yaakov's passing, and on Tisha BeAv, and they dislike hearing it at other times. Ajam and Hijaz combined give another maqam with its own name.
  • Siga. Very common, and the maqam of the Shema and of the Jerusalem-style Torah reading. The Turkish tradition combines it with Ajam in Maqam Huzam, which the guest called one of the most beautiful.
  • Kurd. His favourite. It belongs to the Bayat family but has no quarter tones, so it sounds almost European.
  • Nawa. Very difficult, said by tradition to come from the Beit HaMikdash, "though I don't know if that's so true". Most hazzanim use the related Nahawand instead.

The Bakkashot

The Shirat HaBakkashot began in Spain and spread through the kabbalists to Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, Greece, Egypt, Turkey and Syria, and later to Italy and, for a time, Amsterdam and London. In Syrian and Moroccan communities, on winter Friday nights, people rise at about three in the morning and sing piyyutim until Shaharit, about four hours, with tea and coffee to keep everyone awake. The Rabbi marvelled that anyone today could sit for hours singing without a phone.

A participant described the custom in Damascus, where Bakkashot were sung between Minhah and Arvit on Shabbat afternoon, and in Aleppo, where they were sung on Shabbat morning. In New York, some synagogues still sing them every week, and teachers bring young children to learn, so that talent is spotted early.

East and West

The Rabbi set out the map: the Spanish and Portuguese tradition is very Western, the Syrians, Iraqis and Tunisians very Eastern, and Morocco has its own system in between. A guest with Western musical training suggested that Moroccan and Gibraltar melodies show European influence, and the hazzan added the influence of Spanish and Andalusian music.

To hear the difference, the guests sang El Nora Alilah in the Jerusalem style and in the Spanish and Portuguese style: similar, but the Western version is more fixed, "suit and tie", while the Eastern one is free. Someone asked whether the melodies share a common origin. The guests compared tunes, including Kol Nidre, and concluded that in some cases it is hard to say.

Emotion and how to learn

Can you change a maqam to fit the mood? Yes, the hazzan said. That is the difference between Arabic and Western music: Western music is more organised; Arabic music is full of emotion, changing and adding, and an old Arabic song might have a ten or fifteen minute introduction just to build the feeling. "You can change somebody's life with music."

He told a story. At a Hanukah event in Glasgow, an elderly man took his hand and wept. He was a Holocaust survivor who as a child had loved going to synagogue with his father to hear the hazzan. After losing his whole family, he had abandoned religion. "Today you brought me back."

How should someone musical begin? Take songs you know, ask which maqam each belongs to, and compare them. Then take proper lessons. Today it is much easier, he said: there are courses across Israel for the Jerusalem and Moroccan traditions, and books that print each song with its maqam.

At the end, someone reflected on Parashat Zakhor, which some synagogues read several times in different traditions of cantillation, so that one of them "resonates" with each listener. The Rabbi thanked his guests.

Summary

  • Music runs through the Torah from Yuval and Lavan to Tehillim and the Leviim, and is linked to joy, prophecy and healing.
  • In the Talmud a hazzan was the community's official; the title later passed to the leader of prayer.
  • A maqam is a mode with its own emotional colour and set occasions: Ajam and Rast for joy, Bayat for celebrations, Hijaz for sadness, Siga for the Shema and Torah reading.
  • To learn, group the melodies you know by maqam, then study with a teacher.
