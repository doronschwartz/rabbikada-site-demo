Topic: Why the Hakhamim added the Musaf shofar blasts, and what it means to confuse the Satan, building on an idea of Rabbi Sacks Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 20 September 2022, a few days before Rosh Hashanah

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why add blasts at all?

The Rabbi opened the new term with a question about the shofar. The Torah requires nine blasts: three teruot, each with a tekiah before and after. Because we are unsure what a teruah is, we blow thirty. Then, in Musaf, the Hakhamim added more blasts, and a custom brings the total to a hundred.

Usually a rabbinic enactment extends a Torah misvah. Outside the Mikdash the Torah requires lulav only on the first day; the Hakhamim extended it to seven days in memory of the Mikdash. We still count the Omer without the offering. We wait between meat and milk as a fence around the Torah's prohibition. But the extra shofar blasts are different. There was never a hundred blasts in the Mikdash to remember. The Hakhamim simply added something new. Why?

The shofar of the King

What does Rosh Hashanah commemorate? "Today the world was conceived" means the creation of Adam, the purpose of creation. And the misvah of the day is to blow. The Gemara learns that the blowing must be with a shofar.

Tehillim makes the meaning clear. "God has ascended with a teruah, Hashem with the sound of the shofar... for God is King of all the earth" (47). "With trumpets and the sound of the shofar, shout before the King... for He comes to judge the earth" (98). The shofar means a King is present and in session, judging the whole world. The Rabbi pointed to the trumpets and pageantry of the past ten days, after the death of the Queen: that sound says royalty is here. The Gemara says, "Say before Me Malkhuyot so that you make Me King over you... and how? With the shofar." That is the plain meaning of the thirty blasts: the King is here, and we are being recreated and judged.

The shofar of the ram

But the Gemara also gives a very different reason. Why blow with a ram's horn? "So that I remember for you the binding of Yishak, and it is as if you bound yourselves before Me." Avraham and Yishak never blew a shofar; the ram was caught by its horns and offered instead. And there is no intrinsic link in the Torah between the Akedah and Rosh Hashanah. Some Midrashim place the Akedah on Rosh Hashanah, others on Yom Kippur, which is why, as the Hida writes, Sephardim read the Akedah before Minhah on Yom Kippur.

Later in the same Gemara, Rabbi Yishak asks: why do we blow seated and then blow again standing? And he answers: "to confuse the Satan." Commentators explain this differently. One view is that the prosecutor can only present his case once, and when we blow again he is gone. Others say the second blowing is an extra act of submission. But, the Rabbi said, the idea of a Satan as an outside prosecutor is strange in Judaism. We speak constantly about the yeser hara, our base animal instincts. A prosecuting Satan barely appears except on Rosh Hashanah.

The one place the Satan prosecutes

There is only one place in Tanakh where the Satan prosecutes: the opening of Iyov (Job). "And it was the day," and the Satan comes before Hashem. Hashem praises Iyov; the Satan answers that Iyov fears God only because he is blessed. Take it away, and he will curse You.

Who was Iyov? The Gemara offers many opinions, spanning centuries, and one opinion says Iyov never existed and the book is a parable. The Rabbi took that as a sign that Iyov is not a history book but a book of theology. Then why does it matter when the story happens? "And it was the day," says Rashi, means Rosh Hashanah. The Satan and Rosh Hashanah are bound together.

Rabbi Sacks' insight: humanity on trial

The usual reading is that Iyov is on trial. Rabbi Sacks suggested that if Iyov is a parable, he stands for humanity, and what is on trial is whether creating human beings was a good idea.

The Gemara in Sanhedrin says that when Hashem consulted the angels about creating man, the first two groups objected and were consumed. The third said, wisely, "The world is Yours; do as You wish." Later, at the flood and the tower, the angels said, "Were the first ones not right?" Hashem answered, "Until old age I will bear them." So there is an ongoing debate between Hashem and the angels about whether humanity is worth it.

That, Rabbi Sacks suggests, is the debate in Iyov. The Satan, standing for the prosecuting angels, says people serve You only when life is good. Hashem says: look at Iyov. Iyov loses everything, his children and his wealth. He protests, refuses his friends' claim that he must have sinned, and asks hard questions. "You never tell people when they're suffering that they're suffering because they sinned." But he never loses his faith. For a person like that, creation is justified.

And the question returns every year. Every festival is not just a commemoration but a reenactment: on Pesah we leave Egypt again, on Tisha BeAv the same destructive energy returns. On Rosh Hashanah we are recreated, and the angels ask again: is it worth it? That is the Satan: not a Christian-style devil, but the question from the dawn of creation.

Avraham's shofar

Who in the Torah was promised everything and saw it taken away? Avraham. He was promised descendants like the stars. On the first day of Rosh Hashanah we read that he was told to send away Yishmael, probably thinking his son would die in the desert. On the second day we read that he was told to offer Yishak, the son of the promise. He went anyway, with his faith intact. A participant compared Moshe, whose mission at first made things worse; the Rabbi noted the difference between a national and a personal loss, and that Hashem actually contrasted Moshe's questions with the patriarchs' silence.

So the shofar of the ram silences the Satan. Here is a human being who keeps faith when everything is taken away.

Why the second blowing was added

Now the question from the beginning is answered. In biblical times there were kings, judges and the Mikdash, and the king himself was subject to the Torah. The shofar of the Torah is the shofar of kingship. Then came the destruction, Bar Kokhba and the Hadrianic persecutions. Things were so bleak that the Gemara in Bava Batra says that by right we should have decreed not to marry and have children, letting the line of Avraham end. The Rabbi said this is what some felt after the Holocaust, and that someone who left religion after the Holocaust can never be judged.

When we had lost king, Mikdash and prophecy, the Hakhamim reframed the shofar. They added a second blowing: the shofar of Avraham and Iyov, of faith in the darkest times. That is why it is a new enactment rather than an extension, and why it comes with tears. The first blasts proclaim the King. The second say: Hashem, You have taken things from us, but we are still here for You. That faith vindicates the creation of humanity and confuses the Satan.

Asked whether we are relying on our ancestors, the Rabbi said no. Zekhut avot is like an inheritance: if you only draw on it, it runs out. Avraham shows us how to behave, and we must reenact it ourselves. The shofar awakens that faith in us; without that, a shofar alone can only do so much.

He ended by noting that people today say they do not want to bring children into such a world, and would rather have animals. "That's the argument of the Satan." Rosh Hashanah answers it. The next class would be on Erev Yom Kippur, on teshuvah.

Summary

  • The Musaf blasts are not an extension of the Torah's blasts but a new enactment with a new meaning.
  • The Torah's thirty blasts are the shofar of kingship: the King is in session.
  • In Iyov the Satan argues that people serve Hashem only when life is good; Rabbi Sacks reads this as the angels' case against creating humanity.
  • Avraham at the Akedah, like Iyov, kept faith when everything was taken from him.
  • After the destruction, the Hakhamim added the second shofar, of faith in dark times, which silences the Satan.
