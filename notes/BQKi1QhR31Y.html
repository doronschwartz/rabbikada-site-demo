Topic: How to learn halacha from its sources, and the source and reasons of the misvah of the Shabbat candle Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 263, seif 1, with the Mishnah Berurah Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada, the opening class of his Thursday-night Hilchot Shabbat series, Thursday 17 June 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why learn halacha from the text

The Rabbi opened the series by explaining its purpose. There are many shiurim where people hear halachot. What is missing is learning from the text itself, so that a person develops the skill to learn on his own and knows where each halacha comes from. "We keep lots of halakhot, but we don't always realise what's the status of these halakhot. Are they merely customs?"

The Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh. In his introduction to the Mishneh Torah, the Rambam writes that he records only what was decided by the consensus of the Hakhamim up to the close of the Talmud. That is binding on every Jew, and without a Sanhedrin it cannot be changed. Almost every line of the Mishneh Torah has a source of that standing, unless the Rambam says it is his own view. The Shulhan Arukh works differently. It gathers many books, including later customs and Ashkenazi rulings. It became the accepted authority, but a reader must know which lines are the law of the Gemara and which were added later and carry far less weight. Throughout the series the Rabbi said he would point this out.

Why Hilchot Shabbat. It is the most practical area of life, and the Mishnah Berurah writes in his introduction that without learning it regularly one can hardly keep Shabbat properly. The Ramban explains that learning Hilchot Shabbat during the week is a way of fulfilling "Zakhor et yom haShabbat", and Thursday night is the natural time to begin thinking about Shabbat.

The map. The Shulhan Arukh has four parts: Orah Hayim (every Jew's daily life), Yoreh Deah (what a rabbi is asked), Even HaEzer and Hoshen Mishpat (the dayan's sections). Orah Hayim itself has six areas, and the third is Shabbat, simanim 242 to 344. Up to 301 it covers honouring Shabbat, the meals, Kiddush, Havdalah and the candles. From 301 it covers the 39 Melakhot. The Rabbi chose to begin with lighting the candles, and stressed at once that the misvah is as much a man's as a woman's.

What is the obligation, and where is it from?

Siman 263 opens: "Be careful to light a nice candle." The word is ner, a single flame, not candles. In the time of the Gemara a ner was a clay lamp with oil and a wick; siman 264 deals with which oils and wicks may be used.

The Gemara in Shabbat (25b) calls lighting a hovah, an obligation, without giving its source. The Beit Yosef brings the Midrash Tanhuma, which points to the verse in Yeshayahu read in the haftarah: "Vekarata laShabbat oneg", call Shabbat a delight, and honour it.

Rashi and Tosafot. Rashi says the reason is kevod Shabbat: an important meal is held in the light, not in the dark. Tosafot say it is oneg Shabbat, the delight of Shabbat. The Shibbolei HaLeket explains the pleasure: you cannot enjoy food you cannot see, as anyone knows who has eaten a well-presented meal. The Rabbi said this difference will matter later in the series, when they come to electricity.

What status does it have? The source is neither the Torah nor the Hakhamim, but the prophets. The Gemara calls such laws Divrei Kabbalah, a middle category. Purim and the reading of the Megillah are another example. In a case of doubt, Divrei Kabbalah are treated like Torah law: if you are unsure whether you fulfilled a misvah of Purim, you go back and do it. So too wearing nice clothes and eating well on Shabbat are, on a simple level, from the prophets, not the Torah.

Shalom bayit

The Gemara rules that if you can afford only one, the Shabbat candle comes before the Hanukkah candle, and before wine for the daytime Kiddush, because of shalom bayit. The Rabbi asked the class: why give a new reason, and why does the Rambam never mention it? The Rambam calls the candle part of oneg Shabbat in chapter 5 and part of kevod Shabbat in chapter 30.

After a lively discussion, he brought the Arukh HaShulhan, whom he praised for his clarity, and who was the main reference for European rabbis before the Mishnah Berurah. For the Rambam there are two elements. At the table the candle is kavod. In the rest of the house it is oneg, so that people are not left stumbling and quarrelling in the dark. Shalom bayit is not a separate reason with its own source; it is a part of oneg Shabbat. That is why the Rambam does not list it, and why light is needed in every room that will be used, not only where one eats.

The Mishnah Berurah writes that the main reason is oneg Shabbat, following Tosafot.

A "nice" candle: hiddur misvah

Where does "nice" come from? The Mishnah Berurah answers: hiddur misvah, "Zeh Eli veanvehu". Every misvah has its core and its beautification, and one must know the difference. "If I'm not going to get the most perfect candle, just light it. It's fine." The core is the flame.

The Gemara promises that one who is careful with the candle will have children who are talmidei hakhamim, because "ner misvah veTorah or". The Rabbi explained that children who see their parents, and especially their mother, take a misvah seriously learn to take Torah seriously. That is "al titosh Torat imekha", the Torah of the mother who sets the values of the home. The Mishnah Berurah adds that this is a fitting time to pray.

Customs: two, seven, and one per child

The Shulhan Arukh continues: some have the practice to light two wicks, one for Zakhor and one for Shamor. The source is the Kolbo, a fourteenth-century Ashkenazi work. The Rabbi suggested how such customs arise: people added a candle, and a reason was found afterwards. One candle fulfils the misvah. On holiday there is no need to carry two.

The Ben Ish Hai writes seven, and some light ten. The custom of a candle for each child is much newer. The Rabbi said he searched for its source and found nothing older than about a hundred years. It is a beautiful custom, but not halacha, and he warned against a woman preparing many candles and lighting late.

The Rema writes that one may light as many as one wishes. This is not the custom of a candle per child.

The Maharil rules that a woman who once forgot to light adds a candle for the rest of her life. Ashkenazim, for whom the Rema is the posek, should keep it. For Sefaradim it is not halacha.

Practical summary

  • Lighting the Shabbat candle is an obligation from Divrei Kabbalah, based on the verse "call Shabbat a delight". Men and women are equally obligated.
  • Rashi sees it as kevod Shabbat and Tosafot as oneg Shabbat. Shalom bayit is part of oneg, which is why light is needed in every room in use.
  • The misvah is one flame. A nice candle is hiddur misvah, not the core.
  • Two candles, seven, or one per child are customs. They are good, but one candle fulfils the misvah.
  • The extra candle for a woman who forgot comes from the Maharil. Ashkenazim keep it; it is not binding on Sefaradim.
  • Know where each line of the Shulhan Arukh comes from before deciding how much weight it carries.
