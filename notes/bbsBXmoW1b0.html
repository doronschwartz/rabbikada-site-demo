Topic: Is there a difference between Jews and non-Jews, and if so, what is it? Sources: Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi, the Kuzari; the Rambam, Letter to Ovadyah the Convert, Hilchot Teshuvah 5:2, Hilchot Issurei Biah 19:17 and Hilchot Shemitah VeYovel 13:13 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 17 June 2024, the final class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The last fundamental

Dayan Kada opened the final class of the series by recalling what had been covered: God, the World to Come, reincarnation and, last time, suffering. He had planned a second class on suffering but decided it had been covered at length.

The last question was: is there a difference between Jews and non-Jews, and if so, what is it? Understanding this, he said, changes our relationship with our religion and with God, and some of it would surprise them.

He asked the group first. Some said there is no difference at birth, only in obligations. Others felt there is a difference but could not say what it was. He pressed them. When a man is called to the Torah he says "who chose us from all the nations." Does that imply superiority? And what about a person born Jewish who lives his whole life exactly like a decent, upstanding non-Jew, keeping nothing at all? Is he any different from his neighbour?

He explained that there are two approaches among our Hakhamim, and that he would try to bring them together at the end.

The Kuzari: a difference in essence

Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi lived in Spain in its golden age, when Torah flourished there. He gave up a comfortable life to go to the Land of Israel, out of love for the land, and died soon after arriving. His Kuzari is written as a dialogue between a king and representatives of different faiths, and sets out to show that Judaism is true.

The Kuzari builds on an ancient and widely accepted division of creation into four levels: the inanimate (rocks, water), plants, animals and human beings. Each level is different in kind from the one below. A plant grows; an animal feels pain; a human being speaks and thinks.

Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi added further levels within humanity. The Jewish people are qualitatively different from other nations, and within Israel the kohanim are different again. The difference lies in one's essence, from birth. He held that this special quality passed from Adam through Noah to Avraham, Yishak and the Jewish people. He even wrote that a convert, whom we welcome and love, cannot become a prophet, because his soul lacks the dimension needed to receive prophecy.

Someone asked how that fits with David HaMelekh, a descendant of Ruth. Dayan Kada explained that in this view the limit concerns prophecy alone; a descendant of converts can certainly be king.

He noted that this approach does not claim that every Jew is better than every non-Jew; a kind, moral non-Jew is obviously better than a wicked Jew. It speaks about the average and about the soul.

He made a broader point about context. Halachic authorities read their sources against the society they lived in. Where Jews lived among neighbours who were friendly, as in Spain, the danger of intermarriage was real and the laws designed to prevent it were applied strictly. Where Jews were persecuted, that danger hardly existed. This, he suggested, is one reason Sefaradim are stricter than Ashkenazim on bishul akum: in a Sefaradi restaurant a Jew must put the food on the fire himself, while many Ashkenazi authorities allow a Jew to light the fire in the morning.

The Rambam: the disciples of Avraham

"This is why I love the Rambam," he said. The Rambam takes a very different approach.

The letter to Ovadyah the Convert. A convert asked the Rambam whether he could say "our God and God of our fathers," "who sanctified us," "who chose us" and "who brought us out of Egypt" in his prayers. His ancestors were not taken out of Egypt; perhaps they were Egyptians. The Rambam answered that he must say everything exactly as written, alone or with the congregation, exactly as a born Jew does. Avraham taught the world the unity of God and brought many under the wings of the Shekhinah. Whoever accepts Judaism and the unity of God is counted among the disciples of Avraham, and Avraham is his father too.

Everyone can be like Moshe. In Hilchot Teshuvah the Rambam rejects the idea, held by fools among the nations and many simple Jews, that God decides at a person's creation whether he will be righteous or wicked. "Every person is fit to be righteous like Moshe Rabbenu." Every single person.

Character. The Rambam writes that cruelty and arrogance are found among idol worshippers, while the descendants of Avraham, to whom Hashem gave the Torah and its righteous laws, are merciful to all. The difference comes from the Torah they were given, not from their birth.

Any person in the world. At the end of Hilchot Shemitah VeYovel the Rambam writes that not only the tribe of Levi, but any person in the world whose spirit moves him to stand before God, serve Him and know Him, becomes holy of holies, and God will be his portion forever.

The other side of this is just as clear. The Rambam writes that a Jew who denies God and the principles of faith has left the community of Israel. He cannot be counted in a minyan, and his shehitah is not kosher, even if he does everything correctly, just as the shehitah of a non-Jew is not. "Someone comes to me and says, Rabbi, I like coming to synagogue, but I don't believe in God. Can't count that person. Still nine." "It's not about who you are, it's about what you do."

Chosen as role models

So is there any difference? Yes, said Dayan Kada, but it is not in our essence. Hashem chose a people to lead by example. A school appoints one head boy and one head girl; you cannot make the whole class head boy. Israel was chosen to be "a light unto the nations," and when Jews behave well, God takes pride in them.

The difference is in obligation and opportunity. The more misvot a person does, the more his character is refined, and that is the purpose of the whole Torah. A Jew who takes up these opportunities should become kinder, more compassionate and more caring. A Jew who does not "is exactly the same as a non-Jewish person." And in the Rambam's view, a non-Jew who kept everything would be the same as a Jew.

This is also why a Jew who misbehaves is judged more harshly: he is the one who taught the world how to behave.

Living among the nations

Discussion turned to Jews in exile. Yirmeyahu told the exiles to seek the welfare of the city where they live and pray for it, and this is the source of the prayer for the royal family. The Talmud also teaches that in exile Jews should not provoke the anger of the nations around them.

The group discussed whether Jews have been loyal to the countries they lived in. Dayan Kada said he loves Britain, which welcomed his parents from Syria, and that Jews fought loyally for their countries in both World Wars. When Jews are made to feel part of a country, they are loyal to it. The challenge is balance. "It's very hard to walk the middle path in life," to be part of society and care for one's country while knowing that one is a Jew and that one's loyalty is first to God.

What the misvot are for

He ended by showing how misvot refine character.

  • Shabbat teaches gratitude. Every week we remember that Someone created the world. When God does something for you, you thank Him, and that carries over into how we treat people.
  • Kashrut trains self-restraint. Not everything you see and smell is good for you. Eating meat is a concession: an animal is killed for it. When people salted their own meat at home, cut away the forbidden fats and removed the blood, the whole process reminded them of this. Today, he noted, we buy it ready, and lose some of that awareness.

Summary

  • The Kuzari holds that the Jewish people differ from other nations in essence, from birth; in this view even a convert cannot attain prophecy.
  • The Rambam holds that there is no difference in essence: anyone who accepts the unity of God is a disciple of Avraham, and any person can become righteous like Moshe.
  • For the Rambam, a Jew who rejects God and the Torah leaves the community of Israel; what defines us is what we do.
  • Israel was chosen to be a role model and a light unto the nations. Its distinction is greater obligation and greater opportunity.
  • The misvot exist to refine our character. Only by taking up that opportunity do we fulfil what it means to be chosen.
