Topic: Where the piyutim of the Yamim Noraim come from, attitudes to their authors, and where they may be inserted

Sources: Rabbi Shelomoh ibn Gabirol; Rabbi Elazar HaKalir; Tosafot; Ibn Ezra; the Arizal; the Rambam; the Tur; Shulhan Arukh; Rabbenu Tam; the Rama

Daily Halacha: Rosh Hashanah, Wednesday 6 September 2023

The points

  • The prayers the Hakhamim instituted for Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur (a long Musaf, a special Shaharit) are fairly short. The length comes from piyutim, later poetry not mentioned in the Talmud.
  • Most Sepharadi piyutim are by Rabbi Shelomoh ibn Gabirol of 11th-century Andalusia, who wrote over 100, including some read on Shavuot.
  • Most Ashkenazi piyutim are by Rabbi Elazar HaKalir. Where his piyutim contradict the Hakhamim, Tosafot forbids erasing them, identifying him as a Tanna (son of Rabbi Shimon bar Yohai) who followed Israeli traditions. The Ashkenazi Rishonim held him in great esteem.
  • Ibn Ezra mocked HaKalir's piyutim as often unintelligible and loose in gender and structure, so they did not enter Sepharadi prayer. Yet the Arizal accepted only HaKalir's piyutim, which is why Kabbalist services omit the usual Sepharadi ones.
  • The Rambam disliked piyutim altogether as time taken from words of wisdom.
  • The Tur and the Shulhan Arukh rule that piyutim may not be inserted within a berakhah, such as between Barukh SheAmar and Yishtabah. Rabbenu Tam permits additions within a long berakhah, and the Rama and Ashkenazim follow him.
  • Middle Eastern Sepharadim say piyutim after Shaharit, before taking out the Torah, or before Barukh SheAmar. The Spanish and Portuguese and some Moroccan communities keep them around Nishmat, where they were written to be said.

In one line

Ashkenazim follow HaKalir and insert piyutim within the berakhot, while Middle Eastern Sepharadim follow ibn Gabirol and keep them outside.
