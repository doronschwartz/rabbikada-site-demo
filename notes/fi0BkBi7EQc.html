Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Even HaEzer, Hilchot Ishut Siman: 4, Seif 20, with the later teshuvot on its use today Shiur: A Dayanut shiur by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Wednesday 31 January 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review of Seif 20

The Rabbi began by reviewing the previous class: Seifim 15-19 (the promiscuous woman, a woman who remarried while her husband was alive, the half-slave, and the Mamzer's children), and Seif 20, which is the law of a mamzer and a shifhah. The class then moved to the question of what happens today.

The review, from the Gemara in Kiddushin:

  • A male mamzer is forbidden to marry a Jewish woman, since the children would be mamzerim.
  • One option, which is not permitted for us to authorize but works if done, is that he marries a non-Jewish woman and has children, and then the children convert. They were born non-Jewish, so they cannot be mamzerim.
  • Another option is a shifhah. The child of a mamzer and a shifhah is a slave, and a slave cannot be a mamzer. When freed, the child is a regular Jew. The procedure must be done properly: she accepts the misvot of a woman and immerses, and then once the children are born she is freed, becomes a Jewish wife, and the children are free Jews.
  • It only works for a male mamzer. A mamzeret with a non-Jewish man would have Jewish children, who are mamzerim.
  • Why may a mamzer take a shifhah? The Rambam holds the prohibition for a Jew is rabbinic, and the Rabbanan waived it so as to repair the children. The Rishonim who hold it is Torah-level learn it from Lo yihyeh kadesh: a mamzer is already "set aside" (kadesh veomed).
  • It works only for a vadai mamzer, not a safek mamzer. The Pithei Teshuvah mentions the Radbaz, who may not allow, but the Rabbi said everyone rejects this.

The questions today

The Rabbi asked the class to think: if a male mamzer in the community wants to marry, why not advise him to make a shifhah, have children, and free her? The problems raised:

  1. Dina demalkhuta dina. Slavery is illegal almost everywhere. Can one use a process that is against the law of the land?
  2. Finding a woman. Who will agree to become a shifhah for years, while the couple live together?
  3. Is she really a shifhah? If the ceremony is done, but in practice they live as husband and wife, is this haramah (a trick)?

A student suggested going to a country where slavery still exists to do the procedure and then coming back. The Rabbi said that would be a big undertaking, and that at least the halachic requirements would have to be carried out there.

Part 1: the older teshuvot on dina demalkhuta

He shared a source sheet.

  • The Maharival (as quoted in the Keneset HaGedolah of Rav Hayim Benveniste) said it will not work: dina demalkhuta applies, and the woman remains a non-Jew. The Rabbi explained that if one did it, the woman remains a non-Jew, and because of that the children are kosher once they convert. So the result is still kosher children, but Beit Din cannot sanction it.
  • The Maharit held that dina demalkhuta does not apply when a law is directed only at Jews, since a dina must be universal. So even when the king forbade Jews from owning slaves, the shifhah procedure still works. The Maharit discusses a story of a wedding in Constantinople in which rabbis took part, and he gave local reasons. The Rabbi said that does not help us today, but it shows a mahloket of 400 years ago.
  • The Radbaz was seen to agree with the Maharit.

He noted that the same mahloket appears for Shabbat. If the king forbids Jews from owning slaves, is a non-Jewish worker in a Jewish home bound by the laws of shevitat avdo? The Maharit and the Radbaz would be strict (there is a shifhah), while the Maharival's approach would be lenient. This was an introduction.

Part 2: the post-war teshuvot

The main part of the class was two long teshuvot exchanged between the Helkat Yaakov (Rav Yaakov Breish, Zurich) and the Minhat Yishak (Rav Yishak Weiss, Manchester). After the war, women who thought their husbands had been killed remarried and had children, and then the first husband returned, perhaps 20 years later. The children are then vadai mamzerim. The Minhat Yishak had at least ten such cases. The question: can the children's fathers (male mamzerim) use the shifhah solution?

The Helkat Yaakov's reply

He argued that it can work, and gave reasoning along these lines:

  • The Rabbi gave an example: if the law of England said a non-Jew may not convert, and three dayanim convert someone anyway, no one says it does not work, because dina demalkhuta applies to money and not to Israel (isur). A story of a Vilna convert was mentioned: he was killed for converting, and nobody doubted his conversion.
  • A shifhah has two aspects: isur (the prohibition and the conversion side) and mamon (money, ownership). Dina demalkhuta affects the second. Normally one might say the isur follows the mamon, but here everyone, including Beit Din and the couple, knows the procedure is done only for the isur side. So it works.
  • Even if one doubts it, the result is still that the child is kosher: either a shifhah's child, or a non-Jewish woman's child who later converts.

The Minhat Yishak's reply

He did not accept this. The Helkat Yaakov's approach leaves a possible safek that the man is having relations with a non-Jewish woman. His own suggestion:

  1. Do the shifhah procedure (Yoreh Deah, Siman 266).
  2. The woman then goes to Beit Din, without her master's permission, and does a full conversion.
  3. If the Helkat Yaakov is right and she is a shifhah, the conversion without her master's authority has no value, and she stays a shifhah, with whom a mamzer may live. If he is wrong and she is not a shifhah, then she is a convert. Either way the man is with a permitted woman.

The weakness: if she is a convert, then the children are mamzerim. The Minhat Yishak answered that bediavad the children can be taken as kosher, since it is more likely that the Helkat Yaakov is right, so this is not a safek mamzer, only a safek of a lighter kind. The Rabbi explained that the Minhat Yishak is therefore using the conversion as a precaution, so that one does not allow relations with a non-Jewish woman, and is not really worried about the children. The Helkat Yaakov replied that he is the one who is concerned about the children, and the whole point of the exercise is the children, so his own advice is better.

The class discussion

A student suggested the advice may depend on family plans. The Rabbi agreed in part: if there are no plans for children, the Minhat Yishak's route (or just a conversion) works, but if the aim is children, the Helkat Yaakov's is better. The class asked how the hashash can be enough to require a conversion but not enough to treat the children as doubtful; the Rabbi's answer was that mamzerut is a serious, stringent matter, while the question of relations is one of lehatehilah versus bediavad: one cannot authorize a man to start something that may be forbidden, but once the child is born one relies on the more likely view.

The Minhat Yishak's own objections to his own idea

He then says he will not accept his own suggestion, for two reasons.

  1. A halachic reason: haramah. The woman is not treated as a shifhah, so the procedure is only a trick. If she is not a real shifhah, the children are full mamzerim.
  2. Policy. If this becomes known, it would encourage a loophole in conversion. The Rabbi described how many people come for conversion, find it too hard, and leave. If a person can first do a shifhah ceremony and then do an easier conversion, less committed people may enter this way. He added that the Minhat Yishak worried about non-Haredi batei din copying it. The Rabbi added that this is why batei din today insist that both partners in a couple be equally committed.

The Helkat Yaakov's response

  • On haramah: this is not haramah. Haramah is pretending to do something that is not done. Here the woman and the man fully intend her to have the status of a shifhah, but also agree not to treat her as one in daily life. That is not haramah, just as Hazal themselves set out the process, and just as the 16th-17th century wedding showed. He brings comparisons: selling hames before Pesah and the heter iska, where we rely on a legal mechanism.
  • On policy: the Haredi rabbis know not to do this and the non-Haredi (progressive) rabbis will not be involved in a procedure that is so anti-progressive. So there is nothing to worry about.

Conclusion

The Rabbi summed up the two proposals:

Helkat Yaakov

Minhat Yishak

Method

A real shifhah procedure

Shifhah procedure, then a full conversion

Concern

Possibly relations with a non-Jewish woman if he is wrong

Possibly mamzerut for the children, and haramah

Children

Kosher either way

Kosher bediavad

Policy

Not concerned

Concerned about its effect on conversions

He said the Helkat Yaakov, at the end, still wanted another authority to agree with him. As for what is done in practice today, the Rabbi said he did not know.

Practical summary

  • The shifhah solution only helps a vadai male mamzer, not a mamzeret and not a safek mamzer.
  • The Maharit and the Maharival disagree whether dina demalkhuta can stop a shifhah from being made, when the law is aimed only at Jews.
  • The Helkat Yaakov permitted the procedure and the Minhat Yishak did not, offering instead a shifhah procedure followed by conversion, which he later withdrew.
  • Beit Din cannot simply authorize a man to start a relationship with a non-Jewish woman, even if the children would be kosher afterwards.
  • The concerns are haramah, dina demalkhuta, and the effect on conversion policy.
