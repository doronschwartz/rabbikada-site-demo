Topic: Conduct at the Torah reading: answering Amen, calling relatives, going up and down from the Tevah, keri and ketiv, and correcting the Baal Koreh Source: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Siman 141:5-8 and Siman 142 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at the Thursday Shulhan Arukh class, Tiferet Eyal, Thursday 17 November 2022

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Answering Amen before the reading

The class continued the laws of Keriat HaTorah. The Shulhan Arukh (141:5) rules that the congregation answers Amen only once the person called up has finished the whole berakhah, "Barukh Atah Hashem, Noten HaTorah." The Baal Koreh must not begin reading until everyone has answered. The Mishnah Berurah adds that he waits even for the minority who draw out their Amen.

The custom is that the Baal Koreh also answers Amen, together with the congregation, but draws it out a little longer than everyone else, as if to announce that he is about to begin. Then he pauses briefly and starts.

The Rabbi pointed to a common mistake. Some readers think they must say Amen last, so they wait for everyone to finish and only then say their own Amen. That is wrong: it is an Amen yetomah, an orphaned Amen, said with a gap after the berakhah. The correct way is to answer together with everyone and simply lengthen it.

Calling up a father and son, or two brothers

Two brothers, or a father and son, are not called up one after the other. The Rabbi stressed the source: this is not from the Gemara. It comes from the Mordekhai, a student of the Rosh, because of ayin hara, and the Rambam does not mention it at all. "Again, people think it's a big issur."

Since it is only a custom, if a gabbai did call two brothers by name, the second must still go up. Someone called by name may not refuse, and the Gemara speaks severely about refusing an aliyah. This is one reason many places in Israel no longer call people up by name.

Some details he gave:

  • Half-brothers are treated like full brothers, as they are in most areas of halacha.
  • Grandfather and grandson is a dispute. The Mishnah Berurah allows it where there is a need, and Rav Ovadia Yoseph allows it even from the outset. This often matters at a bar misvah with only five aliyot. Ashkenazim are more hesitant, but may also do it when necessary.
  • A teacher and student are not included. The concern is family.
  • The Rema writes that even if one is the seventh and the other is Maftir, one should not call them. The Mishnah Berurah infers that where Maftir is called without a name (as in the Spanish and Portuguese community) it is allowed. Rav Ovadia Yoseph does not make this distinction.
  • When a second Sefer Torah is taken out, with Kaddish in between, it is like a new activity, and a father may be Shevi'i and the son called to the second scroll.

Someone asked what the ayin hara is, when the whole family sits together in shul anyway. The Rabbi said it is about being called up publicly, one after the other, in a single activity, which can invite envy.

He added a sense of proportion: if a gabbai calls up relatives, "don't make fights out of it." Where there are two Kohanim and only one Levi who is the brother of one of them, the order of Kohen, Levi, Yisrael comes first. One alternates the Kohanim from week to week, and ayin hara does not override the required order.

Going up and coming down

One goes up to the Tevah by the shortest route and returns by the longer one. Two reasons are given: respect for the Torah, and tirha deSibura, not making the congregation wait. The Rabbi called the second "an underrated halachic concept." If both routes are equal, one goes up on the right and returns on the left. This does not mean inventing a detour to make the way back longer.

The Mishnah Berurah writes that one should go up briskly, but not run. When called, do not start looking for your tallit or your jacket. In the Spanish and Portuguese custom the Parnas hands the tallit to the person before he is called, so he is ready. "They should know to get ready."

How long must one stay at the Tevah? The Rabbi set out the levels:

  1. The basic law (Rema): stay until the next person has come up, so the Sefer Torah is not left alone.
  2. The custom (Mishnah Berurah): wait until the next person begins his berakhah.
  3. The pious wait until the next reading has finished, so as not to miss any of the reading while walking back.
  4. Waiting until the next person's closing berakhah "has no basis at all."

In the Spanish and Portuguese custom, the next person says his berakhah, the two nod to each other, and the first walks down. If someone is elderly, tired or needs to leave, do not make him stand through a long aliyah. As soon as the next person comes up, he may go.

He also answered a question about a hazan waiting for the rabbi to finish the silent Amidah. There is no halacha to wait for the rabbi. One waits for the majority of the congregation, not just six people, in a large shul. Waiting for the rabbi sets the pace and is a good custom, but the rabbi in turn must not keep everyone waiting.

Keri and ketiv

Some words in the Torah are written one way and read another, a halacha leMosheh miSinai (141:8). They are written as in the Torah and read according to the tradition. The most striking examples are in the curses in Devarim, where three letters differ between what is written and what is read.

The Beit Yoseph records an incident from his own time in Safed. A Baal Koreh insisted on reading such a word as it was written. The hakhamim warned him, and when he refused they removed him from reading. The Rabbi said a reader who refuses to read properly is treated like the reader who could not continue, discussed the week before: someone else takes over.

Correcting the Baal Koreh (Siman 142)

The Shulhan Arukh (142:1) rules that if the reader made a mistake, "afilu bedikduk ot ahat," even in the grammar of one letter, he is made to go back. The Rema holds that one goes back only if the mistake changes the meaning. If it does not, "we rebuke him" but do not make him repeat.

The Rabbi explained that even for the Rema, a change of meaning can come through the vowels or the cantillation, not only the letters:

  • Ya'aseh (he shall do) and ye'aseh (it shall be done) mean different things.
  • Halav (milk) and halev (fat) are different words.
  • Yoshev and yashav change the tense.
  • The cantillation marks are punctuation. The atnah is a full stop and the zakef katon a comma. Reading straight through them can change the meaning.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that this applies even after the reading has finished and the closing berakhah has been said. One goes back and reads that verse with at least three verses.

The Rabbi then added cautions about who corrects:

  • If you are not an expert, do not correct. If there is a rabbi or someone more knowledgeable, it is not your place.
  • You must be 100% certain before correcting.
  • Ideally one person is assigned to correct. At a bar misvah he announces that he is the only one correcting.
  • If you are worried you heard a mistake, go to the rabbi afterwards.

He mentioned that the 1656 bylaws of the Spanish and Portuguese community fined a reader for each mistake, deducting it from his salary.

A Baal Koreh who cannot pronounce a certain letter is not "making a mistake." That is how he reads. Someone from a different tradition should follow the place where he is reading, and should only read if he can read as they do. The Haftarah and Maftir, which are repeated or less central, are not corrected in the same way.

Finally, the case of a town with a minyan but no one who can read with the correct cantillation (142:2). They still read with the berakhot, as best they can. The Mishnah Berurah brings a dispute about someone with no idea at all. The Rabbi said the words matter most, the cantillation is "a bonus," and he would not be comfortable with berakhot over someone reading one word at a time.

Practical summary

  • The Baal Koreh answers Amen together with the congregation, lengthening it slightly, and does not wait to say an orphaned Amen at the end.
  • Brothers or a father and son are not called one after the other, but this is a custom; if called by name, go up. Grandfather and grandson may be called where needed.
  • Go up the short way, briskly, and be ready when called. Stay at least until the next person comes up; the custom is until he begins his berakhah.
  • Sefaradim correct any mistake in the reading; Ashkenazim correct a mistake that changes the meaning, which includes vowels and cantillation.
  • Only an expert who is certain should correct, ideally one appointed person.
