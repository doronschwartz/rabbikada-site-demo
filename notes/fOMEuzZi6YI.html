Urhas and karpas

This class continued a discussion of the Seder begun the day before, picking up at Urhas. It was a lively, interactive class, with plenty of questions and back-and-forth from the group.

Why Sephardim wash at Urhas. Any food dipped in a liquid needs proper washing of the hands, all year round. We wash without a berakhah, but it is still a proper washing.

Speaking after washing. All year, the Shulhan Arukh says it is good not to speak between washing and Hamosi, though strictly it is permitted. The real concern is any interruption (hefsek), not only words. People think they may take as long as they like as long as they do not speak. Long conversations in sign language are a bigger interruption than a quick word. "You need something, say it out, and then continue." The same applies between Urhas and karpas: whatever is needed, such as asking for the salt water, is permitted, but nothing unnecessary.

Why karpas? Normally washing leads to a meal; here it leads to a vegetable, which arouses the curiosity of the children. Do we lean? One explanation sees karpas as the perekh, the hard labour, with the salt water as our tears, so we do not lean. Another sees it as the way of free aristocrats, who begin a meal with an hors d'oeuvre, which would call for leaning. We follow the first reason and do not lean. Rav Ben Sion Abba Shaul writes that one who wishes to be strict may take two small pieces, the first without leaning and the second with leaning.

Less than a kazayit. Karpas is not a full rabbinic eating, so there is no requirement of a kazayit. In fact you should not eat a kazayit, because the berakhah on karpas is meant to cover the maror later, and a kazayit would raise the question of a berakhah aharonah. The Rabbi takes 27 grams as the kazayit for stringency, but according to the Rambam it is already 18 grams, so one should not eat more than 18 grams of karpas. If you take Rav Ben Sion's two pieces, make sure both together stay small. "Make sure, as always, the humrah doesn't end up coming to a kulah."

Any vegetable will do. The preference is celery or parsley. Ashkenazim used potatoes, he noted, though clearly that was never the original intention.

Hungry before the Seder? Rav Ovadia Yoseph writes that if you are starving after Kiddush, eat something small, make a berakhah aharonah, and then have the karpas. Bear in mind that from the tenth hour of Erev Pesah one may not eat even fruit and vegetables in a way that spoils one's appetite.

Yahas and the afikoman

We break a massah so that the Haggadah is said over lehem oni, broken bread. The top massah stays whole for Hamosi; the middle one is broken for the berakhah al akhilat massah.

The custom of children "stealing" the afikoman, the Rabbi said, is drawn from the Rambam, who speaks of snatching the massot to keep the children awake. But training children on this holy night to take things and demand a ransom was not the custom of his community. Many, as in Aleppo, simply pass it around the table so everyone has a turn holding it.

Maggid

The Haggadah is a Torah misvah, but we remember the Exodus every night in the Shema. What is unique about this night?

  • It must be by question and answer, as the Rambam writes. Asking questions is part of freedom: emotional and physical freedom, and intellectual freedom too.
  • We act it out: in every generation a person must see himself as if he left Egypt.
  • It should be a real discussion, not just a recital of statements.

Why is there no berakhah on this Torah misvah? The main answer offered in the class: because it has no fixed limit. Other suggestions were discussed and left open.

Women. Women are obliged in the misvah too and should take part. There is no need for them to read in Hebrew if they do not understand it. "It's about understanding, it's about talking about it." The core is the question (Mah Nishtanah), the answer (Avadim Hayinu) and the Hallel at the end. If a woman is busy with the children, saying these few paragraphs in English, five to ten minutes, is the core. Men who do not understand Hebrew should at least translate these paragraphs, which is why many families translate them aloud.

How much massah?

"This is made much more complicated than it really is." From the Torah, one kazayit of massah fulfils the misvah. How do we arrive at people demanding five?

  • Mosi Massah. There is a dispute among the Rishonim whether the kazayit should come from the whole top massah or the broken middle one. So the head of the house eats a kazayit from each. Everyone else is given a little from both and supplements it from the box: one kazayit is enough for them. Rav Ben Sion writes that a married family visiting their parents should have their own plate and massot, and then they too would eat two.
  • Korekh. One kazayit.
  • Afikoman. One kazayit, a reminder of the Pesah offering. Some add a second kazayit to recall the massah eaten together with the offering. Try to, but there is no need to stuff oneself.

So: three kezeitim for everyone, four for the head of the house, five at most. For someone old or ill who finds it hard, even the head of the house can manage with one kazayit at Mosi Massah: the doubling is only a stringency. Eating from the "wrong" massah does not invalidate the misvah; "massah is massah".

The afikoman is the larger piece (the "vav"), about two thirds, and the smaller piece (the "dalet") goes back. The head of the house should make sure a kazayit's worth remains in the top and middle massot for himself.

Maror and Korekh

From washing until finishing Korekh, there should be no unnecessary talk, because the berakhot on massah and maror extend to Korekh. Necessary words are fine: "pass me the haroset". This is not the moment for children's prepared presentations; those can wait for the meal.

Dipping in haroset. The haroset recalls the mortar of Egypt. The Shulhan Arukh rules that you dip the maror right in, then shake the haroset off, so as not to dilute the bitterness. "You're going right into the servitude... and then you shake it off."

Korekh. Maror today is rabbinic, since the Torah mentions it only together with the Pesah offering, whereas massah stands on its own. Korekh recalls Hillel, who ate them together. Since we have already fulfilled maror on its own, at Korekh you may dip as much as you like and need not shake it off.

Practical summary

  • At Urhas and Mosi Massah, avoid any needless interruption, spoken or not; necessary requests are fine.
  • Eat less than 18 grams of karpas, without leaning, so the berakhah covers the maror.
  • Women should say at least Mah Nishtanah, Avadim Hayinu and the closing Hallel, in a language they understand.
  • Three kezeitim of massah for everyone, four for the head of the house; one is enough for those who struggle.
  • Submerge the maror in haroset and shake it off; at Korekh there is no need to shake it off.
