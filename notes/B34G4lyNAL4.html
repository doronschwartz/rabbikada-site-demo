Part 1: Cleaning & Searching the House

The Torah Prohibition: Bal Yera'eh u-Val Yimatzei

The Torah (Parashat Bo) states: "Ve-lo yera'eh lecha hames" — it is forbidden to own hames on Pesah. This constitutes two separate negative prohibitions (lo ta'aseh):

  1. Bal yera'eh — it shall not be seen (i.e., owned) by you
  2. Bal yimatzei — it shall not be found (i.e., owned) by you

Critical distinction: The prohibition is on owning hames, not on seeing it. If hames is in your home but doesn't belong to you, there is no Torah prohibition.

Bitul (Nullification) — The Torah-Level Solution

From the Torah's perspective, a person can simply nullify ownership of their hames (bitul) and that is sufficient. Even if the hames physically remains in the house — as long as you have genuinely abandoned ownership, there is no Torah violation.

This is done by declaring: "Kol chamira de-ika bi-reshuti... le-batil ve-lehevei hefker ke-afra de-ar'a" — all hames in my possession is nullified and ownerless like the dust of the earth.

The test of sincerity: If someone walks into your home and takes all your hames, you cannot be upset or object — because it doesn't belong to you anymore.

Why the Hachamim Required More Than Bitul

The Hachamim instituted that bitul alone is not sufficient. You must also physically dispose of hames. Two reasons:

Reason

Explanation

Insincere nullification

If you have hundreds of pounds of hames, the Hachamim were concerned you won't genuinely abandon ownership. You'll say the words but not mean them.

Temptation

Even if the nullification is sincere, having hames around you creates a risk — you might forget it's Pesah and eat it.

The Major Question: What Must You Dispose Of?

This is where the misconception arises. When the Hachamim said "dispose of hames," what exactly did they mean?

Opinion

What You Must Search For

Reasoning

Minority opinion

Everything — including crumbs

Even crumbs could tempt a person

Majority opinion

Only a ke-zayit (substantial piece)

A person won't fail to sincerely nullify crumbs, and won't be tempted by crumbs

A ke-zayit = approximately 17–27 grams. Not crumbs. A small piece of bread, something visible and substantial.

The Halacha (Hacham Ovadia and Many Poskim)

Since this is a rabbinic misvah (the Torah only requires bitul) and the logic supports the second opinion:

When searching for hames, you are looking for PIECES of hames — not crumbs.

This alone should save hours of Pesah preparation.

Why crumbs matter in the kitchen/table: Not because of the prohibition of owning hames (which only applies to a ke-zayit), but because of the prohibition of eating hames — crumbs near food preparation could get mixed into your Pesah food.

The Exception: Young Children in the House

If there are toddlers/young children (below the age of responsibility):

  • You must check everywhere — bathrooms, toilets, every room — because the Gemara says children are like mice: they carry hames everywhere.
  • However: Only check where the children can reach. If you have a high cupboard they can't access and you never put food there — no need to check it.

Specific Common Questions

Pockets: Do you need to check your pockets?

  • If you never put unwrapped food in your pockets → No need to check.
  • If you regularly put food in your pockets → Yes, check for crumbs (because you might put food in the pocket on Pesah and it'll mix with hames crumbs).
  • Adults generally don't need to. Children's pockets — yes, check them.

Books: Do you need to check books for crumbs?

  • If the books will not be used while eating on Pesah → No need to check. Crumbs in books are nullified.
  • If the books will be used while eating (e.g., Birkat HaMazon booklets) → Yes, check them. Or better: use different copies for Pesah.

Garden: Do you need to do bedikat hames in the garden?

  • Yes, if you eat/bring food there. Walk around, check for pieces. Takes 5 minutes.
  • If the last time anyone ate in the garden was many months ago (e.g., last summer in England), the food would have been consumed by animals or become inedible — no need to check.

Bedikat Hames — The Night-Time Search

The Hachamim instituted a formal search for hames on the night before Pesah.

Why at night?

  • People are busy during the day
  • A flame/candle provides focused light for checking behind furniture, in corners, etc.

When to do it:

  • Best: from nightfall (~8:45 PM in London area)
  • Acceptable: from sunset (~8:00 PM) — since it's a misvah de-rabbanan, bein ha-shmashot is fine
  • Especially if young children want to participate — can start from sunset

If you missed the night:

When You Remember

What to Do

Next morning (Erev Pesah)

Do bedikat hames during the day

During Pesah / Hol HaMoed

Still do bedikat hames — even on the 8th day

After Pesah is over

Still do bedikat hames — because hames she-avar alav ha-Pesah is asur be-hana'ah (hames owned over Pesah is permanently forbidden)

Places already checked: Any room thoroughly cleaned before bedikat hames night — where no hames has entered since — does not need to be checked again on the night of bedikat hames.

The Berakhah: Al Biur Hames (Not Bedikat Hames)

The blessing recited before the search is "al bi'ur hames" (on the disposal of hames) — not "al bedikat hames" (on the searching).

Why? Because searching is the beginning of a process that concludes with disposal the next morning. The main purpose of searching is to dispose. The Hachamim made the berakhah on the end goal, not the preliminary step.

The Minhag of Placing 10 Pieces

Origin: The Arizal instituted placing specifically 10 pieces of hames around the house before the search.

The Ra'avad's criticism: "Minhag nashim hu" — a custom of the unlearned. The misvah isn't to find hames; it's to search and then dispose. If you search and find nothing — you've still fulfilled the misvah.

The Hacham Tzvi would mock this practice.

Nevertheless — it is our minhag and we do it. But two critical points:

  1. Don't turn bedikat hames into a treasure hunt for 10 pieces. The purpose is to search the house for hames. Finding the 10 pieces is incidental — not the goal.
  2. Use pieces smaller than a ke-zayit. If you lose one of the 10 pieces, you don't need to turn the house upside down — because as we learned, you don't need to search for less than a ke-zayit. Just do bitul and you're fine.

Part 2: Hechsherim — What Needs a Pesah Certification?

Two Foundational Principles

Principle 1 — Hames on Pesah is not nullified (be-mashehu): Unlike regular kashrus where 1-in-60 nullifies a forbidden substance, on Pesah itself even a drop of hames that falls into food makes the entire food forbidden.

However: This stringency only applies on Pesah itself. Before Pesah, regular nullification (1-in-60) works.

The Sephardi position: Hames that was nullified before Pesah does not reawaken on Pesah (ein chozer ve-ne'or). This is a critical distinction from the Ashkenazi position.

Position

Before Pesah nullification

On Pesah itself

Sephardim

Works. Stays nullified even on Pesah.

A drop of hames prohibits everything.

Ashkenazim

The nullification "reawakens" on Pesah (chozer ve-ne'or).

A drop of hames prohibits everything.

Principle 2 — Achzukei issura lo machzikinan: We do not assume something went wrong unless we see it go wrong. The Shulhan Arukh gives the example of meat, cheese, and fish set aside for Pesah — even if no one was careful to guard them from hames — the food is permitted, because we don't assume hames fell in.

Why buy before Pesah? If bought on Pesah (e.g., Hol HaMoed), there's a possibility the product was manufactured on Pesah itself — and nullification doesn't work on Pesah. Buying before Pesah ensures any incidental hames contact was nullified before Pesah.

After Pesah: Products bought after Pesah are also fine — because the moment Pesah ends, nullification kicks in retroactively. The nullification does not "reawaken" the following Pesah.

Why do these items have Pesah hechsherim in stores? For Ashkenazim, these items do need certification.

The balance: Hacham Ovadia emphasizes — if you can afford to be strict, wonderful. But recognize it is a chumra, not a halachic requirement for Sephardim. And being stringent in one area often leads to leniency elsewhere (chumra ati lidei kula).

Tablets, Medications & Vitamins

The Rambam's principle: Anything with no satisfaction to the palate (she-ein bo hana'at ha-cheich) does not fall under the prohibition of hames.

The standard: Food is forbidden on Pesah only if it is ra'ui la-achilat kelev (fit for a dog to consume). Anything inedible is not prohibited.

Part 3: Kitniyot

Kitniyot Is NOT Hames

Hames = only the five grains: wheat, barley, spelt, oats, rye. Nothing else can be hames.

Kitniyot (legumes, rice, beans, corn, etc.) is a minhag — a custom that evolved among European Ashkenazim in the 13th century. Reasons given include: grains of wheat were commonly mixed with legumes, leading to confusion.

Hacham Ben Tzion Abba Shaul's observation: People are extremely particular about avoiding even a derivative of kitniyot — yet the food most statistically likely to contain hames on Pesah is massah itself (timing errors during baking, water contact, going over 18 minutes). A person who eats nothing but massah all Pesah to avoid kitniyot may actually be increasing their hames exposure.

Practical Implications

Ashkenazim eating at a Sephardi home:

  • An Ashkenazi who doesn't eat kitniyot may eat at a Sephardi home where kitniyot is served.
  • Food cooked in a pot that had kitniyot 24 hours earlier is absolutely fine for Ashkenazim — because kitniyot is not real hames. The ta'am (absorbed flavor) of kitniyot in the pot is not a problem.

An Ashkenazi woman married to a Sephardi man:

  • She may eat kitniyot. She does not need hatarat nedarim according to Hacham Ovadia (though this is a broader discussion).
