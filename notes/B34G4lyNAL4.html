Part 1: Cleaning & Searching the House

The Torah Prohibition: Bal Yera'eh u-Val Yimatzei

The Torah (Parashat Bo) states: "Ve-lo yera'eh lecha chametz" — it is forbidden to own chametz on Pesach. This constitutes two separate negative prohibitions (lo ta'aseh):

  1. Bal yera'eh — it shall not be seen (i.e., owned) by you
  2. Bal yimatzei — it shall not be found (i.e., owned) by you

Critical distinction: The prohibition is on owning chametz, not on seeing it. If chametz is in your home but doesn't belong to you, there is no Torah prohibition.

Bitul (Nullification) — The Torah-Level Solution

From the Torah's perspective, a person can simply nullify ownership of their chametz (bitul) and that is sufficient. Even if the chametz physically remains in the house — as long as you have genuinely abandoned ownership, there is no Torah violation.

This is done by declaring: "Kol chamira de-ika bi-reshuti... le-batil ve-lehevei hefker ke-afra de-ar'a" — all chametz in my possession is nullified and ownerless like the dust of the earth.

The test of sincerity: If someone walks into your home and takes all your chametz, you cannot be upset or object — because it doesn't belong to you anymore.

Why the Chachamim Required More Than Bitul

The Chachamim instituted that bitul alone is not sufficient. You must also physically dispose of chametz. Two reasons:

Reason

Explanation

Insincere nullification

If you have hundreds of pounds of chametz, the Chachamim were concerned you won't genuinely abandon ownership. You'll say the words but not mean them.

Temptation

Even if the nullification is sincere, having chametz around you creates a risk — you might forget it's Pesach and eat it.

The Major Question: What Must You Dispose Of?

This is where the misconception arises. When the Chachamim said "dispose of chametz," what exactly did they mean?

Opinion

What You Must Search For

Reasoning

Minority opinion

Everything — including crumbs

Even crumbs could tempt a person

Majority opinion

Only a ke-zayit (substantial piece)

A person won't fail to sincerely nullify crumbs, and won't be tempted by crumbs

A ke-zayit = approximately 17–27 grams. Not crumbs. A small piece of bread, something visible and substantial.

The Halacha (Chacham Ovadia and Many Poskim)

Since this is a rabbinic mitzvah (the Torah only requires bitul) and the logic supports the second opinion:

When searching for chametz, you are looking for PIECES of chametz — not crumbs.

This alone should save hours of Pesach preparation.

Why crumbs matter in the kitchen/table: Not because of the prohibition of owning chametz (which only applies to a ke-zayit), but because of the prohibition of eating chametz — crumbs near food preparation could get mixed into your Pesach food.

The Exception: Young Children in the House

If there are toddlers/young children (below the age of responsibility):

  • You must check everywhere — bathrooms, toilets, every room — because the Gemara says children are like mice: they carry chametz everywhere.
  • However: Only check where the children can reach. If you have a high cupboard they can't access and you never put food there — no need to check it.

Specific Common Questions

Pockets: Do you need to check your pockets?

  • If you never put unwrapped food in your pockets → No need to check.
  • If you regularly put food in your pockets → Yes, check for crumbs (because you might put food in the pocket on Pesach and it'll mix with chametz crumbs).
  • Adults generally don't need to. Children's pockets — yes, check them.

Books: Do you need to check books for crumbs?

  • If the books will not be used while eating on Pesach → No need to check. Crumbs in books are nullified.
  • If the books will be used while eating (e.g., Birkat HaMazon booklets) → Yes, check them. Or better: use different copies for Pesach.

Garden: Do you need to do bedikat chametz in the garden?

  • Yes, if you eat/bring food there. Walk around, check for pieces. Takes 5 minutes.
  • If the last time anyone ate in the garden was many months ago (e.g., last summer in England), the food would have been consumed by animals or become inedible — no need to check.

Bedikat Chametz — The Night-Time Search

The Chachamim instituted a formal search for chametz on the night before Pesach.

Why at night?

  • People are busy during the day
  • A flame/candle provides focused light for checking behind furniture, in corners, etc.

When to do it:

  • Best: from nightfall (~8:45 PM in London area)
  • Acceptable: from sunset (~8:00 PM) — since it's a mitzvah de-rabbanan, bein ha-shmashot is fine
  • Especially if young children want to participate — can start from sunset

If you missed the night:

When You Remember

What to Do

Next morning (Erev Pesach)

Do bedikat chametz during the day

During Pesach / Chol HaMoed

Still do bedikat chametz — even on the 8th day

After Pesach is over

Still do bedikat chametz — because chametz she-avar alav ha-Pesach is asur be-hana'ah (chametz owned over Pesach is permanently forbidden)

Places already checked: Any room thoroughly cleaned before bedikat chametz night — where no chametz has entered since — does not need to be checked again on the night of bedikat chametz.

Bi'ur Chametz — Disposing of Chametz

Tradition

Method

Sephardim (me'ikar ha-din)

Any form of disposal: crush it, throw it in a public bin, pour chemicals on it to make it inedible. Burning is not required.

Ashkenazim (minhag)

Burn the chametz

The Bracha: Al Bi'ur Chametz (Not Bedikat Chametz)

The blessing recited before the search is "al bi'ur chametz" (on the disposal of chametz) — not "al bedikat chametz" (on the searching).

Why? Because searching is the beginning of a process that concludes with disposal the next morning. The main purpose of searching is to dispose. The Chachamim made the bracha on the end goal, not the preliminary step.

The Minhag of Placing 10 Pieces

Origin: The Arizal instituted placing specifically 10 pieces of chametz around the house before the search.

The Ra'avad's criticism: "Minhag nashim hu" — a custom of the unlearned. The mitzvah isn't to find chametz; it's to search and then dispose. If you search and find nothing — you've still fulfilled the mitzvah.

The Chacham Tzvi would mock this practice.

Nevertheless — it is our minhag and we do it. But two critical points:

  1. Don't turn bedikat chametz into a treasure hunt for 10 pieces. The purpose is to search the house for chametz. Finding the 10 pieces is incidental — not the goal.
  2. Use pieces smaller than a ke-zayit. If you lose one of the 10 pieces, you don't need to turn the house upside down — because as we learned, you don't need to search for less than a ke-zayit. Just do bitul and you're fine.

Part 2: Hechsherim — What Needs a Pesach Certification?

Two Foundational Principles

Principle 1 — Chametz on Pesach is not nullified (be-mashehu): Unlike regular kashrus where 1-in-60 nullifies a forbidden substance, on Pesach itself even a drop of chametz that falls into food makes the entire food forbidden.

However: This stringency only applies on Pesach itself. Before Pesach, regular nullification (1-in-60) works.

The Sephardi position: Chametz that was nullified before Pesach does not reawaken on Pesach (ein chozer ve-ne'or). This is a critical distinction from the Ashkenazi position.

Position

Before Pesach nullification

On Pesach itself

Sephardim

Works. Stays nullified even on Pesach.

A drop of chametz prohibits everything.

Ashkenazim

The nullification "reawakens" on Pesach (chozer ve-ne'or).

A drop of chametz prohibits everything.

Principle 2 — Achzukei issura lo machzikinan: We do not assume something went wrong unless we see it go wrong. The Shulchan Aruch gives the example of meat, cheese, and fish set aside for Pesach — even if no one was careful to guard them from chametz — the food is permitted, because we don't assume chametz fell in.

Why buy before Pesach? If bought on Pesach (e.g., Chol HaMoed), there's a possibility the product was manufactured on Pesach itself — and nullification doesn't work on Pesach. Buying before Pesach ensures any incidental chametz contact was nullified before Pesach.

After Pesach: Products bought after Pesach are also fine — because the moment Pesach ends, nullification kicks in retroactively. The nullification does not "reawaken" the following Pesach.

Why do these items have Pesach hechsherim in stores? Because the kashrus market is primarily run according to Ashkenazi standards, where chozer ve-ne'or applies. For Ashkenazim, these items do need certification.

The balance: Chacham Ovadia emphasizes — if you can afford to be strict, wonderful. But recognize it is a chumra, not a halachic requirement for Sephardim. And being stringent in one area often leads to leniency elsewhere (chumra ati lidei kula).

Tablets, Medications & Vitamins

The Rambam's principle: Anything with no satisfaction to the palate (she-ein bo hana'at ha-cheich) does not fall under the prohibition of chametz.

The standard: Food is forbidden on Pesach only if it is ra'ui la-achilat kelev (fit for a dog to consume). Anything inedible is not prohibited.

Part 3: Kitniyot

Kitniyot Is NOT Chametz

Chametz = only the five grains: wheat, barley, spelt, oats, rye. Nothing else can be chametz.

Kitniyot (legumes, rice, beans, corn, etc.) is a minhag — a custom that evolved among European Ashkenazim in the 13th century. Reasons given include: grains of wheat were commonly mixed with legumes, leading to confusion.

Chacham Ben Tzion Abba Shaul's observation: People are extremely particular about avoiding even a derivative of kitniyot — yet the food most statistically likely to contain chametz on Pesach is matzah itself (timing errors during baking, water contact, going over 18 minutes). A person who eats nothing but matzah all Pesach to avoid kitniyot may actually be increasing their chametz exposure.

Practical Implications

Ashkenazim eating at a Sephardi home:

  • An Ashkenazi who doesn't eat kitniyot may eat at a Sephardi home where kitniyot is served.
  • Food cooked in a pot that had kitniyot 24 hours earlier is absolutely fine for Ashkenazim — because kitniyot is not real chametz. The ta'am (absorbed flavor) of kitniyot in the pot is not a problem.

An Ashkenazi woman married to a Sephardi man:

  • She may eat kitniyot. She does not need hatarat nedarim according to Chacham Ovadia (though this is a broader discussion).
