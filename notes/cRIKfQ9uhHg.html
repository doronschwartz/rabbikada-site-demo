Review in the Shema

"You'll never get all of Hebrew grammar in three lectures," the Rabbi began. Tonight would review the rules of sheva and kamats, then move on to syllables and the taamim, and finally the dagesh. The class went through the Shema, with the sheva rules highlighted in one colour and the kamats rules in another.

  • שְׁמַע: a sheva at the start of a word is sounded, "Shema". He also pointed to the ד at the end of אֶחָד, which has no dagesh and can be drawn out.
  • בְּכָל־לְבָבְךָ: "bekhol", because of the makaf. Then "levavekha", four syllables, because the little gaya under the first ב makes the next sheva sounded.
  • נַפְשְׁךָ: two shevas, the first silent and the second sounded.
  • הַדְּבָרִים and מְצַוְּךָ: a dagesh makes the sheva sounded.
  • וּבְשָׁכְבְּךָ: here the kamats has no gaya and is followed by a silent sheva, so it is "uveshokhbekha". Compare it with לְבָבְךָ, where the gaya changes everything.
  • In the second paragraph, וּלְעָבְדוֹ is "ulovdo" for the same reason, and בְּשָׂדְךָ is "besadekha" because of the gaya.

Someone said their siddur had a gaya under the opening ו of וּבְשָׁכְבְּךָ. That is not how we read it, he answered; our siddurim have no gaya there. Another asked about a rule they remembered, that a sheva after a long vowel is sounded. Only when there is a gaya, he said.

He also showed an exception: וְשָׂבָעְתָּ, which by the rules should have a sounded sheva but is read with a silent one. "There's always an exception to the rule." Some more practice followed: in הַמְּלָכִים the sheva is sounded for two reasons, twin letters and a dagesh.

Every word has one correct stress

Every Hebrew word has one correct pronunciation, and the taamim tell you where the stress falls. This is why everyone, not only the baal koreh, should learn from a Humash with taamim. He said he always does this with his own children.

The parashah that week was Vayakhel. Most children will say "VaYAKhel", but it is וַיַּקְהֵל, with the dagesh in the י, and the stress on the last syllable, as the note under the ה shows. In the first verse: - מֹשֶׁה is "MOsheh", stressed on the first syllable. - וַיֹּאמֶר is "vaYOmer", one of the minority of words not stressed on the last syllable. - אֵלֶּה is "Eleh". - In the next verse, שֵׁשֶׁת is "SHEshet".

About ninety percent of Hebrew words are milra, stressed on the last syllable; about ten percent are milel. When a word has two marks, such as תֵּעָשֶׂה, the main stress is on the second, but the first syllable is slightly lifted. Someone asked about the double pashta. That is an exception, he said: with two pashtas, the stress is on the first.

Where to pause

The taamim also tell you where to pause. The sof pasuk ends the verse. The etnahta is like a full stop, and the zakef katon like a comma. Pause in the wrong place, after אֵלֶּה, and you get "he said to them these", which changes the sense of the verse. With the etnahta on אֲלֵהֶם, it reads: Moshe gathered the people and said to them: these are the things that Hashem commanded.

A change of stress, a change of meaning

He gave clear pairs: - In Hoshea 12, וְדִבַּרְתִּי עַל הַנְּבִיאִים is stressed on the second-last syllable and means "I spoke to the prophets", in the past. In Parashat Terumah, וְדִבַּרְתִּי אִתְּךָ is stressed on the last syllable and means "I will speak with you". Same letters, same vowels, same dagesh. Read the second one wrongly and Hashem is saying, "I spoke to you from the kapporet," which is nonsense. - In the prayers, וְשַׂמְתָּ שְּׁמוֹ אַבְרָהָם, "VeSAMta", means "You made his name Avraham". In Parashat Yitro, וְשַׂמְתָּ עֲלֵהֶם, "vesamTA", means "You shall place over them" officers of thousands.

And this mistake, he said, is made every day in the Shema. וְאָהַבְתָּ must be stressed on the last syllable: "you shall love". Stress the middle, and it means "and you loved". The same applies to וְשִׁנַּנְתָּם, "you shall teach them", and וְדִבַּרְתָּ בָּם, "you shall speak of them". This holds whether you read with a Sephardi or an Ashkenazi accent.

Which words are milel?

There are twelve rules; he gave a few: - Pairs. עֵינַיִם, אָזְנַיִם, רַגְלַיִם, יָדַיִם are all stressed before the last syllable. - Words ending in ח, ע or ה with a furtive patah. נֹחַ is "NOah", גָּבֹהַּ is "gaVOah", רָקִיעַ is "raKIa". - Segolates. Nouns with two segols, his favourite rule: מֶלֶךְ, קֶרֶן, דֶּלֶת, שֶׁמֶן, בֶּטֶן, שֶׁלֶג, גֶּפֶן, all stressed on the first syllable. The same pattern exists in Arabic. Beginners sometimes overcorrect and say "seMAH David", but צֶמַח is a segolate too. With a guttural letter the vowels shift, as in נַעַל, שֹׁחַד, נַחַל and שַׁחַר, but they are still segolates. Someone suggested חָלָב; that is a different pattern, he said, with two kamatses. - The exception. אֱמֶת is stressed on the last syllable. His own suggestion: emet is truth, and the truth is that Hebrew words are normally milra.

Dagesh kal and dagesh hazak

In last week's parashah, Ki Tissa, the verse says אֵין קוֹל עֲנוֹת גְּבוּרָה וְאֵין קוֹל עֲנוֹת חֲלוּשָׁה קוֹל עַנּוֹת אָנֹכִי שֹׁמֵעַ. The first two words have no dagesh and come from the root "to answer"; the third, עַנּוֹת, has a dagesh and is a different word. A baal koreh who misses it has changed the meaning and must be corrected. So too on Yom Kippur, עֲנֵנוּ means "answer us", while עַנֵּנוּ would mean "afflict us".

The dagesh kal appears only in בגד כפת. It does not double the letter. It only tells you whether to say "b" or "v", "k" or "kh". It comes at the start of a word, as in כֹּהֵן, בְּרָכָה and תּוֹרָה, and after a silent sheva, as in מִזְבַּח and וְאַרְגָּמָן, where you say "mizbah", not "miz-bbah".

The exception: אהו"י. If the previous word ends in א, ה, ו or י, the next בגד כפת loses its dagesh. He showed it in the Shirah: נִצְּבוּ כְמוֹ נֵד, and וַתֵּצֶאןָ כָל הַנָּשִׁים, where the kamats sounds like a final ה. In כִּי גָאֹה גָּאָה, the first ג has no dagesh after כִּי.

The exception to the exception. If the earlier word carries a disjunctive accent, a pause, the dagesh returns, as in נֶאְדָּרִי בַּכֹּחַ and in נָמֹגוּ כֹּל יֹשְׁבֵי כְנָעַן.

And one famous oddity: מִי כָמֹכָה בָּאֵלִם ה' מִי כָּמֹכָה נֶאְדָּר בַּקֹּדֶשׁ. The same phrase, the same connecting accents, yet the first has no dagesh and the second does. "It's a famous exception, and there's no answer to it."

The dagesh hazak. Any dagesh in a letter outside בגד כפת, or in one of those letters where the rules of the dagesh kal do not apply, is strong, and the letter is doubled. Some reasons he listed: - The definite article: הַשִּׁירָה, הַתֹּף. - A missing letter: מִיַּם סוּף is "from the Red Sea", with the נ of "min" dropped; בַּמִּדְבָּר stands for "behammidbar"; מִמָּרָה is "from Marah". - A dagesh for beauty, very rare, with no grammatical reason: מִקְּדָשׁ ה' כּוֹנְנוּ יָדֶיךָ in the Shirah, and עִנְּבֵי נְזִירֶךָ in Parashat Behar.

Summary

  • The taamim mark the stress and the pauses; a wrong stress can turn "you shall love" into "you loved".
  • Most Hebrew words are milra; pairs, furtive patah words and segolates are milel, with אֱמֶת as the exception.
  • A dagesh kal in בגד כפת only changes the sound; it comes at the start of a word and after a silent sheva, unless the previous word ends in אהו"י.
  • A dagesh hazak doubles the letter, for the definite article, a missing letter, or occasionally for beauty.
  • Read with a Humash that has taamim.
