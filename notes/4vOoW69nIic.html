Where we are

Siman 1 is finished: the importance of marriage, what one may sell in order to marry, how many children one needs, and how many wives one may take, with Rabbenu Gershom. Now the Shulhan Arukh turns to the question of whom one may marry. The Rabbi gave an overview of the coming simanim:

  • Siman 2: general policies about marriage, and what one must check when marrying a woman.
  • Siman 3: Kehunah.
  • Siman 4: the forbidden unions (pesulei kahal), such as mamzer and halal.

In Siman 2, the Rabbi did Seif 1 and then Seifim 6-10 quickly, because they are easy. He then began the big sugya of Seifim 2-5: when must we check a person's family?

Seif 1: do not marry a woman who is unfit for you

The seif begins: do not marry a woman who has a pesul (a disqualification) that makes her forbidden to you. The Rema adds a ruling on a man who marries for money, quoting the Rivash and another source (the Orhot Hayim).

The family may object. Hazal were very careful about yihus (lineage); as the Gemara says, "when men fight, they fight about lineage." If a man marries a woman he may not marry, the whole family's lineage is affected, so they may object. If he goes ahead, the Beit Yoseph says they should make a clear sign, such as throwing fruit in front of his house, that he is no longer part of the family.

Marrying for money. The Rema seems to contradict itself. The beginning, quoting the Rivash, seems to say that when a woman is unfit and you marry her for money, you have unfit children. The end says that when a man marries for the money promised, his children are "not good children". The Gemara in Kiddushin has the principle that someone who marries a woman for money will have children who are not fit (banim shenei mehuganim).

  • The Helkat Mehokek's answer: there is no contradiction. If a man is attracted to a kosher woman who has money, and her parents give it out of goodwill, all agree that it is fine. The problem is when he insists on the money: they promised, then did not give, and he forces it, "I will not marry you unless you keep your promise." That is the "marrying for money" the Gemara criticises.
  • The Gra disagrees. He understands the Rema's two sources as conflicting, so that when money promised is a big part of the decision, it is a problem even without a pesul.

The Rabbi applied this to yeshiva shiddukhim: a young man says he will go forward only if he is promised a certain sum. That is certainly an issue according to the Gra. The counter-argument, in the Taz, is that someone who will spend his life learning Torah has a justification for asking for financial agreements, rather than depending on charity.

Fairness cuts both ways. The Pithei Teshuvah warns against pressing parents-in-law to pay what they promised, but also quotes the Shelah: parents must keep their word and not take advantage of the groom's embarrassment to cancel.

Seifim 6-10 in brief

These are all taken from the Gemara.

  • Seif 6: whom to marry. One should always try to marry the daughter of a talmid hakham, or marry one's own daughter to a talmid hakham. If that is not possible, a daughter of the leaders of the community, then of the charity officers, then of a teacher. Never marry the daughter of an am haares. The Gemara in Pesahim is harsh about this. The seif also says it is a misvah to marry one's niece (the Rabbi noted that nowadays we do not do this, for health reasons). The Rabbi explained that the am haares here is one who does not know or care about the misvot at all, as the Beit Shemuel says: not a question of lineage, but someone who does not understand there are misvot.
  • Seif 7: a family with a hereditary condition. If three people in a family have a disease, one should not marry into that family, since the children are likely to have it as well. Normally a hazakah is formed by three occurrences, though for milah we follow Rabbi (two boys who died). The Beit Yoseph holds that for families, even Rabbi agrees that three are needed. The Rabbi added that today, with an understanding of genetics, a single known medical condition would call for investigation.
  • Seif 8: do not marry a Kohen's daughter to an am haares.
  • Seif 9: a young man should not marry an old woman, nor an old man a young girl, since it can lead to zenut (dissatisfaction). The Helkat Mehokek says that if both agree willingly, without pressure, it is permitted.
  • Seif 10: marrying a woman with the intention to divorce. As in the Gemara of Abaye and Rava, one may say beforehand that it is for a limited period. Then it is permitted, even though the intention is not to stay.
  • Two wives in two different places: one should not do this, because the children will not know each other and may end up marrying. The Rabbi asked: what if the first wife died and he then moved to a new country? The Beit Shemuel answers that he would take his children with him, so the concern is small. After a divorce the children usually stay with the mother, so the question remains there.

The big sugya: do we have to check?

Seifim 2-5 form one big sugya. Two questions arise, both common for every rabbi:

  1. Do we need to check that a person is a Jew at all? This is addressed indirectly, and the Rabbi said it would be taken next time.
  2. Do we need to check for pesulim: whether someone is a mamzer, or a halal (a Kohen's disqualified child) or a netin? And who has to check?

The Mishnah in Kiddushin

The Mishnah says a man who marries a woman must check four generations of her mothers (and for a Kohen, a further generation), eight women, and twelve with the extra generation. Why must the man check the woman and not she him? The Gemara explains: men fight about lineage, so any problem on his side would be heard, while women do not, so her side must be checked.

The Gemara then connects this to Rabbi Yehudah, who said that kesherot may marry pesulim (a woman may marry a halal), which suggests the Mishnah speaks only of a Kohen. The Gemara says it applies to Israel as well. It concludes with two answers: either the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir, while according to the Hakhamim (whom we follow) one need not check unless there is an irur (a complaint or objection), or the Mishnah is speaking about a case with an irur. So when there is an irur, we check. But who checks, and for what, is not yet clear.

The second sugya, in Ketubot

The Gemara says that if a man says of his friend "he is a Kohen", he is believed to allow him to receive terumah, but not to allow his friend to marry a woman. The difficulty: if a woman does not need to check, what check is being done here? And how does this fit with Kiddushin?

Rashi. The man checks and the woman does not, because we find, in the case of Kehunah, that the Torah is stricter on men. Since the higher level of checking (for halalim) applies only to men, the lower level (pesulei kahal) is also required only of men. The Gemara in Ketubot, which says the friend is not believed, is about a family that came from far away, whom no one knows. Then both must check, with no irur needed. Where a family is known in the community and no irur is heard, no checks are needed on either side. So according to Rashi, a family that arrives in a community and wants to marry in, with no one knowing who they are, must be checked for four or five generations.

Rabbenu Tam. The most lenient. Everything is about a Kohen, who must check for halalut. Since Hazal required that, they required the mothers' side too (mamzerut), "derekh agav". A woman need not check, because a woman may marry a halal. An Israelite who arrives from nowhere can simply be accepted, with no single-status letter.

The Ramban. Everyone always checks for mamzerut and netinut, five generations. A Kohen adds the extra check for halalut, up to four. The Gemara's statement that the woman does not check refers only to that extra Kohen check.

The Shulhan Arukh quotes the Rambam, and the Rabbi began to look at Seif 3, noting that it appears to follow Rashi. This continues next time, with the practical results and the question of Jewish identity.

Practical summary

  • A man who marries a woman for the family's money, where the woman herself is kosher, has done nothing wrong, as long as the money is given willingly. If he forces the money, the Gemara is critical.
  • A family may object to a relative marrying someone unfit, since it affects the lineage of the whole family.
  • Pick a spouse from a good family and avoid families with a repeated medical condition.
  • The Mishnah's four-generation check applies when there is an irur, or where the family is unknown, according to the main shitot.
  • Rashi, Rabbenu Tam and the Ramban give three different answers to who must check and when. The Rabbi planned to summarise them and see the Shulhan Arukh next time.
