Chapter 8, Part 1: Can the Most Subtle Be the Strongest?

Before he gets to anthropomorphism, Rav Saadia asks one more question. We have established that Hashem is the most subtle of all, with no diversity. But how can the most subtle also be the strongest? Isn't that an oxymoron?

Look at reality, he says. Reality tells you that the more subtle something is, the more powerful it is:

Coarser

Wisdom

Water

Earth

Wind

Water

Wind is intangible, yet it moves the water

Fire

Wind

Fire is the strongest of all (he goes into some of the old science of fire)

This is why we find wisdom specifically attributed to HaKadosh Baruch Hu:

ה׳ בחכמה יסד ארץ Hashem founded the earth with wisdom. (Mishlei 3:19)

So Hashem's being the most subtle of all does not take away from His strength. On the contrary, the more subtle, the stronger.

Chapter 8, Part 2: The Foundational Principle

Now Rav Saadia turns to the question of physical descriptions, which will occupy several chapters. "Everyone after him really accepted this," the Rabbi said, then corrected himself:

"I'm not being precise there, because there were Rishonim, believe it or not. I know this is shocking. One of the Ba'alei HaTosafot writes explicitly that HaKadosh Baruch Hu has physical attributes. That's a very, very difficult concept to understand, but we have to reject that."

Rav Saadia will give convincing proofs, from Scripture, from logic and from tradition, that we cannot attribute any physical qualities to Hashem.

Substance and happening

He begins: we hear believers speaking about Hashem as if He listens to our prayers, hears us, sees everything we do. Let me first set out a principle:

Any hint of substance (an ear, an eye, a nose) or of happening ("Hashem came down to see Sodom", "Hashem stretched out His hand") is inappropriate to say of the Creator. Not a little inappropriate. It is false.

Why? We established in Essay 1 that the Creator created all physical things. There is no substance and no event in this universe that He did not create. It is ludicrous to describe Him with one of the things He Himself created.

Three points

  1. It is rationally impossible to attribute any substance or happening to Hashem.
  2. All verses in Scripture that seem to imply physical attributes must be given an explanation zulat hagashmah, other than the physical one, "until it sits well with our logic."
  3. When we speak with each other, when rabbis and believers say "Hashem is listening" and so on, we are only borrowing the terms. There is absolutely no comparison to human properties.

Once these three points are clear, says Rav Saadia, do not be troubled by what you read. Everything that follows, in these chapters and in all of Scripture, is built on this foundational principle. "You build upwards from the bottom. You don't build from the top downwards." Whenever you find something in the Tanach that gives you doubts, follow this principle.

Why does the Torah speak this way at all?

The Rabbi raised a question Rav Saadia will address explicitly later: why use these expressions? Why give the impression that Hashem has an eye and an ear? Why not speak plainly?

A participant answered that it helps people understand, by giving examples from human life. "Very good," said the Rabbi, and added more:

"If we weren't able to use these expressions, we would have no words to talk about God."

He mentioned a group of Muslim thinkers who held that one could not speak about God in any of these terms. They were mocked and lost any ability to teach, "because they can never talk about Hashem." If you only allow purely spiritual terms, you have nothing to say except that He exists. Even the words "the way Hashem relates to us" imply some association. "So you end up with nothing."

A participant compared it to the Japanese koan: we know the sound of two hands clapping, but what is the sound of one hand? It is very hard to convey such things without human terms. "Nice analogy," said the Rabbi.

1. Substance

People debate what Hashem is "made of":

  • Some say He is like a human, only a huge and very powerful one.
  • Some say He is fire, from the verse כי ה׳ אלהיך אש אכלה הוא, "Hashem your God is a consuming fire" (Devarim 4:24), "which we read on Tisha b'Av". They picture a huge ball of fire.
  • Some say He is air.
  • Some say He is space. (The Rabbi noted that the Hebrew edition's word here, kesef, silver, is a mistranslation from the Arabic. It should be space: they believe He is one great space.)

Rav Saadia's answer: since He is the Creator of humans, of fire, of air, of space and of all existence, as established in Essay 1, logic tells you none of these can be part of His essence.

The five levels of existence

Rav Saadia shows the same from Scripture. There are five levels of existence (leaving humans aside), and for each the Tanach tells us not to compare Hashem to it. The Rabbi noted that Rabbi Yehuda HaLevi does something similar in the Kuzari about a century later.

Level

Verse

Inanimate: the most precious are gold and silver

למי תדמיוני ותשוו... הזלים זהב מכיס וכסף בקנה ישקלו: "To whom will you liken Me and make Me equal... those who pour gold from the purse and weigh silver on the scale" (Yeshayahu 46:5-6)

Vegetation: the most precious is the cedar, the tallest and most beautiful tree, to which the righteous are compared

Yeshayahu's mockery of the idol carved from a tree: neither the image nor the cedar is comparable to Him

Animals

The Torah's warning not to make an image of any animal, bird, creeping thing or fish (Devarim 4)

כי מי בשחק יערך לה׳: "Who in the heavens can be compared to Hashem?" (Tehillim 89:7)

On the double expression in Yeshayahu ("to whom will you liken Me, and make Me equal"), Rav Saadia explains it works both ways: nothing is comparable to Hashem, and Hashem is not comparable to anything.

These verses gather together all five levels of existence and push us away from comparing Hashem to any of them. "This should be your foundation" whenever you meet a problematic verse.

"In the Image of God"

Now Rav Saadia faces one of the most difficult verses in the Torah:

ויברא אלהים את האדם בצלמו, בצלם אלהים ברא אותו God created man in His image, in the image of God He created him. (Bereishit 1:27)

The Rabbi asked the class how they had always understood it.

Flora suggested it means that just as Hashem is full of mercy and kindness, we should follow His ways, though she noted this is difficult since we have been told His middot are not really describable. She added Tehillim 8: ותחסרהו מעט מאלהים, "You have made him a little less than the divine."

The Rabbi gave the classical approach: tzelem means we have, as it were, the same power as God. Not identical, but the same kind of ability. Just as HaKadosh Baruch Hu creates and can destroy, we can create and destroy, physically and spiritually. When we do the right thing we create spiritual worlds. When we do the wrong thing we destroy them.

Rav Saadia's reading

Rav Saadia takes tzelem to mean a physical form. "And to be honest, he's right," the Rabbi said, "because tzelem normally has the connotation of something physical." In modern Hebrew, letzalem means to take a photograph.

Rav Saadia: This is simply an expression of honour and distinction for the human being. All lands belong to Hashem, yet He says "My land": עיני ה׳ אלהיך בה, "the eyes of Hashem your God are always on it" (Devarim 11:12). Does He own this land more? No, it signifies special importance. All mountains belong to Hashem, yet He says "My mountain." All forms of all creatures, fish, animals and birds, are His, and He distinguished one of them by calling it "My form."

So according to Rav Saadia, the form is physical, but "My form" means "this is My favourite form of all the forms I have created," not "I have a form too."

A lively debate

Chaim found it weak: "It's very general, and it's not convincing, to me at least." He suggested that something in our form and construction lets us act almost on the level of God, giving the human power to go beyond himself. The Rabbi said that is the approach of the Nefesh HaChaim, from about 200 years ago, and is quite acceptable, but it means tzelem is entirely spiritual. Rav Saadia wanted to keep the word's physical meaning.

The Rabbi offered an illustration of Rav Saadia's point: "If I have a class of students and I really like one of them, I'll say 'this is my child'." Chaim objected that the Torah is extremely precise, and if it meant "this is Mine," it would have said so. The Rabbi replied that every interpretation here involves some assumption. Another participant noted that idols are also called tzelamim because they are formed things, and wondered how one can be made "in the form" of One who has no form.

A participant offered her own simple approach: Hashem has no form at all. "Tzelem" means we should do as Hashem tells us in the Torah, which He wrote before creating the world. "I don't know if it's too simple." The Rabbi said it was an absolutely fine explanation, and that she and Chaim were really saying the same thing: that we are godly. "But just remember, you take away the simple meaning of tzelem." Rav Saadia, who is very precise about verses, would not do that, so he keeps tzelem physical and reads the possessive as love and distinction.

The evidence of the Targum

The Rabbi explained the background. The two main Targumim are Targum Onkelos, by the convert Onkelos, around the first century, and Targum Yonatan ben Uziel. The Gemara speaks of the Targum's importance, and rules that each week one should read the parasha twice with one translation, the Targum, which was the Aramaic vernacular of the time.

Rav Saadia's point: whenever these Targumim meet a verse that seems to give Hashem physicality, they change it. Here, the Rabbi suggested, Rav Saadia is "clearly taking a knock at the Karaites." Our Sages, who are trusted in their interpretation of the Torah, the students of the prophets, consistently steered away from physical readings, from their knowledge of the prophets and from their own reasoning.

The Rabbi previewed the next question. Summary

  1. Chapter 8: the most subtle is the strongest. Soul over body, wisdom over soul, water over earth, wind over water. So Hashem's subtlety does not lessen His power: "with wisdom He founded the earth."
  2. The foundational principle: no substance and no "happening" may be attributed to the Creator, who created all of them. Every such verse must be explained non-physically, and our everyday language about Hashem only borrows terms.
  3. Why the Torah speaks this way: without such language we could say nothing about Hashem but that He exists.
  4. The ten categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, possession, position, action, being acted upon.
  5. Substance: not human, fire, air or space, since He created them all. Scripture rules out comparison with all five levels of existence, from gold to angels.
  6. Tzelem Elokim: for Rav Saadia, a physical form that Hashem singled out with love ("My form," like "My land" and "My mountain"). The classical and Nefesh HaChaim readings make tzelem spiritual instead.
  7. "A consuming fire" means like fire. Scripture often drops the kaf.
  8. Quantity: no dimensions, no addition or division, proven by logic, by verse and by the Targumim, which consistently turn the physical into the non-physical.
  9. The ten organs (head, eye, ear, mouth, lip, face, hand, heart, innards, foot) are borrowed language, as when the heavens "speak" and the hills "dress in joy."
