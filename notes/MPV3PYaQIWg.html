Why a framework matters: an opening like Shabbat

This was the first of two evenings on the High Holy Day services, with Yom Kippur to follow two weeks later. Many people come to the services every year without understanding them. You cannot go through every word in one evening, the Rabbi said, but you can at least have a framework: know where the service is going and which parts are the core. He focused on the morning: Shaharit, the reading of the Torah and Musaf. The page numbers below refer to the mahzor used in the class.

Until page 73 the service is the same as a regular Shabbat morning, which the class had studied over the winter: Birkot HaShahar (which you must say whatever time you arrive), the korbanot, the introductory psalms and Pesukei DeZimrah. The only addition is the psalm for Rosh Hashanah, Psalm 81, "Tikeu vahodesh shofar," which also happens to be the psalm for Thursday.

Barukh SheAmar, on page 66, is a berakhah. It opens one long blessing that ends with Yishtabah. That is why, from Barukh SheAmar onwards, you should not start a conversation: you can return a greeting out of respect, but you are in the middle of a blessing, just as you would not stop to chat in the middle of a berakhah on water.

The piyutim and their poets

The core prayers of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur go back to the Men of the Great Assembly, at least 2,000 years. Over the centuries things were added, and nothing taken away. The main additions are the piyutim, poems mostly written in medieval Spain, many of them signed by their authors in acrostics. - "Elohai al tedineni kemaali" ("Do not judge me by my deeds"): an alphabetical petition, each line ending "my." The Rabbi attributed it to Rabbi Yehudah HaLevi of Toledo, author of the Kuzari, who gave up his position and moved to the Land of Israel, where he died soon after. - "Shofet kol haares": signed Shelomoh, by Shelomoh ibn Gabirol, the source of most of our piyutim through the year. Each stanza asks that the morning prayer we are about to say take the place of the daily morning offering. - A poem on Hashem's name, attributed to Yehudah HaLevi: four sections beginning with yud, he, vav and he, with ten, five, six and five lines, matching the numerical value of each letter. - "Yedei rashim", signed Yehudah: a lofty, mystical poem of angels and stars joining our prayer. - A poem with the refrain "Yishtabah", also signed Yehudah, clearly written to come just before Kaddish and Barekhu.

The controversy over piyutim The Rambam never wrote a poem and did not think highly of poetry in prayer. The Ibn Ezra criticised the poet Elazar HaKalir, whose long compositions fill the Ashkenazi services, for his loose Hebrew.

The bigger problem is placement. Several of these poems sit in the middle of Pesukei DeZimrah, inside the long berakhah from Barukh SheAmar to Yishtabah. The Shulhan Arukh, ruling for Sefaradim, says you should not add piyutim there, and the Middle Eastern mahzorim all move them, either to before Barukh SheAmar or, more commonly, after the Amidah of Shaharit before the reading of the Torah. Rabbenu Tam held that the concern applies only to a short berakhah, and the Rema follows this for Ashkenazim, who add piyutim inside the blessings of the Shema on festivals too. The Spanish and Portuguese took on the Ashkenazi custom here, though the Rabbi mentioned that Wembley moved them to the end of Shaharit.

His advice: if you arrive late, do not join the community in these songs. Go back to the beginning and say Birkot HaShahar and Barukh SheAmar. "That's much more important than saying this." The tunes are lovely, but most people singing do not know what the words mean.

The Amidah of Shaharit

The core of the Amidah is the third blessing, "Atah kadosh," which on Rosh Hashanah grows into a long prayer about Hashem's unity and sovereignty: may everyone recognise You, may the righteous rejoice, may all evil dissipate like smoke. It ends "HaMelekh HaKadosh." He stressed that this vision is universal: "all creatures," "everything that has breath."

"Rosh Hashanah is not about you, it's about God." People get it wrong when they arrive with a list of requests. The four additions asking for life (Zokhrenu LeHayim, Mi khamokha, Ukhtov and Besefer Hayim) were added by the Geonim around the eighth or ninth century; that is why you do not repeat the Amidah if you forget them, while forgetting HaMelekh HaKadosh means starting again. The Rambam records that some said them and some did not. Apart from these additions, the original Amidot of Shaharit and Musaf contain no personal requests at all. The Geonim added them because God's sovereignty is abstract and people need something relatable to focus on.

Asked about personal judgment on Rosh Hashanah, he agreed it exists and is sealed on Yom Kippur, but said it follows automatically once we recognise that Hashem is in control. Avinu Malkenu and the extra lines in Kaddish asking for "gates of light" are also later additions. The Spanish and Portuguese do not say Avinu Malkenu on Shabbat because it is so full of requests; the Middle Eastern Sefaradim do.

The Torah readings

  • Day one: "Vahashem pakad et Sarah," because Sarah was remembered on Rosh Hashanah, a theme of Zikhronot. The haftarah is Hannah, who also conceived on Rosh Hashanah.
  • Day two: the Akedah, for the merit of Avraham and Yishak, and because the Gemara says it took place on Rosh Hashanah. The two readings are consecutive in the Torah, so for once there is no need to roll from place to place.
  • The second haftarah (Yirmiyahu) is read for its last verse, "Zakhor ezkerenu od," which we repeat in Musaf.

Asked why Sefaradim do not say Unetaneh Tokef, he explained that it is a later Ashkenazi addition, associated with Rabbi Amnon of Mainz; a participant mentioned that it has been found in the Cairo Genizah and may be older. Before the shofar we sing "Et shaarei rason," a dramatic poem on the Akedah signed by Yehudah Shemuel Abbas, born in Fez and died in Aleppo. He observed that the High Holy Day tunes in Middle Eastern and Spanish and Portuguese communities are very similar, which suggests they predate the expulsion from Spain.

Musaf: Malkhuyot, Zikhronot, Shofarot

Musaf, the longest Amidah of the year, opens with "Ohilah laEl," a request from the hazan for the right words. After the first three blessings come three sections, which he called the key to the whole service: - Malkhuyot, sovereignty; Zikhronot, Hashem recalls everything; Shofarot, the power of the shofar. - Each has an introduction, ten verses and a closing blessing: three from the Torah, three from Ketuvim (all from Tehillim), three from the Prophets, and a tenth from the Torah. - Ketuvim come before the Prophets here because the psalms are by King David, who lived before the prophets. - In Malkhuyot, nine verses contain the word "melekh," but the tenth is Shema Yisrael. "This is the crown of malkhut": the highest sovereignty is Hashem's unity. - Why ten? The Gemara gives three reasons: the ten utterances of creation, the Ten Commandments, and the ten praises in Psalm 150, which is why that psalm is added in Shofarot.

Sitting and standing for the shofar The Torah says "Yom teruah yihyeh lakhem," and from "lakhem" the Gemara learns, as with the Omer, that the shofar is heard standing. Only thirty sounds are required; we blow 101, and only the Yemenites blow fewer. Since the congregation will stand for the blasts during Musaf, the first thirty may be heard seated. But someone who must leave early, such as a doctor on call, should stand for those first thirty.

Summary

  • Until page 73 the service is a regular Shabbat morning; Barukh SheAmar to Yishtabah is one long blessing.
  • Most piyutim are medieval Spanish poems added later; Sefaradim move them out of Pesukei DeZimrah, and latecomers should prioritise the core prayers.
  • The heart of Shaharit is the third blessing on Hashem's sovereignty; all personal requests are later additions.
  • Musaf is built on Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot, each with ten verses, crowned in Malkhuyot by Shema Yisrael.
  • Only thirty shofar sounds are required; we may sit for the first thirty because we stand for those in Musaf.
