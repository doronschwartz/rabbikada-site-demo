Sefer: Derech Hashem by Rabbi Moshe Hayim Luzzatto (the Ramhal) Section: Part 1, Chapter 4, paragraphs 3-4 Shiur: From Dayan Daniel Kada's Derech Hashem class for the Hazak girls' group (Wednesday evenings), Wednesday 10 February 2021. It fills a gap in his Derech Hashem series at Tiferet Eyal, where these paragraphs were taught but not recorded.

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review

The purpose of creation is that we earn the good by cleaving to Hashem. Had Adam chosen well, his body would have been elevated at once; since the sin, we and the world must go through death and renewal, while the soul waits in the world of souls. The more we refine ourselves here, the less our body will be a barrier later. Chapter 4 began by showing that our body is very physical.

Paragraph 3: a material world

Our surroundings are physical too. We must eat, drink, find shelter and clothing, and earn the money to pay for them, so we are "immersed in materialism", and great effort is needed to rise above it. As the Ramhal said earlier, if you stand still, you fall.

How do we deal with this? The Rabbi described two approaches the world has tried.

Living for the body. Much of the West says we are social animals: enjoy yourself, you only live once. The Rabbi quoted Rabbi Dr. Avraham Twerski, the psychiatrist and Hasidic Rebbe who had just passed away, who wrote that after more than fifty years of practice he saw one of the greatest causes of depression in people having no spiritual dimension in their lives. As Rabbi Sacks wrote, we are social mammals, but we seek meaning. Everyone has moments, at a death, a celebration or seeing something beautiful, when they feel there is more to the world than this.

Denying the body. The other approach is to ignore the body: do not marry, live in a monastery, pursue only the spirit. But the body has needs that cannot be suppressed for ever.

The Torah's way is neither. You have a body and a soul, and when you use them together in the right way, it is fine to engage with the physical. Hashem gives a framework of boundaries within which to do it.

Paragraph 4: lowering that lifts

The Ramhal writes that Hashem in His wisdom arranged that, even submerged in the material world, we can reach perfection through it. "Hashpalato itah hi aliyato": the very fact that we must lower ourselves to deal with the physical becomes our elevation, when we keep the rules. We turn darkness into light. Hashem took into account all our deficiencies and what we need to cleave to Him, and made a system to fit.

Food. Eating is something we share with every animal. Yet think how much goes into eating as a Jew: choosing kosher food, slaughtering correctly, removing the blood, checking vegetables, making sure it is a day and a time when we may eat, and finally the right berakhah before and after. Through all these stages, a purely physical act becomes a way of serving Hashem.

Marriage is the same: we do not avoid it, but there is a framework for it. The Ramhal adds that, were it not for the decree of death, every misvah would immediately strengthen the soul and refine the body; now this happens in potential, to be revealed at the resurrection.

The Torah does not ask us to deny ourselves

This principle, the Rabbi said, is shared by almost everyone. The Nazir, who abstains from wine for a time, is called a sinner. Another teaching says a person will have to give an account for every permitted fruit he saw and did not eat. "Hashem wants us to partake of the world," in the right manner: with berakhot, checking, the right time and the right intentions.

A participant asked how far we may go. The Ramhal does not give a measure here. The Rabbi's reading: as long as it does not go against a prohibition and does not come at the expense of your spiritual life, why not? Taste the fruits, look at their beauty, pursue a field of wisdom you enjoy. The problem comes when pleasure becomes an end in itself and crowds out the soul.

The Hovot HaLevavot taught that hardly any action is truly neutral. Working only to pile up money so as never to need Hashem again is a loss; working to provide for your family and give sedakah turns the same work into a misvah. Eating to stay healthy, and to see Hashem in a beautiful fruit, raises the eating.

Other questions

  • What about non-Jews? Their seven laws are broad, covering justice, loyalty, kindness and treating animals humanely, and a meal with family, eaten humanely and gratefully, can also bring one closer to God. We have more ways to elevate the world, and more responsibility too.
  • Why are there non-kosher animals? To give us a choice. One approach the Rabbi likes is that nothing is intrinsically wrong with them, just as nothing about red means stop: the limits are there to curb the pursuit of pleasure and remind us of Hashem. Rabbinic teachings are not monolithic, he added: you will usually find more than one view.

Adding prohibitions

The Rambam writes that a person who forbids himself things the Torah permitted is following a wrong path, and so too one who fasts constantly: "Are the things the Torah forbade not enough for you?" As Shlomo said, "Al tehi saddik harbeh", do not be overly righteous.

Summary

  • Our bodies and our world are physical, and without effort we fall.
  • Neither living for the body nor denying it works; the Torah gives a framework for using the world.
  • Lowering ourselves to deal with the physical, within the misvot, is itself our elevation.
  • Ordinary acts such as eating and working become service of Hashem through the right manner and intention.
  • The Rambam warns against adding prohibitions the Torah did not make.
