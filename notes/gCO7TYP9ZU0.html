Topic: The seventh principle: Moshe's prophecy was of a higher kind than any other prophet's, past or future Sources: The Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, Introduction to Helek; Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 7:6 and 8; Derech Hashem Shiur: Eighth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's Tuesday-night series at Tiferet Eyal on the Rambam's 13 Principles of Faith, Tuesday 11 February 2020

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Two kinds of prophecy

After a week's break, the class continued the second group, revelation. Principle 6 established that prophecy is God's communication with people, not mainly a matter of messages. Principle 7 adds two things: 1. There is a kind of prophecy qualitatively higher than all other prophecy. 2. Only Moshe ever had it, before or since.

As the Ramhal puts it, "prophecy divides into two levels: that of all the prophets besides Moshe, and that of our teacher Moshe."

The Rabbi noted that this is perhaps the least disputed of the principles, yet the Rambam writes about it at greater length than any other. The reason, he suggested, would emerge at the end.

The Rambam's four differences

The Rambam writes that Moshe was "the father of all the prophets before him", that he comprehended more of God than anyone ever has or will, that he reached the level of the angels, and that "there was no veil he did not pierce". His prophecy differed in four ways: 1. No intermediary. Other prophets received prophecy through an angel. To Moshe, "mouth to mouth I speak with him." 2. Awake. Other prophets prophesied in a dream or a deep sleep. Moshe heard "the voice speaking to him" while standing in the Tent of Meeting. 3. No terror. Other prophets were overwhelmed; Daniel says "no strength remained in me." Moshe spoke "face to face, as a man speaks to his friend". 4. At will. Other prophets waited days or months, and some, like Elisha, prepared with music. Moshe could say "wait, and I will hear what Hashem commands", as he did for those who missed the Pesah offering.

1. No intermediary: the polished lens

A participant asked how God could ever speak "directly", since He has no form and speech is a form. Exactly so, said the Rabbi, recalling principle 3: "speaking" means a perception transmitted to the mind, like an email that arrives with no mouth or voice.

The Ramhal explains the difference with lenses. Other prophets saw as through a series of dull lenses, the image refracted from one to the next. Moshe saw as through one clear, polished lens. So other prophecies came as riddles and allegories that the prophet had to interpret.

The source is the episode in Behaalotekha. Aharon and Miriam spoke about Moshe's separation from his wife: we too are prophets, and we live normal lives. God answered: "If there is a prophet among you, I make Myself known to him in a vision, I speak to him in a dream. Not so My servant Moshe... mouth to mouth I speak with him, in a vision and not in riddles." Until then, the Rabbi said with a smile, no one knew about principle 7.

This explains why each prophet has his own style, so that experts can tell Yeshayahu from Yirmiyahu. The books of the prophets are the prophets' own record and understanding of a vision that was not fully clear, like two students describing the same complex lecture differently. The Torah is the word of God through a polished lens. That is why, in halacha, a Humash may be placed on a book of the Prophets but not the other way round.

2 and 3. Awake and unafraid: "Ish HaElohim"

Think of someone who hears shocking news: his jaw drops, and he cannot control himself. A sleepwalker can barely stand, because the senses that keep us upright are off. So too ordinary prophets, overwhelmed by God's revelation, lost control and fell to the ground. Shaul prophesied stripped of his clothes. When Elisha's student anointed Yehu, Yehu's servants asked, "Why did this madman come to you?"

The reason is that even great prophets had not fully united their physical and spiritual sides; when the divine came, the physical gave way. Moshe is called "Ish HaElohim", the man of God. In him the human and the divine were one, so he could stand and speak with God as with a friend.

4. At will, and never "back to your tents"

After Sinai, God told the people "return to your tents", but told Moshe "you stand here with Me". The Rambam explains: other prophets returned to ordinary life when prophecy left them. Moshe never did, which is why he separated from his wife. Last time we asked why prophets fluctuate. Moshe did not: he was in constant readiness for God.

Never again?

The Rambam holds that no prophet will ever equal Moshe, even Mashiah, who will be "a great prophet, close to the level of Moshe". His source: "There has not arisen [lo kam] again [od] a prophet in Israel like Moshe." The Rambam reads "lo kam" as the past and "od" as the future.

Here, the Rabbi said, is the point of disagreement. The Ramban and Rabbi Yosef Albo held that the verse speaks only of the past, and that Mashiah will be greater than Moshe. The Ralbag noted that the verse says "in Israel": in the end of days there could be a prophet like Moshe among all humanity.

The Ani Maamin says "the prophecy of Moshe was true, and he was the chief of the prophets". The Rabbi felt the first half misplaces the point, since the truth of Moshe's Torah is principle 8. The Yigdal is closer: "No prophet like Moshe arose again, one who beheld His image."

Why is this a principle at all?

It is true and explicit in the Torah, but why make it one of the 13? The Rabbi admitted that he had first planned to combine it with principle 6. One participant suggested that Moshe must be unique for the Torah to be unique. The Rabbi offered his own view, which he called possibly controversial: the Rambam lived among Muslims and Christians who claimed that Muhammad or Jesus surpassed Moshe. "Even our greatest rabbis didn't live in a vacuum." The principle warned ordinary people never to entertain that claim. This may also be why he read the verse to cover the future.

Bringing Moshe closer

Putting Moshe so high can leave us feeling, "what has he got to do with us?" The Rabbi told a legend from the Tiferet Yisrael: a king had Moshe's portrait painted, and his experts in faces declared the man in it of terrible character. Moshe replied: "I was born like this, but I changed myself." He doubted it is true. It is only about 200 years old, and the Torah says Moshe's mother saw "that he was good". But he saw in it a need to humanise Moshe, to show that we too can grow. That, perhaps, is also why Moshe's burial place is unknown, so that he would never be made into a god.

Finally, the Rambam (Yesodei HaTorah 8) says the people believed in Moshe not because of his miracles but because they themselves witnessed God speaking to him at Sinai, in this week's parashah, Yitro. So if someone later performs wonders and tells us to change the Torah, we must not believe him. "Someone does a miracle or tells you something about the future, don't get excited."

Summary

  • Moshe's prophecy was a different kind: without intermediary, awake, without fear, and at will.
  • Other prophets saw through "dull lenses" and wrote in their own style; the Torah came through a clear lens, which is why it is holier than the Prophets.
  • In Moshe, "the man of God", the physical and the spiritual were fully united.
  • The Rambam holds no one will ever equal Moshe; the Ramban and Rabbi Yosef Albo hold that Mashiah will be greater.
  • The Rabbi suggested the Rambam made this a principle in response to claims that later prophets surpassed Moshe.
