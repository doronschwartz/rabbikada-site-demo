Source: Igrot HaRambam: the Letter to the Sages of Montpellier on Astrology (c. 1194–95), second half

Shiur: Third class in the series on the Rambam's letters, given by Dayan Daniel Kada on Zoom, Tuesday 4 February 2025

These notes are based on the Sofer.ai transcript of the recording.

Recap of Part 1

  • The letter (1194 or 1195), the Rambam's first to Provence, in Hebrew, to the sages of Montpellier and Rabbi Yonatan HaKohen of Lunel, a Tosafist and staunch defender of the Rambam.
  • Three sources of knowledge: reason, the senses, tradition (as Rav Saadiah said 200 years earlier). Uncritical acceptance of whatever is written is "a great illness."
  • Belief in astrology cost us our sovereignty and the Temple.
  • "Don't reject astrology because there are no proofs for it; reject it because there are proofs against it."
  • He read every book on paganism and found reasons for mitzvot (sha'atnez, meat and milk, pe'ot, the razor).
  • Astronomy is "an exceptional science"; astrology is a pseudo-science.

The Mishneh Torah says it all in one halacha (Avodah Zarah 11:16):

ודברים האלו כולן דברי שקר וכזב הן… וכל המאמין בדברים האלו וכיוצא בהן, ומחשב בליבו שהן אמת ודבר חכמה אבל התורה מנעה אותן, אינן אלא מן הסכלים ומחסרי הדעת… אבל בעלי החכמה ותמימי הדעת ידעו בראיות ברורות שכל אלו הדברים… אינם דברי חכמה אלא תוהו והבל

These are all lies… Whoever believes in them and thinks they're true and wise, only the Torah forbade them, is among the fools. The wise know by clear proofs that they are not wisdom but emptiness.

(Elsewhere he adds "and women and children, whose minds are not complete," reflecting a time when women were far less educated than men.)

Part 1: Three Views of Creation

Everyone in the Rambam's day, philosophers included, believed in a Creator, "basically the case until relatively recently." The debate was how the universe came about:

View

Whose

1

The world is eternal, never began and never ends, co-existing with God

Aristotle ("the majority")

2

God created the world, but from eternal unformed matter (hylē, chomer) that always existed alongside Him; even God can't make something from nothing. (In the Moreh: one day it will return to that matter)

Plato; the Ibn Ezra on Bereishit seems to hold it

3

God created everything from absolutely nothing

The prophets and our tradition (a minority of philosophers, the Moreh says)

"Thousands of books" argue each view; the Moreh says the debate is at least 3,000 years old, so 4,000 by now. The Monday class saw Rav Saadiah's thirteen views of creation and his refutation of Plato a year ago.

He doesn't name Aristotle or Plato here. The sages of Provence didn't have Golden-Age Spain's exposure to philosophy, so naming them "wouldn't be appreciated."

The Rambam here vs. in the Moreh

Here he's unequivocal: you must believe the third view; deny it and you've denied a fundamental. He wrote a whole book in Arabic (the Moreh) proving God's existence, explaining anthropomorphic verses as metaphor, dismantling the proofs for eternity, and answering the question "how can something come from nothing?"

Note the wording: he dismantled the case for eternity and answered the questions. He doesn't claim to have proved creation from nothing. In the Moreh he says explicitly that he can't prove it.

A theme of the letters: the Rambam writes for his recipient. In the Moreh (II:25) he rejects Aristotle outright but is far more sympathetic to Plato. He concludes for creation from nothing, but doesn't treat Plato's view as heresy, and some Rishonim held it. That same chapter famously says: if Aristotle had been proven, "I'd have no problem reinterpreting 'In the beginning God created' allegorically," as he does with the hundreds of anthropomorphic verses. "That's not my problem. His proofs aren't good enough." "We will see this over and over again, especially in the Letter to Yemen."

Part 2: What All Three Agree On

Intermediaries: the "trickle-down"

All three views agree God acts through intermediaries: the spheres and stars, through which His energy (shefa) flows down to create and sustain plants, animals and minerals. The Rambam accepts this: "it does not conflict with our tradition; the Sages believed it too," as he shows in the Moreh.

  • In the Moreh: ten levels; each of nine spheres has an associated intelligence, and the tenth, the Active Intellect, governs the lowest realm, our world.
  • מלאכים aren't "angels." That translation came from our exposure to Christianity. מלאך means messenger, emissary, agent: וישלח יעקב מלאכים. In the Rambam they are incorporeal intelligences through which the flow passes.
  • A participant asked: what about Gavriel, Michael and Raphael, who came to Avraham? (The Rabbi noted that Daniel, also suggested, is not an angel at all.) The names exist to give an identity to the different intermediaries: like one sunlight seen through different coloured glasses.

Free will

All three also agree a person's actions are in his own hands: serve Hashem and study, or follow the wicked into adultery and theft. Nothing pulls him either way. That's why the Torah commands at all. (The Rambam refers to his Arabic writings, the Eight Chapters.)

Where we part company: what happens to us

All three philosophical views agree what happens to a person is chance: neither God nor the stars decide it.

To them, there's no difference between a lion eating a man and a cat eating a mouse; a man falling off a roof and a stone breaking a branch; armies fighting and dogs fighting over a carcass. All chance.

We reject that. There is justice. Hashem watches every human action.

ואם תלכו עמי קרי… והלכתי עמכם בחמת קרי: "If you walk with Me by chance…, I will walk with you in the fury of chance." If you say, when bad things happen, "it's just random," I'll leave you to randomness.

Suffering comes only through sin.

Two important points

  1. For the Rambam, providence covers humans only (and what affects them). The lions and cheetahs on National Geographic really are random. His complaint about the philosophers is that they lump humans in with nature.
  2. The taxi analogy: a man with 100 identical taxis retiring to keep only 10 sells the first 90 that come to hand. He cares that ten remain (general providence over a species), not which ten. Ownerless sheep are like that. A farmer's 100 sheep, by contrast, come under providence because he does.
  3. Treat life as random, and it becomes random. Providence is withdrawn and you're left to exist like an animal: you may prosper as a lion prospers, but you're left to chance.

Part 3: Reuven the Tanner and Shimon the Spice-Merchant

Reuven is a poor tanner whose children died; Shimon a wealthy perfumer with lovely children. Three explanations:

Why?

Can it change?

The philosophers

Chance

Yes, randomly

The astrologers ("whom you've been hearing in France")

The stars decree it

Not while the stars say so

Our faith

Hashem's will, which responds to their actions

At any moment. If Shimon sins he'll become poor; if Reuven mends his ways, all will be good

Both alternatives fail logically (astrology has been disproved) and religiously: if life were random or star-fixed, what would the Torah's laws be for?

"But I don't see it that way"

The Rambam covers this in one sentence: when someone improves and things don't change, there is a backlog of sin still to be paid, or (he adds) suffering to earn a greater share in the World to Come. "We can't always understand how Hashem works."

A subtlety: two weeks ago on Monday the class saw Rav Saadiah teach that suffering can increase one's share in Olam HaBa. The Rambam doesn't hold that; in the Moreh he follows אין יסורין בלא עוון, no suffering without sin. So the editor, Rav Shilat, says that here the Rambam is citing others' view, not his own. "Difficult, I accept, but consistent." The point is simply: there is a reason.

Part 4: What About the Talmud's Astrology?

The question from last week. The Gemara (Shabbat 156) devotes half a page to it: born on Sunday, all one thing or another; Monday, angry; Tuesday, wealthy and promiscuous; Wednesday, wise; Thursday, kind; then by planet: Mars sheds blood, and so on.

I know you'll find in the Talmud and Midrashim statements of individual sages that seem to say the stars determine a person's fate. Don't let it trouble you. It isn't right to abandon a law that is logical and proven, wash your hands of it, and rely on a single sage's statement…

"Where did we see almost these exact words? Two weeks ago": the letter to Ovadia, about the Gemara that marriages are decreed forty days before birth. Don't abandon the roots for one or two statements.

Three explanations

1

The matter was hidden from him: that sage was mistaken

ממנו can mean from him or from us. Some, uncomfortable, read it as "hidden from us." "It shows how, because of personal feeling, people will reinterpret things." The Rabbi reads it as the sage himself missing something

2

He meant something deeper, hinted, metaphorical

3

He was responding to a situation

E.g. Rava: "life, children and livelihood depend on mazal, not merit." He saw two equally pious sages, Rabbah and Rav Chisda, one with a hard life, one a good one

Like zugot: the Gemara warns against doing things in pairs. "Nobody does this any more," though some do with eggs, and the S&P calls only an odd number of aliyot when adding extras (an eighth means calling a ninth too, then 11, 13, 15). Rav Moshe Feinstein wrote that such warnings apply to those who believe in them. Similarly, some say ayin hara has power only if you let it; the Rambam, the Rabbi thinks, didn't believe in it at all. "Sometimes we do that as rabbis too": people say things we don't believe, and "we don't want to shake their faith, so we give it a religious touch."

If we interpret Torah verses metaphorically, all the more so a few Rabbinic statements.

ואל ישליך אדם דעתו אחריו, כי העיניים לפנים הם ולא לאחור: don't throw your intelligence behind you; your eyes are in front, not behind.

Part 5: A False Mashiach, and the Signature

They'd heard of a false Mashiach in Isfahan. No, says the Rambam: you mixed it up. It was in Yemen, about 22 years earlier: a man claiming to be the Mashiach's herald, gathering Jews and Arabs, changing the prayers, said to work miracles. His students asked him how to relate to it.

I wrote that he means well but is a fool, that it was all falsehood, and that I feared for the Jews when the government found out. A year later he was caught. The Arab king asked for proof. "Cut off my head and I'll live." "Fine. If you're right, I'll convert." He was killed. תהא מיתתו כפרה לו ולכל ישראל. The Jews were fined in many places, and there are still fools there who say he'll rise again.

(A "second Jesus." Much more on this in the Letter to Yemen.)

The close:

Uproot the thorns and poisonous weeds; plant in their place the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; eat its fruit and enjoy it… May Hashem let us and you be satisfied from His goodness forever.

Written under great pressure, on the 11th of Tishrei, in Egypt.

"The day after Yom Kippur! He didn't spend it recovering." And: "Forgive my terseness; I'm so busy working for the gentiles." A later letter describes his schedule: busy at the Sultan's palace from Shabbat to Shabbat, studying only on Shabbat. He only replied because his friend Rav Pinchas sent a messenger with orders not to leave until the Rambam wrote.

Part 6: Others Disagree

Most Rishonim disagreed with the Rambam: the Ramban, Sefer HaChinuch, Meiri, Ralbag held astrology is a real science.

- The Ramban: don't seek out astrologers, but if you hear something from one, don't ignore it; don't rely on a miracle: take care and increase prayer.

- The Vilna Gaon famously writes sharply against the Rambam: so many Talmudic passages speak of mazal and magic, but "philosophy led him astray," and he explained the Talmud metaphorically, uprooting its simple meaning. The Gaon says there are deeper meanings, but Kabbalistic, not philosophical.

- With the Rambam: Ibn Pakuda and others before him likewise held astrology to be futile.

Robert asked: astrologers say your circumstances are set, but surely not your reactions? Reward and punishment could still work. The Ran and others who accept astrology say it gives only a tendency ("born under Mars, you tend toward blood; you can control it, but it's harder"). The Rambam himself says something similar about character in the Eight Chapters: one can be born with a tendency. But to attribute that to the stars "is too much: too much destiny." A participant suggested the Rambam might allow it as a variable, and the Rabbi agreed that is the majority view of the Sages, though not the Rambam's. "The Rambam was an outlier on this point. Each person needs to make their own choices whom they follow."

Part 7: So What Does "Mazal Tov" Mean?

"Good luck," everyone says. But we just spent two weeks saying luck is nonsense!

  1. The Rambam would never have said it. "I really can't believe he would have."
  2. Read מזל literally as flow, from נוזל: may your actions bring down a good flow**. That fits the "trickle-down" he does accept.
  3. Most people just mean congratulations.

The Shulchan Aruch forbids consulting astrologers and superstitions (Yoreh De'ah 179), then in the next halacha records the custom not to begin things on Mondays and Wednesdays and to marry when the moon is waxing. Isn't that superstition? No: it's a סימן, a sign.

That's why Sephardim traditionally say בסימן טוב, or בסימנא טבא (as at the end of the Torah reading cycle). It doesn't mean good luck; it means starting on the right foot.

The Meiri on the Rosh Hashanah foods: don't be stupid and think eating a date or an apple brings you a good year. A siman sets your frame of mind.

The practical takeaway: if you accept the Rambam, wish people "b'siman tov", or if you say "mazal tov," mean a good flow.

"It would be interesting to research when 'mazal tov' came into use. It can't be very long ago."

Summary

  1. Creation: three views (Aristotle, Plato, creation from nothing). The Rambam insists here on the third, more sharply than in the Moreh, because he writes for his audience.
  2. All agree God works through intermediaries (spheres, intelligences; mal'achim are messengers) and that humans have free will.
  3. But philosophers say what happens to us is chance, astrologers say it is fixed by the stars. Judaism says it is just, responsive to our deeds, and can change at any moment. Treat life as random and it will be.
  4. Providence in the Rambam covers humans, not animals as individuals.
  5. Talmudic astrology: the sage erred, spoke in hints, or answered a situation. Your eyes are in front, not behind.
  6. Most Rishonim (Ramban, Chinuch, Meiri, Ralbag, later the Vilna Gaon) disagree; the Rambam is an outlier.
  7. Say "b'siman tov."
