Shohet: the mosquito at night

In the Mishkan, one of the roofs was made from the hides of rams and the tahash, so the animals had to be caught and killed. Hence the melakhah of Shohet.

The question. A mosquito is keeping you awake. May you trap it, or kill it? The Shulhan Arukh says one may not trap a flea unless it is on your skin and biting, and even then one may not kill it.

Why the difference? Both acts are melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah, since you do not want the insect, only to be rid of it. But trapping a creature that people do not usually hunt is only rabbinic in the first place, so where there is real discomfort the Hakhamim waived it. Killing is a melakhah of the Torah, and doing it without needing the creature makes it rabbinic, but that is the most severe rabbinic prohibition, as the class saw in the second shiur. It is not waived for discomfort. (For the Rambam it is a Torah prohibition.)

Spraying. Spraying into the air to drive them away is permitted, since you do not necessarily intend to kill; if some die, that is a davar sheeino mitkaven. Spraying directly onto them would seemingly be forbidden. You may spray around them so they leave. If there is a real risk of disease or a dangerous allergy, that may be a matter of danger and the rules are different.

Flushing the toilet over insects

The poskim discuss insects in the toilet, and give five reasons to permit flushing:

  1. At worst it is melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah, since you do not want the insects.
  2. It may not be pesik reisha at all: the insects may drown in the extra water rather than be killed by the flow, which would be gerama.
  3. Mitasek. Some acts are so removed from the result that, even if it is inevitable, they are not the melakhah. Walking past a house whose sensor light comes on is not an act of kindling; flushing is not an act of killing. Compare cutting a chicken's head off "to play with it", which is an act of slaughter, or pouring water over grass, which is an act of watering. The Rashba permits closing a door on a deer for the same reason, and the Maggid Mishneh says something similar.
  4. Kavod haberiyot, human dignity, which permits even melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah, as with rubbing spittle into the ground.
  5. Hazon Ovadia adds that flushing only releases water held back, so perhaps it is gerama, though the Rabbi thought that debatable.

Walking past a sensor light. Many poskim rely on the mitasek reasoning to walk past a light that comes on, as long as you do not need or want the light.

Insects in the sink. Here there is no kavod haberiyot and not all the reasons apply. If there is another option, use it. If you must, and the water does not fall directly on them, it may be permitted as gerama, for a need.

Infested vegetables. Many say you must wash them before Shabbat. The Rabbi disagreed. Most produce is not certainly infested, so it is a davar sheeino mitkaven. Even when a batch is known to be infested but you cannot see where, each rub is a safek pesik reisha, which the halacha permits. Better to wash before Shabbat; but if not, it is permitted. Killing an insect you can see is forbidden.

Drawing blood. Most Rishonim forbid wounding on Shabbat because of Shohet; the Rambam because of Dash, extracting.

Brushing teeth

With water only. Squeezing water from the bristles is at worst rabbinic, since the bristles do not absorb water, like drying hair, and a sponge with a handle is permitted. Bleeding: if your gums bleed every time, it would be forbidden; if sometimes they do and sometimes not, even most of the time, it is a davar sheeino mitkaven and permitted. The same for flossing. "There's no need to designate a Shabbos toothbrush."

With toothpaste. The concern is memareah, smoothing. In Yabia Omer, Hakham Ovadia permits it: smoothing is forbidden when you want the smooth surface, like sealing a barrel with wax, not when you have no interest in it and it lasts a moment. Many disagree, but Sefaradim following Hakham Ovadia may brush with toothpaste.

Danger to a limb. Asked about bee allergies, the Rabbi said any real chance of danger to life overrides Shabbat. In principle, danger only to a limb permits rabbinic acts but not a Torah prohibition. But today we are told that any significant loss of blood, even from a finger, is a danger to life, so in practice it is treated as such. He told of Rav Hayim of Brisk, who was very strict, yet told the sick to eat on Yom Kippur. Asked why, he said he was being consistent: "I'm mahmir in pikuah nefesh."

Dash: squeezing fruit

Squeezing olives and grapes is a Torah prohibition, a toladah of Dash. Other fruits commonly juiced, such as oranges, apples, strawberries and pomegranates, are rabbinic, because only wine and oil are true liquids, as their special berakhot show. Fruits not commonly squeezed may be squeezed. He recalled a class where he said melon juice was permitted, and his host had melon juice on the table; he still thought it permitted.

Lemons. The Beit Yosef saw people in Egypt squeezing lemons into water with sugar and gave two reasons it is permitted: lemon juice is never drunk alone; or it is squeezed directly into something else. The Mishnah Berurah follows the second, so Ashkenazim do not squeeze lemons except onto food. But the Shulhan Arukh (320) writes simply, "one may squeeze lemons", and Hazon Ovadia and others rule that he relied on the first reason.

Onto solid food all agree it is permitted, since the juice absorbed into food counts as food.

Ice, snow and melting fat

The Gemara forbids crushing snow or hail to get water, but allows putting it in a drink to melt. Rashi: it resembles creating something. The Rambam, who places it in the laws of Dash: it resembles extracting. The Sefer HaTerumah: it is nolad, a new thing created on Shabbat.

Ice cubes on the table. For Rashi and the Rambam, leaving ice to melt by itself is fine. For the Sefer HaTerumah it is a problem. The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam in several places, and in most of them the Rema is strict.

So Ashkenazim do not leave ice to melt, or put congealed fish sauce on the hot plate. Sefaradim may, since it melts by itself. Someone was told congealed gravy may melt "with conditions"; the Rabbi explained: when the melted liquid is absorbed into the food and not discernible, even the strict view permits. Making ice cubes on Shabbat is permitted for Sefaradim.

Practical summary

  • A biting insect on your skin may be trapped but not killed; spraying into the air to drive insects away is permitted.
  • One may flush the toilet even if insects will die, for several combined reasons.
  • Infested produce may be washed on Shabbat if there is no visible insect, though it is better done before Shabbat.
  • Brushing with water is permitted unless your gums always bleed; Hakham Ovadia permits toothpaste.
  • Sefaradim may squeeze lemons; Ashkenazim only onto food. Olives and grapes are a Torah prohibition.
  • Sefaradim may leave ice or fat to melt by itself; Ashkenazim avoid it unless it melts into food.
