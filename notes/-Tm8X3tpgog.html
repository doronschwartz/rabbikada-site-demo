The basics of kiddushin

The Rabbi set out to compare two teshuvot of Rav Moshe Feinstein, one on egalitarian weddings and one on Reform weddings, and to draw out a principle about how halacha is decided.

Every Jewish wedding has two parts: erusin, the betrothal, and nisuin, the marriage itself. They used to be about a year apart; today both happen under the huppah. Once a woman is betrothed (erusin, also called kiddushin), she needs a get to marry anyone else.

Kiddushin takes effect when the groom gives the bride something of monetary value and says that she is betrothed to him, in front of two kosher witnesses. For a few hundred years the established custom among Ashkenazim and Sefaradim alike has been to use a ring: "Harei at mekudeshet li." The bride's part is silent acceptance. Her consent is essential, and she shows it by holding out her finger. She can otherwise be entirely passive.

With egalitarianism on the rise, some brides ask: if he gives me a ring and speaks, why can I not give him a ring and speak too? The first posek to address this was Rav Moshe Feinstein, around 1970.

He must take her, not give himself

The Shulhan Arukh (Even HaEzer 27) rules that if a man says to a woman "I am your husband" or "I am your betrothed", nothing happens. The Torah says "when a man takes a wife" (Devarim 24:1). He must bring her to him, not himself to her.

The Pithei Teshuvah there quotes the Sefer HaMakneh, by the author of the Haflaah: if he says "you are my wife, and I am your husband", it works. He has said the essential words, "you are my wife". What he adds afterwards does not take that away. This point comes back later.

Why must it be the man? The Rabbi drew on an idea Rabbi Tatz has written about. In the Torah's view the masculine represents potential, the first burst of energy that starts a process. The feminine turns vision into reality. A child is conceived in a moment and developed by the mother over nine months. That is why Shlomo HaMelekh writes, "do not forsake the Torah of your mother" (Mishlei 1:8). Fathers may have taught the Torah, but it is the mother who makes it real in the home. A marriage too is begun by the man, and the two of them then nurture it together.

The first teshuvah: an Orthodox wedding

Rav Moshe (Even HaEzer 3:18) was asked about a wedding where this actually happened. The groom gave the ring and said the words. Then the bride gave him a ring and said something similar. Is she married?

Yes. At the moment he gave her the ring, in front of witnesses, the kiddushin took effect. What she did a few seconds later is, in Rav Moshe's words, divrei hevel, empty words that change nothing. So she would need a get.

But he forbade doing it in the first place, for two reasons:

  1. It is a practice of non-Jews (hukkot hagoyim). The Rabbi noted this is questionable, since many hold that hukkot hagoyim applies only to practices connected with idolatry, and exchanging rings is not.
  2. His main reason: if it becomes common, people will come to think that a woman can betroth a man by giving him a ring, or that both must give. The Gemara gives a similar reason why someone who has immersed in a mikveh should not then shower in drawn water: people might think the shower purified them.

Some may be uneasy with the second argument, he said, since whether we can make new decrees today is a separate question.

The second teshuvah: a Reform wedding

In another teshuvah (Even HaEzer 3:25) Rav Moshe was asked about a woman who married with a Reform rabbi in a Reform temple. She later became religious, received a civil divorce, never received a get, and now wished to marry an Orthodox man. Must she find her former husband for a get?

Rav Moshe ruled that she needs no get at all, not even as a stringency. First, the witnesses were very likely not kosher. But his main point was that even with kosher witnesses there was no kiddushin. Yes, he gave her a ring and said the words. But she also gave him a ring and said the same. That shows they were not intending kiddushin. It was an exchange of gifts.

Worse than a civil marriage

Then comes the part where "you fall off your chair". What about a Jewish couple who marry only civilly, live together for years, and divorce civilly? Rav Henkin held a get is required. Rav Moshe disagreed, but said that in the first instance we should try to obtain one. Batei Din across the world follow this: they try to arrange a get as a stringency, and if the husband cannot be found or refuses, they allow her to remarry, since strictly speaking it is not needed. The concern is that by living together openly they may have intended their relations to create kiddushin.

So why would a Reform wedding, followed by years together, need less? Rav Moshe answers that a couple with no ceremony at all may have meant their life together to serve as the marriage. A Reform couple believe their ceremony was the religious marriage. In his eyes that ceremony was nothing, and they "leave all the religion under the huppah". Everything afterwards is done on the strength of a ceremony that did not work. He compares this to a man who gave something he thought was worth a perutah, which turned out to be worth less: we do not say his later relations were meant as kiddushin, because he thought the ring had already done it. Rav Moshe adds that where there are children, one should not require a get.

Context decides

The same act, he gives her a ring and she gives him one, produces opposite results. In an Orthodox setting it is a valid kiddushin and her ring is irrelevant. In a Reform setting the whole thing is an exchange of gifts and no get is needed.

Rav Moshe was later asked whether his teshuvot contradict each other. He said no. The first was about a proper rabbi who did a proper kiddushin and then allowed an extra gesture. In a Reform wedding the mutual exchange is an essential part of the ceremony, so it is clear no kiddushin of the Torah is happening. "It's the context which tells you everything."

Someone suggested that the exchange of rings would make the kiddushin stronger. The Rabbi explained that it is the opposite. Where the denomination requires both to give, that shows it is not an act of kiddushin at all.

Conservative weddings

On Conservative weddings (Even HaEzer 4:13) Rav Moshe was uncertain. It could be a valid kiddushin with an irrelevant addition, or like a Reform exchange of gifts. The Rabbi noted a pattern in his teshuvot: anything Reform he rejected outright, including Reform conversions, but on Conservative he was more equivocal, because it depended on the rabbi and the community. In that case a get would seemingly be required as a stringency, out of doubt.

In practice

Rav Moshe also wrote that an Orthodox rabbi under heavy pressure, who might lose his position, may allow the bride to give a ring, provided he announces that the kiddushin takes effect through the groom's ring and hers is an extra gift. But in the first instance he did not want it done.

The common practice of Orthodox Batei Din today, the Rabbi said, is to tell couples who ask that she may give him a ring at the end of the ceremony or in the yihud room. Rav Moshe's concern is about doing it under the huppah, together with the kiddushin.

He then raised a suggestion he had seen, stressing that it is debatable and needs more rabbinic support. In many Sefaradi communities the kinyan for the ketubah is done under the huppah, between the kiddushin and the nisuin. The object used for that kinyan does not have to be the rabbi's pen. So after the kiddushin, the bride could hand the groom her ring and ask him to lift it and thereby accept the obligations of the ketubah. That is clearly not part of the kiddushin, so Rav Moshe's concern would be much smaller. "I'm not sure if it's correct. It needs further thought."

Practical summary

  • Kiddushin takes effect when the groom gives the ring and says the words before two kosher witnesses. A ring given by the bride does not betroth.
  • Rav Moshe forbade the bride giving a ring under the huppah, but if it happened at an Orthodox wedding the kiddushin is valid and a get is needed.
  • In his view a Reform double-ring ceremony is an exchange of gifts and needs no get, less even than a civil marriage. On Conservative weddings he was in doubt.
  • Batei Din today let the bride give a ring at the end of the ceremony or in the yihud room.
  • Using her ring for the ketubah kinyan is only a suggestion, which the Rabbi said needs further thought.
