Topic: The three oaths, the halachic answers to them, and the roots of Haredi opposition to Zionism Sources: The Ramban's additions to the Rambam's Sefer HaMisvot, Positive Misvah 4; Ketubot 110b-111a Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Wednesday 22 May 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why now?

Several people had asked at the previous gathering about Neturei Karta, Satmar and opposition to the State of Israel. The question had become sharper that week: the leaders of Neturei Karta had just issued a statement of grief at the death of Iran's president.

Neturei Karta is Aramaic for "guardians of the city": they see themselves as the true guardians of Jerusalem. They number a few thousand, mostly in America, with some in Stamford Hill and in Jerusalem. Dayan Kada's point was that they are the extreme and open edge of a position held quietly by much of the Hasidic world, Satmar being the largest Hasidic group, with hundreds of thousands of members. Much of the mainstream Haredi world is not hostile but apathetic, and Yom HaAsmaut often passes unmarked. The aim of the evening was to understand where this comes from and what the answers are.

Is there a misvah to live in the Land?

The Rambam, listing the 613 misvot, does not count living in Eres Yisrael. The Ramban, in his list of positive misvot the Rambam "forgot", says we are commanded to inherit the Land and not leave it in the hands of the nations or desolate: "You shall inherit the land and dwell in it, for I have given you the land" (Bemidbar 33:53). He also cites the Gemara: a person should live in Eres Yisrael even in a city mostly of non-Jews rather than abroad in a mostly Jewish city, because one who lives in the Land "is as one who has a God".

Why did the Rambam leave it out? A later Spanish commentator who wrote to defend the Rambam against the Ramban explained that the Rambam counts only misvot binding for all generations, and settling the Land is a misvah only when the Temple stands. His proof is the source on which, Dayan Kada said, the whole anti-Zionist philosophy rests, and which the late Satmar Rebbe, Rav Yoel, made central: the three oaths.

The three oaths

A verse in Shir HaShirim, "I adjure you", appears three times. The Gemara in Ketubot reads it as three oaths for the time of exile:

  1. Israel should not go up to the Land as a wall, by force, and conquer it.
  2. Israel should not rebel against the nations among whom they live.
  3. The nations should not oppress Israel excessively.

The second oath, he noted, is why Jews pray for the welfare of their country and why the national flag is always carried beside the Israeli flag at rallies abroad. And no one, including Neturei Karta, forbids individuals from living in the Land. Many of the families of Meah Shearim have been there for centuries. Their claim is that one may live there as a subject of whoever rules, Ottoman, British or other, but not create a state or an army.

Three answers

A broken oath. The laws of oaths state that when two parties make a pact and one breaks it, the other is released. The nations swore not to oppress Israel excessively; after the pogroms, Hebron in 1929 and the Holocaust, our side no longer binds. The class debated whether this works when the oath was made with Hashem; Dayan Kada answered that the context shows the oaths are between the two sides.

Aggadah is not halacha. More fundamentally, this is a homiletic passage, not a legal oath. "Never draw out legal principles from aggadah." Building a whole halachic way of life against the State on one aggadic passage is not legitimate.

Not by force. He read from a rabbi writing in 1921, after the San Remo conference, that now that the nations had granted the Land to the Jewish people, "the fear of the oaths has been removed", and settling the Land is a misvah equal to all the misvot. The more so in 1948, when the United Nations recognised the State. It was done with permission, not by force.

"From a purely dry halachic perspective, the arguments against the three oaths are overwhelmingly convincing." He added that the argument from international recognition does not by itself cover the settlements, which lack that recognition, and which is why they are more controversial halachically, though historically the land is ours.

Why the opposition, then?

Zionism arose from secular leaders such as Herzl. Rabbis in Europe were pained that something as holy as settling the Land was being driven by people with no interest in Torah, and, Dayan Kada said, they then found flimsy sources to justify an objection that was really ideological. He quoted the Lubavitcher Rebbe of the time, who wrote that the Zionists' aim was to persuade the people that Torah and misvot exist merely to strengthen national feeling.

Rav Kook, Chief Rabbi in Mandate Palestine, answered his colleagues in Europe. Look past the secular pioneer paving the roads, he said, to the holy spark within him; he is doing Hashem's will even without knowing it. The prophets promised the ingathering, and just as no one waits at home for Hashem to send a livelihood without effort, so the return requires effort. And if the religious stayed out, the enterprise would remain secular; why not join in?

Over time much changed. The Haredi parties, Shas and the Ashkenazi parties, now take part in elections, while Satmar and Neturei Karta still do not vote. Sefaradim, who came from the Middle East and were not part of the European battles, were always far more sympathetic.

Today

In 2024, Dayan Kada said, the opposition makes no sense. Which other state pays people to study Torah? There has never been a time in Jewish history with so much Torah learned in Eres Yisrael. "You have to be blind not to see the miracles of Hashem" over the last 76 years. Mashiah, he reminded the class, is not the World to Come but a time in this world when Israel is sovereign and recognised by the nations, and he sees the present as the beginning of the redemption. The first covenant, with Avraham, was over the Land, before the covenant of Torah at Sinai.

As for Neturei Karta standing with those who praised 7 October, even Satmar has disowned them, and it is a great hillul HaShem. The root problem, he said, is taking one idea and making it everything. Judaism is a tapestry, as Rabbi Sacks put it; focus on a single brushstroke and you lose the picture. That is where religious extremism comes from.

He concluded that the great majority of contemporary poskim hold there is a misvah to inherit, live in and cultivate the Land, and that the many who volunteered on farms after 7 October were fulfilling it.

Summary

  • The Ramban counts living in Eres Yisrael as a misvah; the Rambam's omission is explained by some as because it does not apply in exile, based on the three oaths.
  • The answers: the nations broke their oath, the oaths are aggadah and not halacha, and the State was established with international permission.
  • Dayan Kada sees the halachic case against the State as very weak; the real roots of the opposition were Zionism's secular origins.
  • Rav Kook urged seeing the holy spark in the secular pioneers and joining the enterprise.
  • Today he sees no justification for the opposition, and holds that settling and defending the Land is a misvah.
