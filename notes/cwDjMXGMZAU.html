These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Mukseh because of a prohibition

Before Hanukkah the class went through the kinds of mukseh. A quick reminder:

  • Kli shemelakhto leisur: a utensil whose main use is forbidden on Shabbat, like a hammer or a pen. The lightest kind. It may be moved for a permitted use (a hammer to crack nuts) or because you need its place.
  • Mukseh mahmat gufo: something with no use at all, like stones or money.
  • Mukseh mahmat hisaron kis: an expensive item you are careful never to use for anything else.
  • Mukseh mahmat isur: mukseh because of a prohibition connected to it. The Gemara's example is clothes that were wet when Shabbat began, since you might wring them out.

Shabbat candles are in the last group. The flame, and what directly holds it (in those days a glass of oil with a wick), are mukseh mahmat isur, because the Hakhamim feared you would put out or adjust the flame. This is a severe mukseh: it may not be moved for any purpose, only in an unusual way (with a shinui) or indirectly.

The base (basis). Whatever holds up a mukseh item takes on its status. The candlesticks hold the glass, so they are mukseh. The table holds the candlesticks, so if they are the only thing on it during twilight, the table is mukseh for the whole of Shabbat. Even if the candlesticks are knocked off later, the table stays mukseh, because whatever is mukseh when Shabbat comes in stays mukseh all Shabbat.

A table is only a base if the mukseh is alone on it, or is the most important thing on it. Importance means usefulness for Shabbat, not price. Bread or wine for Shabbat outweigh silver candlesticks worth a thousand pounds, because on Shabbat you need the bread and wine and only need the flame.

The Rabbi's example: in the drawer by his bed he keeps some cash and the book he reads before sleeping. He may open the drawer, because the book is what matters to him on Shabbat, so the drawer is not a base for the money, and moving the money indirectly is permitted anyway.

Someone asked about phones. A phone may be a kli shemelakhto leisur, which may be picked up and put down normally; some argue it is valuable mukseh, which you would have to nudge with your elbow. Phones are used constantly, which argues against special care; on the other hand we protect them with cases and screen covers. He set out both sides.

Seif 1: the lamp stays mukseh all Shabbat

A lamp lit for Shabbat may not be moved even after it has gone out. The leftover oil may not be used either, not even for your salad. The Mishnah Berurah adds that even if no oil is left, the lamp and wick were a base for the flame when Shabbat began, so they are mukseh for the whole day.

Seif 2: no moving it for its place or use

Seif 2 rejects an opinion that would allow moving the lamp when you need its place or want to use it. Only a kli shemelakhto leisur may be moved for those reasons.

Something that bothers you. The Rema adds that a sensitive person who is bothered by the smell of the lamp may take it out. This is a general rule: mukseh that is offensive may be removed. The Gemara's example is excrement, today a dirty nappy, which may be taken to the bin. But if it is in a room nobody will use on Shabbat, with the door closed, it does not bother anyone and must be left.

Bread, and a stipulation (seifim 3-4)

Putting bread on the candlesticks during Shabbat to make them movable does not help; it is too late. If the bread was there before Shabbat, some allow moving the candlesticks once the candles have gone out, because the bread is the more important item.

That is what we hold: to stop something becoming a base, it is enough to have something useful on it, even if the mukseh is worth much more. Food, wine, and in one family's case the salt on the tray, all work.

The other way is a stipulation (tenai) before Shabbat: "I intend to move the candlesticks once they go out." That works, because it is in your control. You do not need both; either one is enough. While they are burning they may never be moved.

Seif 5: a lamp lit on Shabbat

If a lamp was lit on Shabbat for someone dangerously ill or for a woman giving birth, then once it has gone out it may be moved. Something only becomes a base if it was holding mukseh through the whole twilight period at the start of Shabbat, about half an hour. If the candlesticks were taken off the table before sunset, the table never becomes mukseh.

Siman 280: Friday night in a marriage

The Shulhan Arukh says marital relations are part of the pleasure of Shabbat, and that for healthy Talmidei Hakhamim the set time is Friday night. The Gemara gives different frequencies according to a man's occupation, and this is a misvah towards one's wife. One of Ezra's ten enactments, listed in the seventh chapter of Bava Kamma, was to eat garlic on Friday; the Mishnah Berurah explains why, and the Kaf HaHayim notes it does not apply when the wife is niddah.

The Mishnah Berurah adds that a husband should show his wife more affection that day and avoid any argument: "It's not just the act, it's the build-up." Seif 2 permits the first time after a wedding to be on Friday night. The Mishnah Berurah explains that this is not considered wounding on Shabbat. The Rabbi added that wiping oneself afterwards is not the melakhah of colouring, since it is an act of cleaning, not of dyeing.

Siman 281: Shabbat morning

The Shulhan Arukh now moves to Shabbat morning. In Nishmat one should not bow at the words "to You alone we give thanks." One bows only where the Hakhamim instituted bowing, and does not add extra bows. Full prostration on a stone floor is forbidden outside the Beit HaMikdash, which is why some put down a rug.

The Rema adds that it is customary to start Shabbat morning prayers later than on weekdays. The source is the Mordekhai, a German Rishon: the daily morning offering is described as "on the day," implying a little later. The Rabbi noted there is no Talmudic source. In his view the Mordekhai found a good justification for what people were already doing, and it became custom. The Rema often records what communities do, while the Shulhan Arukh sets out the halacha as it should be. In the Gateshead yeshivah of his youth, Shahrit was at eight o'clock both on weekdays and on Shabbat.

Practical summary

  • Candles, their holders, and a table holding only them at the start of Shabbat are mukseh all Shabbat, even after the candles go out.
  • Put bread, wine or another useful item on the table or tray before Shabbat, or make a stipulation that you will move the candlesticks once they go out.
  • Never move candlesticks while the candles are burning.
  • A lamp lit on Shabbat for a medical emergency may be moved once it goes out.
  • Offensive mukseh, like a dirty nappy, may be removed if it bothers you.
  • In Nishmat and elsewhere, bow only where the Hakhamim instituted.
