These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The last class before the break

This class closes the series for the summer. The aim of the year was to go through the Shemonah Perakim and then see them at work in Pirkei Avot, and Chapter 5 is where the Rambam gathers many of those ideas. The first Mishnayot are factual lists, so the Rabbi went through them quickly. The second half is about middot, character, and there he slowed down.

The tens

The chapter opens with a run of tens. The world was created with ten utterances, though one would have sufficed, so that the wicked who destroy it are punished and the righteous who sustain it are rewarded. The Rambam's lesson is that each of us is the purpose of creation. As the Mishnah in Kiddushin says, a person should see the world as hanging on his own actions.

There were ten generations from Adam to Noah and ten from Noah to Avraham, which shows God's patience. Avraham did not invent monotheism, the Rabbi added. It existed from the beginning, was forgotten, and Avraham re-established it. Avraham was tested ten times. Rashi includes the fire of Ur Kasdim, a Midrash, but the Rambam lists only tests explicit in the Torah, from leaving his land to the Akedah.

Ten miracles were done in Egypt and ten at the sea. For the sea the Torah says little, so the Rambam turns to the Midrash: the water arched over them like a roof, the ground was firm for Israel and mud for the Egyptians, there were twelve paths with transparent walls, and sweet water could be drunk from them. Our ancestors also tested God ten times in the desert, from "are there not enough graves in Egypt?" to the golden calf, the spies and Korah. Ten miracles happened in the Beit HaMikdash, such as meat that never spoiled, no flies, and smoke that rose straight in any wind. The Rabbi pointed out that the Mishnah counts it a miracle that nobody ever said he could not find lodging, or that it was too crowded or expensive.

Ten things created at twilight

The Mishnah lists ten things made at twilight on the first Friday, among them the mouth of the earth that swallowed Korah, the well, the mouth of the donkey (this week's parashah), the rainbow and the manna.

The Rambam repeats what he said in the Shemonah Perakim. God does not keep performing new miracles. At creation He placed into nature everything that would ever happen, so the splitting of the sea was programmed into the sea on the third day, to occur two thousand years later. This does not mean God sits back: the Rambam firmly holds that He oversees and sustains the world constantly. The Rabbi noted that the Ramchal sees a miracle as a fresh decision to intervene, and that the Mishnah's wording fits that more naturally.

Why, then, only these ten? The Rambam answers that these were the last things of creation, while the other miracles were set in nature earlier: water was told on the second day that it would split for Yehoshua, Eliyahu and Elisha, and the sun was told on the fourth day to turn until it would stand still for Yehoshua.

Five kinds of people

Now the chapter turns to character. The Mishnah speaks of seven traits of a golem and seven of a hakham, so the Rambam first defines five terms. Behind them is his central idea: we have two things to develop, the intellect and the character, and they are equally important. - Bur. Like a ploughed field with nothing growing. He has developed neither. The Rambam adds that he is not wicked, just empty. - Am HaAres. Some character and manners but no intellect. He is good for society. - Golem. Both intellect and character, but unfinished and mixed up, like a knife that was made but never sharpened. As the verse says, "golmi ra-u einekha": before the soul was breathed into us we were an unformed lump. - Hakham. Both, in balance. - Hasid. The surprise. A hasid is not simply "pious". The word means one who goes beyond the middle path. Since it is hard to stay exactly at fifty-fifty, one leans a little to the better side, as a safeguard. The Rambam says any exaggeration is called hesed, for good or for bad. The Rabbi's proof is the Torah's use of hesed for a brother and sister: kindness with no boundaries has gone too far. So there are good and bad hasidim.

The seven marks of the hakham

Four are ethical: 1. He does not speak in front of someone wiser. 2. He does not interrupt. Many people in a conversation are only waiting for you to finish, the Rabbi said, and that is not listening. 3. He does not pretend to know what he does not. He says "I do not know". 4. He accepts the truth. It is rare to hear someone in a debate say "that is a good point, I had not thought of it".

Three are intellectual: 1. He is not rushed. Rashi reads this as not answering too fast. The Rambam reads it the other way: when someone tries to trap you with a weak argument, you do not panic, you spot the flaw and answer at once. 2. He asks and answers on the point, in the right field. Do not answer a question about logic with a scientific proof. 3. He keeps order, "the first first and the last last". People are often busy with what is urgent and never get to what is important.

Four types, and the extremes

The next Mishnayot give lists of four: "mine is mine and yours is yours", slow or quick to anger, slow or quick to learn, those who give charity, those who go to the synagogue. "Mine is yours and yours is mine" is an am haares, good socially but naive (he likened it to socialism). "Mine is yours and yours is yours" is a hasid, going beyond the line. "Mine is mine and yours is mine" is a rasha.

Of the student who is slow to understand and quick to forget, the Mishnah does not say rasha but "a bad portion". The Rambam links this to the Shemonah Perakim: our choices are in our hands, but whether we are born with a quick mind is not. Such a person has done nothing wrong and is not culpable, only unfortunate. The Rabbi stressed that the Rambam does not say he cannot grow. Anyone can develop his intellect, even if it is harder.

Then the Rambam adds something new. Extremes in both directions are wrong, but they do not carry the same name. The person who chases every pleasure is a rasha, the worse extreme, because it is much harder to climb out of. The person who cuts himself off from all physical pleasure is called a hote, a sinner, meaning he is doing something incomplete. The Rabbi found this counterintuitive, but it is a sin not to enjoy the pleasures of this world. The same holds for courage and for anger.

The class then revised the terms. The worst kind, the Rambam says, is the clever person who uses his intellect against his own character. Someone with every virtue is extremely rare but possible. He is called a man of God, and this is why Moshe is on a level of his own among the prophets (Shemonah Perakim, Chapter 7).

Learners, and love that lasts

Students are a sponge, a funnel, a strainer that keeps the dregs and remembers only the jokes, or a sieve that keeps the fine flour and lets the chaff go.

The Mishnah says love that depends on something ends when the thing ends, like the love of Amnon and Tamar, while love that depends on nothing lasts, like that of David and Yehonatan. The Rambam believes no one loves without a reason. So he reads it this way: if the basis of the relationship is itself lasting, such as helping each other grow in character and wisdom or working together for the community, the relationship lasts. Love resting only on the physical goes when people age.

Avraham's students and Bilam's

The last Mishnah of the class, linked to the week's parashah: whoever has a good eye, a humble spirit and a restrained soul is of Avraham's students; whoever has an evil eye, arrogance and a broad appetite is of Bilam's. The proof for Bilam's greed is that he said that even if Balak gave him his house full of silver and gold, he could only do what he was told.

A question arises from the next chapter, where a rabbi refuses even all the precious stones and pearls in the world to leave a place of Torah, and is praised. The Rabbi's answer is that Bilam said "his silver and his gold": he wanted what belonged to another. That is the evil eye, always wanting what others have, the source of despondency. It is good to think big, but not at the expense of others.

Summary

  • The tens teach that every person's actions affect the world. The Rambam holds that all miracles were built into nature at creation, while God still oversees it constantly.
  • Five kinds of people: bur, am haares, golem, hakham and hasid. Intellect and character are equally important, and hasid means one who goes beyond the middle, for good or bad.
  • The hakham has four ethical marks (not speaking before the wiser, not interrupting, admitting ignorance, accepting the truth) and three intellectual ones (not panicking, staying on the point, keeping order).
  • Extremes are wrong both ways: the indulgent person is a rasha, the one who denies himself all pleasure a hote. A slow mind is an unfortunate lot, not a fault, and can still be developed.
  • Love that rests on something lasting lasts, and Avraham's student has a good eye, a humble spirit and a restrained soul.
