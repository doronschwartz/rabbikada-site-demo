The Torah's order of inheritance

The Rabbi admitted this was "a slightly morbid" subject, and one people avoid. But there are two things nobody escapes, taxes and death, and anyone with something to leave the next generation needs to know how to pass it on correctly, both halachically and legally.

The source is in Parashat Pinehas. The daughters of Selofhad come to Moshe: our father died without a son; why should his name be lost? Moshe, in his humility, says he does not know and asks God, who agrees: "the daughters of Selofhad speak rightly." God then sets out the general law: if a man dies without a son, his inheritance goes to his daughter; if no daughter, to his brothers; then to his father's brothers; then to the nearest relative.

From the Mishnah and Gemara in Bava Batra, the full order is:

  • A husband inherits his wife.
  • Then sons, and anyone descended from a son, who stands in his place. So a granddaughter through a son who died comes before the deceased's own daughter.
  • Then daughters and their descendants.
  • Then the father, and those who come through him: his other sons, then the grandfather, and so on upward.

So in practice, if a person leaves sons and daughters, the daughters receive nothing. The second legal passage, in Parashat Ki Tesse, adds pi shenayim: the firstborn son receives a double portion. With two sons, the firstborn takes two thirds and the other one third. The Rambam (Hilchot Nahalot 6:1) rules that this cannot be changed by a condition.

The Rabbi admitted that this is hard for us to accept. A Midrash has the daughters of Selofhad say that human beings favour males, but God's mercy is for males and females alike, and God agrees, though only where there are no sons. He also noted that both legal passages arise from strife, which shows how important it is to get these laws right. He explained them against the background of a society in which a husband supported his wife, so the men, who had to provide, received the inheritance.

Three enactments of the Hakhamim

The Hakhamim themselves felt the difficulty and made three enactments for daughters:

  1. Maintenance. The sons inherit, but the estate must support the unmarried daughters. And where the estate is small, the Mishnah in Ketubot rules that the daughters are maintained first, even if the sons are left to go begging. Admon protested, "because I am a male, I lose out?", but the halacha does not follow him.
  2. What a wife brings into the marriage. Property she brings as nikhsei melog remains hers: the husband may enjoy its income, but he does not own it and bears no responsibility for it. This lets a daughter hold and control assets of her own.
  3. Ketubat benin dikhrin. A husband inherits his wife. If she dies and he remarries and has more children, the assets she brought in would be divided among children she never knew. So the Hakhamim enacted that whatever is left from her property goes to her own children. Otherwise, the Gemara explains, fathers would be reluctant to give their daughters a dowry.

Someone asked whether the husband can spend that property in the meantime. Yes: only what remains, for instance a property itself, passes on. "There's nothing foolproof in life," the Rabbi said, but these enactments show that the Hakhamim saw the problem. He was careful to add that they were not making the system equal; the Gemara and Yerushalmi are clear that daughters inherit only where there are no sons.

The question in later generations

The Radbaz, the prolific sixteenth-century Egyptian posek, was asked how this can be fair. He answered that the Torah trusts people: the brothers will give their sisters what they need.

Rav Kook gave a similar answer: the Torah is like nature. The sun is good even though some suffer sunstroke, and we do not abolish the sun. But Rav Yehiel Mikhel Tukachinsky, author of the Gesher HaHayim, wrote of a righteous man who insisted on following the Torah's order exactly, and bequeathed only hatred and division to his children. Rav Herzog, on the other hand, recognised at the founding of the State of Israel that something had to give; in the end, the Rabbi noted, Israeli law did not adopt halachic financial law.

How to make a will work

The Rabbi stressed that this is general guidance: consult your rabbi, a lawyer in your jurisdiction and probably a tax adviser too. Two principles make an ordinary civil will a problem:

  • There is a prohibition against diverting an inheritance from its rightful heir, "even from a bad son to a good one, and all the more so from a son to a daughter". A daughter who takes more than her halachic share through a civil will may be holding property that halachically is not hers.
  • No acquisition can take effect after death. A civil will takes effect the moment after death, so in halacha it achieves nothing.

He set out five approaches:

  1. Rav Moshe Feinstein's view. "The law of the land is the law" cannot override the Torah. But Rav Moshe argued that a kinyan's role is to show firm intent, and signing a will before lawyers shows that intent before death. This is not the accepted practice, but worth knowing.
  2. Matenat shekhiv mera, the gift of someone on his deathbed. Said before two witnesses, it works. If he gave away everything and then recovered, he cannot take it back.
  3. Giving everything away in your lifetime. It works, but the Hakhamim were not happy with it, and it is dangerous: you lose control. He told of an elderly man who divided everything equally among his children while still alive, and was found crying in shul two weeks later because none of them had visited since.
  4. A gift taking effect one moment before death. This is the most common method. It is then a gift, not an inheritance. Its weakness: you cannot give what does not yet exist, so anything acquired after signing still follows the Torah's order.
  5. Shtar hasi zakhar, recorded by the Rema. The father takes on a very large debt to his daughter, secured on his estate, which is cancelled if the sons give her the share he specifies. It covers everything owned at death, and can also deal with the double portion.

In practice, he said, a halachic will usually combines the fourth and fifth methods, alongside a civil will. If some feel uncomfortable "getting around" Torah law, he compared it to the sale of hames: poskim in every generation have found creative ways to apply the Torah to the realities of life. "Torah is dynamic... not, has veshalom, to be changed, but to be applied in each and every generation."

Finally he explained kim li: in monetary cases, the one holding the money can say "I follow the minority opinion", because the claimant must prove his case. So even a plain civil will may be arguable before a Beit Din, depending on who is in possession.

Practical summary

  • By Torah law sons inherit before daughters, and a firstborn son takes a double portion; this cannot be changed by a simple condition.
  • The Hakhamim protected daughters through maintenance, protected marital property and ketubat benin dikhrin.
  • A civil will alone takes effect after death, which does not work in halacha.
  • The usual solution combines a gift taking effect a moment before death with a shtar hasi zakhar, alongside a civil will.
  • Take advice from a rabbi, a lawyer and a tax adviser before writing a will.
