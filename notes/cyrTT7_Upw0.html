Back to the principle that was skipped

The class had already reached principle 10, providence, the week before. Principle 9 had been postponed, so the Rabbi went back to it before moving on to principle 11.

He began with a lockdown announcement. Firstborns would not fast on Erev Pesah: he would make a siyum on Zoom, and the poskim he had consulted agreed that joining a siyum virtually is enough, even though a virtual minyan is not. Nobody should fast this year, as fasting weakens the immune system.

Principles 1 to 5 are about God, 10 to 13 about reward and punishment, and 6 to 9 about revelation: prophecy, Moshe, the Torah from heaven, and now the Torah that does not change.

Two parts to the principle

The Rambam writes that the Torah of Moshe will never be abrogated, no other law will come from God, and nothing will be added to it or taken from it, in the written or the oral law, as the verse says, "Do not add to it and do not take away from it."

The Rabbi drew out two elements: 1. Judaism will never be replaced by another religion or "testament". 2. Within the Torah itself, nothing will change.

Someone could accept the first and still want to change laws inside the Torah; the Rambam rules out both. The Ani Maamin and the Yigdal ("God will not change His law for any other, for ever") mainly express the first.

Part one: the "new covenant"

Few people today struggle to choose between Judaism and another faith, he said, but it is worth knowing the answer to the best-known proof text.

Yirmiyahu (chapter 31) says, "A time is coming, says Hashem, when I will make a new covenant (brit hadashah) with the house of Israel and the house of Yehudah." This is where the term "New Testament" comes from. His answers: - Yehezkel (chapter 16) and the end of the curses in Vayikra say the opposite: God will never reject the original covenant. Verses must be read in context. - The verse speaks of a deeper commitment, Torah "written on their hearts", not the Torah being annulled. - It is made with "the house of Israel and the house of Yehudah", the ten tribes and the two reunited. That kingdom had been split for centuries before Christianity, and the tribes have still not returned. - It says no one will need to teach his neighbour, because everyone will know God. That clearly did not happen 2,000 years ago. - If God revealed Himself to a whole nation to give the Torah, why would He change it through one person's private vision?

The verses describe the end of days, a renewal of the same covenant. So we should not call our Torah the "Old Testament"; "Bible" is fine.

He then played "spot the difference" with two passages, both by Rabbi Sacks. The first, from the 2002 edition of The Dignity of Difference, said that God has spoken to mankind through Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and that no single faith is the faith of all humanity. It drew sharp criticism and was revised. The second says God made a covenant with one people, while other peoples may find their relationship with God within the framework of the Noahide laws. We do not ask other nations to convert, he said, but we cannot say that God spoke through other religions. Someone suggested the first version was written for a general readership; the Rabbi agreed Rabbi Sacks wrote as an Orthodox rabbi, but said one cannot dilute one's beliefs to reach an audience.

Part two: nothing changes within the Torah

Its values never change; our attitudes and the way we apply them can. Torah comes from lehorot, to instruct, and must speak to every generation. This is where Reform and Conservative Judaism depart: they keep what makes sense and set aside the rest. We look for a taam, a reason (the word also means taste), but we do first and then try to understand.

The Rambam's scriptural source (Hilchot Yesodei HaTorah 9) is "do not add and do not take away" and "it is not in heaven": a prophet who claims God sent him to add or remove a misvah, or says a misvah was only for a limited time, is a false prophet.

Rabbi Yosef Albo, in the Sefer HaIkkarim, offers three reasons the Torah should not change (God does not change, a nation does not change, truth does not change) and then questions them. Adam was only allowed plants, while Noah was allowed meat. Like a doctor changing a prescription as the patient changes, God could change the Torah. But it would take a prophet greater than Moshe, which principle 7 rules out, or a revelation greater than Sinai. So Albo saw no need to make immutability a fundamental principle. The Rambam reads "do not add" as saying change is impossible; Albo reads it as saying that we cannot change it.

Part three: in the times of Mashiah

Some sources suggest change. The Gemara in Kiddushin says mamzerim will become pure in the future. A Midrash on Tehillim 146 reads matir asurim as "He permits the forbidden", including forbidden foods. Another Midrash says all offerings will cease except the thanksgiving offering.

Historians argue that Shabtai Sevi exploited such sources when his followers abandoned the Torah. "Imagine a great rabbi now declares himself to be Mashiah and points to this Midrash."

So the Rambam may mean the Torah does not change in our times, but he did not say so openly because Judaism was under attack from Christianity and Islam, as in the Ramban's disputation with Pablo Christiani in 1263. In Hilchot Teshuvah (chapter 3), among those who deny the Torah, he lists one who says God has replaced a misvah, in the past tense. He says nothing about the future, perhaps leaving that open.

Part four: how can the Rabbis add?

If adding is forbidden, how could the Hakhamim institute Hanukkah, washing before bread or the second day of Yom Tov? Rashi and the Raavad hold "do not add" means adding within a misvah: a fifth species to the lulav or a fifth parchment in tefillin. A new institution is not an addition.

The Rambam disagrees (Hilchot Mamrim 2). A court may forbid what is permitted, even for all generations, as long as it says this is a safeguard and not Torah law. By Torah law, meat and milk is forbidden for domestic and wild animals but not for poultry. A court that says poultry is included in the Torah prohibition is adding. A court that says "poultry is permitted by the Torah, but we forbid it as a fence" is not.

His illustration was Adam and Hava. God forbade eating from the tree; Adam told Hava not even to touch it, without saying the extra part was his own. She touched it, nothing happened, and she ate. Make a fence, he said, but say it is a fence.

Asked whether a rabbi today may call a rabbinic law Torah law, he said no: by the Rambam that transgresses "do not add".

His last point was laws that seem irrelevant, such as slavery. The value behind them, that the powerful may not exploit the weak, still applies to employers and employees.

Questions from the class

  • Why not a one-day Seder, at least this year? The second day is an enactment that only a greater court, a Sanhedrin, could annul; the Gemara itself asks why we keep it once the calendar was fixed. "Many things will change due to corona. One thing that will not change until Mashiah comes... second day Yom Tov."
  • A Zoom minyan? The ten must be together in one place. Rav Eliezer Melamed allows Kaddish over Zoom, but almost all poskim agree that the repetition and Kedushah cannot be done this way. Neighbours on balconies in one street are a different question.
  • The Hebrew birthday? There is no source for special powers on it from before about 200 years ago, mainly in Hasidic texts. The only birthday in Tanakh is Pharaoh's, though the Ben Ish Hai writes of making a meal.

Summary

  • Principle 9 has two parts: the Torah will never be replaced, and nothing within it will change.
  • The "new covenant" of Yirmiyahu speaks of the end of days and the reunited tribes, not a new religion.
  • The Rambam holds change impossible; Rabbi Yosef Albo holds it possible in theory, given a revelation greater than Sinai.
  • Some sources suggest change in the times of Mashiah, which the Rambam may have left unsaid.
  • The Rabbis may add fences, but only if they say clearly that they are fences and not Torah law.
