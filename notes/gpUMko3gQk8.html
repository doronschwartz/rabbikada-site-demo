Topic: The three judges of a conversion, Reform and Conservative conversions, and how a woman immerses before a Beit Din Source: Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268 Shiur: Fourth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, Wednesday 9 June 2021

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Review and plan

The Rabbi recalled the ground covered so far: milah and tevilah, the preparation of the candidate, and kabbalat misvot, which requires accepting all the misvot but not being told them all, and which is judged at the time of conversion.

Tonight's plan was to ask how many people are needed, and of what standing, for each stage; then to apply this to Reform and Conservative conversions, which any Beit Din meets regularly in ketubot and gittin; and then to explain how a woman immerses.

Three for the conversion

The Gemara in Yevamot (46b) says a ger needs three, because the Torah writes "one mishpat shall be for you and for the ger," and a mishpat is a judgment. Yet nearby the Gemara suggests that if a woman is seen going to the mikveh every month, she may be presumed Jewish, which seems to need no three at all.

Tosafot answer that three are essential, even after the fact, only for kabbalat misvot. For milah and tevilah, three are needed lekhathilah, but bediavad the conversion stands without them. Tosafot add that a woman does not immerse in front of a man, only in front of a woman attendant.

The Rif and the Rambam hold that three are needed for milah, tevilah and kabbalat misvot alike, and without them there is no conversion. The mikveh in the Gemara proves nothing about conversion. It shows that the person lives a Jewish life, which is enough to accept the children into the community, but the person himself needs proof: a certificate, witnesses, or the Beit Din that converted him.

The Shulhan Arukh brings the view of Tosafot without names and then the Rif and the Rambam by name. Normally when the Shulhan Arukh writes an unnamed view followed by "some say," the halacha follows the first. Someone asked whether it is not strange to follow Tosafot against the Rif and the Rambam. "A hundred percent," the Rabbi answered. That is exactly the question Rav Ovadia Yosef raises, as we will see below.

What kind of three

The Gemara speaks of talmidei hakhamim. The Shulhan Arukh itself only says three; the bracketed words in the printed text are not from Maran or the Rema. In practice, batei din across the world do not examine the knowledge of the third person. There are no truly ordained dayanim or rabbis in the classical sense today anyway: "I don't want to put you off your semikhah programme." The more learned the three the better, but there is no fixed threshold.

Reform and Conservative conversions

Someone suggested that the problem with a Reform conversion is the kabbalat misvot. The Rabbi explained that this is not the main problem: a person who accepts what he is taught in a Reform temple, believing it to be the Torah, may have accepted the misvot as he understands them (though, he agreed when asked, one might draw the line there). The main issue is the three.

Reform. Rav Moshe Feinstein rules consistently, in many teshuvot, that a Reform conversion is nothing at all. The three are not fit to sit as a Beit Din because they are kofrim, heretics. No witnesses are needed to establish this: the very fact of being a minister in a Reform temple makes the presumption. He goes further. A woman converted this way who married in a Reform setting should not be given a get even as a stringency, because that would lend validity to the conversion. The Rabbi compared it to a judge: you cannot sit on the bench unless you are sworn to uphold the law you are applying. "You can have an anarchist lawyer, but you can't have an anarchist judge."

Conservative. Here Rav Moshe was more equivocal, and the Rabbi explained the historical background. Fifty years ago it was common for yeshiva-trained Orthodox rabbis to serve in Conservative synagogues in America. Many ordinary members were, in his phrase, "light Orthodox," much like many communities under the Orthodox umbrella in England today. So:

  • In one teshuvah (Yoreh Deah 1:160) Rav Moshe treats a Conservative Beit Din as presumed kofrim, unfit to convert.
  • In another, about a get signed by a rabbi ordained at a Conservative seminary who prayed and put on tefillin daily but carried on Shabbat, he was unsure. Sinful actions need witnesses, and a person cannot testify against himself. Heresy does not need witnesses, but training at such a seminary might have been only a way to earn a living. He left the question open on that point.
  • Asked whether a Conservative group may use an Orthodox mikveh for conversions, he advised declining if possible; otherwise to send three Orthodox rabbis, which would turn it into a valid conversion. If the mikveh was built with communal funds that included them, they cannot be refused.

Rav Moshe's ruling

Reform conversion

Nothing at all; no get even as a stringency

Conservative, in his day

Presumed invalid, but he was sometimes doubtful and looked at the individuals

Conservative today (the Rabbi's view)

Default is invalid; a quick check on the rabbis involved is still advisable

The Rabbi explained that the Conservative movement has changed greatly in the last fifteen to twenty years, and it is now very rare to find a Conservative rabbi who is both fully observant and free of kefirah. That is why, in his view, it is hard to rely on Rav Moshe's doubts today. In conclusion: "Reform is nothing; Conservative, just do a quick check. But generally speaking, it's nothing."

Asked whether observance alone decides, the Rabbi said that clear evidence that a person is an apikoros disqualifies him even if he keeps the misvot.

Immersing at night or on Shabbat

Briefly, from the Shulhan Arukh: a conversion is not done at night or on Shabbat in the first instance, but if it was, it is valid.

How a woman immerses

For a man the procedure is straightforward: three for the milah, three for the kabbalat misvot, and the dayanim watch the immersion. The Shulhan Arukh, quoting the Rambam, describes a woman's immersion: another woman seats her in the water up to her neck, the dayanim stand by and tell her some of the misvot, she immerses before them, and then they turn and leave so as not to see her as she comes out. She makes the berakhah afterwards, a classic case of a berakhah said after the misvah.

Rav Ovadia Yosef (Yabia Omer, Yoreh Deah 1:19) wrote about this when he was in Egypt, where he saw the dayanim stay outside while the woman immersed before one Jewish woman. Rav Bension Uziel had permitted this, relying on the unnamed view of the Shulhan Arukh, which follows Tosafot. Rav Ovadia rejected that. It is inconceivable, he wrote, that Maran would rule like Tosafot against the Rif and the Rambam; since he named them, he holds like them, and three are needed for the tevilah too.

His solution, which the Rabbi said lies behind much of today's practice:

  1. The woman immerses wearing a very loose robe that does not cling to her body, so it is not a hasisah. The dayanim enter while she is in the water, tell her the misvot, and she immerses before them; and they see that all her hair goes under.
  2. The dayanim leave. The attendant helps her remove the robe and she immerses again without it, while the dayanim wait just outside and hear the immersion. The attendant confirms that every hair went under.
  3. She then makes the berakhah and is wished mazal tov.

Rav Ovadia also mentions covering the water with boards, leaving only a small opening so that only her head is visible, but the robe is the usual practice. This, the Rabbi said, is what his Beit Din and the Sefaradim do, and it satisfies all the opinions.

Practical summary

  • Kabbalat misvot must be before three according to all views. For milah and tevilah, Tosafot are lenient bediavad and the Rif and the Rambam are not.
  • The three need to be observant Jews who understand conversion; they do not need to be qualified dayanim.
  • According to Rav Moshe Feinstein a Reform conversion has no halachic standing; Conservative conversions were doubtful in his day, and the Rabbi treats them as invalid by default today while checking the people involved.
  • A conversion is not done at night or on Shabbat lekhathilah, but bediavad it stands.
  • For a woman, the dayanim witness an immersion in a loose robe; the final immersion without it takes place with a woman attendant while the dayanim wait outside.
