Eating and Benefit from Hasot (De'oraita)

The Torah says that from the time the Korban Pesah could be sacrificed (hasot on Erev Pesah), one may not eat hames — "לא תאכל עליו חמץ." The majority of Rishonim, and the Shulhan Arukh, hold that this prohibition applies even nowadays when there is no Korban Pesah. The Gemara explains that when the Torah says "Lo Tochal" (do not eat), this generally includes a prohibition of hana'ah (benefit) as well. Therefore, from hasot and onwards on Erev Pesah, it is a Torah prohibition to both eat and benefit from hames, carrying the punishment of malkut (lashes).

There are some Rishonim who hold the prohibition is only de-rabbanan, but either way it is forbidden. The Shulhan Arukh follows the majority view that it is de'oraita.

Owning Hames After Hasot on Erev Pesah

Owning hames after hasot on Erev Pesah does not violate a negative prohibition. The Torah says "seven days you shall not have hames in your possessions" — the prohibition of ownership applies only during the seven days of Pesah itself. However, if one still owns hames after hasot, one loses the opportunity to fulfil the positive misvah of tashbitu (destroying hames at the correct time).

Summary: After hasot on Erev Pesah — eating and benefit = Torah prohibition (lav); owning = missing a positive misvah but not violating a lav.

Se'if 1 – The Timeline of Prohibitions on Erev Pesah

Shulhan Arukh: Hames from six hours (hasot) onwards is forbidden to benefit from. The Hachamim forbade eating from two hours before hasot (the beginning of the 5th hour). Although eating is forbidden during the 5th hour, one may still sell it, benefit from it, give it to animals, sell to a non-Jew, or give it to a non-Jew. When giving to animals, one must ensure they finish everything and nothing remains. From the beginning of the 6th hour (one hour before hasot), they forbade benefit from it as well.

Why Did the Hachamim Add Extra Hours?

In the days without clocks, people relied on the sun to tell the time. On a cloudy day, a person could easily miscalculate when hasot is. Therefore:

  • For eating: The Hachamim added two hours before hasot — more severe caution, since eating could lead to karet if it continued into Pesah.
  • For benefit: The Hachamim added one hour before hasot — less severe, since benefit alone doesn't carry karet.

Selling Hames After the Deadline

If you did sell hames after the prohibited time (which you should not do — it's asur):

  • The transaction is valid (chal).
  • The hames would not have the status of hames she'avar alav ha-Pesah — you could use it after Pesah.
  • But this should not be done deliberately.

Is a Gift Considered Hana'ah (Benefit)?

Yes — giving hames as a gift is a form of benefit, because you build a relationship and there may be a mutual benefit later on. This is clear from the halachot of basar be-chalav (meat and milk): meat cooked with milk is asur be-hana'ah, and that includes even giving it as a gift.

However: Leaving hames as hefker (ownerless, on the street for anyone to take) — this is not considered benefit and is permitted. There is no relationship being built when you simply abandon something.

Practical example: With stam yeinam (non-kosher wine) — Sephardim hold it is asur be-hana'ah, so you can't sell it. Rather than pour it down the drain, simply leave it out on the street and let someone else take it.

The Rema: Calculating the Hours — Sha'ot Zemaniyot vs. Fixed Hours

General rule: We use sha'ot zemaniyot (seasonal/halachic hours) — dividing the daylight period into 12 equal parts.

The Terumat HaDeshen's Opinion

The Terumat HaDeshen (a major Ashkenazi posek, ~600 years ago) held a novel position based on the Gemara. The Gemara asks: why did the Hachamim add specifically two hours? It answers: because that's the maximum a person can get confused. The Terumat HaDeshen infers from this that "two hours" means two regular 60-minute hours — fixed, regardless of season. The same applies to the one hour for benefit.

Practical Example (London, April)

  • Hasot is approximately 12:15 PM.
  • Terumat HaDeshen: Latest time to eat = 10:15 AM. Latest time to sell = 11:15 AM.
  • Sha'ot zemaniyot: Latest time to eat ≈ 10:35–10:40 AM (because seasonal hours are slightly longer than 60 minutes after the equinox).

This year, Pesah falls after the equinox (April), so the Terumat HaDeshen is a chumra — his times come out earlier.

Halacha

Everyone agrees the main halacha is sha'ot zemaniyot. However, the Terumat HaDeshen can be relied upon bedi'avad and bishat hadchak (in pressing circumstances) when it results in a leniency — e.g., if you're running late selling hames, this may give you an extra 10–20 minutes. Furthermore, the further north you go, the greater the difference between the two calculations, even in April.

Note on Mekhirat Hames: You can actually do mechirat hames yourself if you know how — you can sell hames to your next-door non-Jewish neighbour directly if you know the proper procedure.

Smelling Hames on Pesah

Question: Can you intentionally walk past a bakery on Pesah and smell the bread?

Background:

  • The prohibition of benefit from hames applies to all hames — not just your own. The Torah says hames shouldn't be eaten, not "your hames."
  • There is an opinion in the Rema (Siman 216) that one says a berakhah on the smell of fresh bread — implying smell is something substantial.
  • Biur Halacha adds: unlike treif food (which you'd never eat, so smelling is fine), hames is food that is normally kosher during the year — you don't have the same repulsion for it, and smelling it may tempt you to eat it.

Halacha (consensus of poskim, including Sephardi poskim):

Distinction from non-kosher food: There is no prohibition to smell treif food generally (e.g., walking past McDonald's is fine). But you cannot smell something that is asur be-hana'ah, like basar be-chalav (meat cooked with milk) — or hames during Pesah.

Se'if 2 – A Jew's Hames Deposited with You

Shulhan Arukh: If a Jewish person left hames in your care and didn't return:

  • If you can communicate: Contact them and ask what to do.
  • If you cannot communicate: Wait until the 5th hour (one hour left to sell). If no one has come, you may sell it — even though you weren't given permission to sell.
  • Why? This is meshiv aveidah (returning lost property) — in one hour, this hames will be permanently worthless (asur be-hana'ah forever). By selling it, you are saving the depositor's property.
  • If selling isn't possible or time is too tight, you can sell even earlier.
  • If you could not sell it: You must destroy it by the end of the 5th hour, even though it's not yours.
  • Why destroy someone else's property? Two reasons: (a) The misvah of tashbitu from the Torah applies. (b) If the depositor didn't do bitul, he will be violating a Torah prohibition of owning hames on Pesah. You have a duty to save him from transgression.

Liability for Not Selling

If you had the opportunity to sell the hames and were lazy or negligent, and then had to destroy it — some poskim hold you bear financial responsibility for the loss (as part of your duty of care as a custodian). This is a minority opinion and considered a weak argument, but an interesting side discussion in Choshen Mishpat.

Se'if 3 – Bartering Hames After the Prohibited Time

Shulhan Arukh: If someone used hames to barter/buy something after the prohibited time (which is asur — a violation):

  • The item received in exchange is permitted — the prohibition does not transfer to the bartered item.
  • Some authorities say: for the person who did it, it's forbidden as a penalty (kenas). But the main halacha is that the item itself is permitted.

Siman 444 – When Erev Pesah Falls on Shabbat (ערב פסח שחל בשבת)

Partially covered — continuation next week

Se'if 1 – Bedikat Hames on Thursday Night

Shulhan Arukh: When the 14th of Nisan falls on Shabbat:

  • Bedikat Hames is done on Thursday night (the night of the 13th) — the Hachamim instituted it on this night.
  • All the regular halachot of Bedikat Hames apply — berakhah, candle, searching properly, etc.
  • All hames must be destroyed before Shabbat (i.e., on Friday). The Shulhan Arukh says "before Shabbat" — even late Friday afternoon is fine, since the prohibition of owning hames only begins on Shabbat after the 5th hour.
  • You leave aside hames for two meals only — Friday night and Shabbat morning.

What If You Missed Thursday Night?

As we learned previously, if you didn't do Bedikat Hames on the night of the 13th, you would do it during the day on the 13th (i.e., Erev Shabbat / Friday). If you missed that too, you could theoretically do Bedikat Hames on Shabbat itself.

Why Only Two Meals?

  • Friday night: Misvah of Seudat Shabbat — use hames (or see alternatives below).
  • Shabbat morning: Misvah of Seudat Shabbat — use hames (or alternatives).
  • Seudah Shlishit: Cannot use hames — it's already after the prohibited time on Shabbat afternoon. Cannot use regular massah — prohibited on Erev Pesah. Must use massah ashirah (Sephardim) or alternatives (Ashkenazim).

The Prohibition of Eating Massah on Erev Pesah

Gemara: כל האוכל מצה בערב פסח כבועל ארוסתו בבית חמיו — "One who eats massah on Erev Pesah is like one who has relations with his fiancée in his father-in-law's house" (i.e., before the wedding).

  • This is an issur de-rabbanan — you must come to the Seder with appetite and excitement for the massah.
  • Important: The halakha is that there is no prohibition from a month before, or from Rosh Hodesh Nisan — those are customs only. The actual prohibition is specifically on Erev Pesah.
  • The specific expression used — fiancée, not just any prohibition — has halachic ramifications (see massah mevushelet section below).

The Ashkenazi Problem

Rama: The Ashkenazi custom is not to eat massah ashirah on Pesah at all.

Concern: The fruit juice may contain some water — especially modern concentrates from supermarkets, which often contain added water. If any water is present, the mixture would become full hames.

Consequence for Seudah Shlishit: Ashkenazim cannot use hames (too late), massah (Erev Pesah prohibition), or massah ashirah. Their options are limited to: meat, fish, fruit, or cakes made from massah meal. They can rely on the four opinions for fulfilling Seudah Shlishit that we learned previously (Siman 289): bread, mezonot, meat and fish, or even fruit.

The Berakhah Problem with Massah Ashirah

Massah ashirah has the status of pat haba'ah be-kisnin (similar to cake/pastry) — normally mezonot, not hamotzi. Can you fulfil Seudat Shabbat (which requires lechem/bread) with something that's mezonot?

Answer: Some poskim argue that if you eat a significant amount (shiur kvi'at seudah), it becomes hamotzi. Others argue that since there's nothing else available, massah ashirah is the "bread of the hour" — similar to how cracker massah becomes hamotzi on Pesah itself because it's the only bread available. But this is debatable.

Note on pat haba'ah be-kisnin: One interpretation is that it refers to something that is "koses" — crunchy, requiring chewing and crushing. This is why cracker massah (which is crunchy) is mezonot during the year according to many opinions, while soft hand-made massah (like the kind made by Dan David in Hendon, or widely available in Israel) is hamotzi year-round because it has the texture of real bread.

The Best Practical Solution: Massah Mevushelet (Boiled/Fried Massah)

Hacham Ovadia Yoseph strongly advocated this approach. The Aruch HaShulchan (Baruch Epstein) writes this for Ashkenazim as well.

The Principle

The Gemara's prohibition of massah on Erev Pesah applies to massah you would use to fulfil the misvah at the Seder. The specific expression — "like one who has relations with his fiancée" — implies: you can't wait a few hours for something you'll be having tonight. If you can't fulfil the misvah with that massah in a few hours' time, then it's not part of the prohibition.

Halacha: If massah is baked and then boiled or fried, bishul (cooking) overrides afiyah (baking) — it loses its status as "baked massah" and cannot fulfil the misvah of eating massah at the Seder. Therefore, it is not prohibited on Erev Pesah.

Source of the principle: This is the same halacha that proves bishul achar afiyah (cooking after baking) changes the status of food — which is relevant in Hilchot Shabbat regarding the question of ein bishul achar bishul. If you bake massah and then boil it in soup, it is no longer "baked massah" — proof that bishul overrides afiyah. This means that on Pesah night itself, if you put baked massah into soup (during Shulchan Orech, later in the Seder), you have not fulfilled the misvah of massah with the soup-soaked piece — everyone agrees on this.

How to Prepare

  1. Before Shabbat (Friday): Take regular massah.
  2. Boil it in soup/water or fry it (e.g., dip in egg and fry) — ensure it is properly cooked/fried so it has the halachic status of being cooked.
  3. Keep it intact — if it crumbles into small pieces, there are issues with lechem mishneh and its bread status.
  4. Use this for all three Shabbat meals — Friday night, Shabbat morning, and Seudah Shlishit.

Note on Ashkenazi gebrochts: Sephardim do not hold of gebrochts (concern about massah + water becoming hames). The massah mevushelet approach does not pose a gebrochts concern since the massah is being prepared before Pesah — there's no issue of gebrochts before Pesah begins.

The Problems with Using Real Hames on Shabbat

If you do use hames for Friday night and Shabbat morning, you face significant practical difficulties:

  • Destroying hames on Shabbat: How do you get rid of the remaining crumbs and bread? You can't burn it, and you can't carry it outside without an eruv.
  • Flushing down the toilet: One of the few available options, but uncomfortable — especially with large families and lots of children where you can't precisely measure how much bread is needed.
  • Switching dishes: You'd have to use non-Pesah dishes for hames meals and then switch to Pesah dishes mid-Shabbat.

This is why the massah mevushelet approach is recommended — it avoids all of these complications.

Seudah Shlishit Timing

  • Seudah Shlishit on Erev Pesah must be eaten before the 10th hour of the day (i.e., two halachic hours before the end of the day).
  • Reason: You need to come to the Seder hungry and with appetite for the massah.
  • The Kabbalists are particular about praying Minhah before Seudah Shlishit. On this Shabbat, that is nearly impossible — Shaharit and Musaf take up the morning, and one needs to eat and dispose of hames by ~10:30 AM. The poskim say it is fine to have Seudah Shlishit before Minhah in this case.

Early Shaharit This Shabbat

  • The standard Shabbat morning service in many communities finishes at 11:30 or 12:00 — far too late this year.
  • One needs to finish eating hames and destroy any remaining hames by the end of the 5th hour (approximately 10:30 AM in London).
  • Therefore, communities must schedule early Shaharit and Musaf so that people can get home, have their Shabbat morning meal, and destroy remaining hames in time.
  • Even if using massah mevushelet (no hames at all), early services are still advisable for the community's sake, since not everyone will have prepared this way.

Key Takeaways

443:1

Three Torah obligations: eating on Pesah (karet), eating/benefit after hasot on Erev Pesah (malkut), and tashbitu (positive misvah to destroy). Hachamim added 2 hours for eating, 1 hour for benefit. Owning hames after hasot on Erev Pesah is not a lav — only during the 7 days.

443:1 (times)

Use sha'ot zemaniyot calculated from dawn to nightfall (stringent). Terumat HaDeshen (fixed 60-minute hours) can be relied upon bedi'avad. United Synagogue calendar uses the more lenient netz-to-shkiyah; Federation calendar uses the stringent opinion. Difference can be 20–30 minutes.

443:1 (smell)

Intentionally smelling hames on Pesah is forbidden — potentially de'oraita. Unintentional wafts are fine. Distinguished from smelling treif food (which is permitted).

443:1 (sale after deadline)

Sale after deadline is asur but valid (chal). Hames would not be classified as hames she'avar alav ha-Pesah. Giving a gift = hana'ah. Leaving as hefker ≠ hana'ah.

443:2

Jewish person's hames deposited with you: contact them if possible. If not, sell it in the 5th hour (meshiv aveidah). If you can't sell, destroy it — to save the depositor from violating a Torah prohibition.

443:3

Bartering hames after the prohibited time: the item received is permitted (prohibition doesn't transfer). Some say a kenas applies to the person who did it.

444:1

Erev Pesah on Shabbat: Bedikah on Thursday night. Destroy hames on Friday. Leave food for two meals only. Seudah Shlishit cannot be hames or massah.

444 (massah on E"P)

Massah is forbidden on Erev Pesah (de-rabbanan). Magen Avraham: only during the day, not the preceding night. Most poskim: entire day.

444 (massah ashirah)

Flour + 100% fruit juice. Not hames, not massah. Ashkenazim don't eat it on Pesah (concern about water content). Berakhah issues (pat haba'ah be-kisnin / mezonot).

444 (massah mevushelet)

Best solution: boiled or fried massah. Bishul overrides afiyah — loses massah status, so not prohibited on Erev Pesah. Can be used for all three Shabbat meals. Recommended for both Sephardim and Ashkenazim.

444 (seudah shlishit)

Must eat before the 10th hour. OK to have before Minhah on this Shabbat. Early Shaharit required this year.
