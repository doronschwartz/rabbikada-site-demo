Where We Are

In Chapters 1 and 2 Rav Saadiah proved three premises: the world had a beginning, it was made by something external to it, and it was made from nothing. Chapter 3 brings twelve rival opinions. Last week the class saw four: Plato's eternal matter, creation out of God Himself (the Upanishads), the combination of the two, and Mani's dualism, which Chazal fought in the wording of our prayers. Tonight: the rest, "selecting the main part," then a start on Chapter 4.

The Four Elements, and the Heavens

The fifth view (Hippocrates): everything is made of four eternal natures, heat, cold, moisture and dryness, which were once separate and then combined into the world. "This is the closest we have so far to the Big Bang."

Rav Saadiah's answer: you refuse creation from nothing because you never saw it. But you never saw heat or dryness on their own either, only hot or dry things, and you never saw fire and water join. "So you're accepting things which you've never seen, in order to run away from something you've never seen. What have you gained?"

Some Jews read veruah Elokim merahefet al penei hamayim as proof that air and water existed before creation. Rav Saadiah: the verse describes what was there after "In the beginning Hashem created the heavens and the earth," not before it.

The sixth view adds eternal matter to the four elements, and falls to the same arguments. The seventh says the eternal heavens made the world; "quite bizarre," and dismissed quickly.

Chance: The Atomists

The eighth view is "relevant for nowadays": the world came about by accident, with no one choosing it. Eternal atoms pressed together; the light ones rose to form the heavens, the heavy ones sank. This was the school of Epicurus, whose name gave us the word apikoros: "Apikoros is not a Hebrew word... the Hakhamim used him as the heretic."

"This is very close to what nowadays is the Big Bang." But who provided the energy?

Rav Saadiah's two arguments are still the ones used today: 1. Where did the atoms come from, and why did they move? Why leave one place and press together? "The Big Bang can't explain that." 2. The house and the ship. Would a heap of stones and wood, struck by lightning, become a house? Would wood and iron in a pile become a ship? Think of the complexity of the eye. Without a Director, "how on earth did that happen?"

Eternity: Aristotle

The ninth view is "much simpler": there was never a beginning. The world always was as it is, and always will be.

The Rambam grappled with this in the Moreh Nevuchim. If Aristotle had sufficient proof, he wrote, he would reinterpret Bereshit bara, just as we reinterpret the verses about God's hands, eyes and "coming down to Egypt." But Aristotle has no proof, so there is no need. "And Rambam proved to be right, because now we know scientifically that there was a beginning to the universe."

Rav Saadiah's answer goes to the root. The Aristotelians say: we only accept what we perceive, and we never perceived a beginning. But you never perceived something with no beginning either. "You've never seen something infinite." Had they said "we don't know," they would have a point; instead they claim eternity, which they have seen no more than creation.

Relativism: The Sophists

The tenth view (the sophists, "where the word sophisticated comes from") holds that truth is only opinion: if one person says the world is eternal and another says it was created, both are true. "They are more foolish than everyone before them."

The Rabbi called this "one of the intellectual diseases of this world": forming reality around your opinions, today's moral relativism, where nothing is objectively good or bad. His examples: the claim that a man can become a woman by feeling it, against biology ("Richard Dawkins has come out very clearly and said a man is a man and a woman is a woman"), and the refusal to call terrorists terrorists because "one man's terrorist is another man's freedom fighter."

Someone asked where such a person would be buried. Halachically, "we bury him with the men." "Halakha goes off reality, not on what you believe."

Rav Saadiah's refutation is pure logic: "It's wrong to think that reality is based on opinions; rather opinions are based on facts." If two opinions make two truths, ten opinions make ten truths, and when someone changes his mind reality changes with him. Nothing is ever false.

Doubt: The Skeptics, and the Deniers

The eleventh view says there is a truth, but it is unattainable, so the right way to live is to abstain from every view: "essentially agnostic." Rav Saadiah: then why do you go to the best doctor when you are ill?

He adds that this is worse than the sophists. "Sometimes it's worse to have no belief than to have a belief which is wrong." A wrong view can be argued with and corrected; someone who is skeptical of everything has nowhere to go. The Rabbi linked this to Avraham, who preferred a wife for Yitzhak from Canaan, people of conviction, even mistaken conviction, and to Amalek: "Amalek in gematria is safek, doubt." Amalek attacked when Israel seemed invincible, to cast doubt into everything.

The twelfth view simply denies everything, even what the senses show. The Rabbi compared it to Descartes, for whom everything may be illusion except "I think, therefore I am," and noted the true point in it: your identity is your consciousness. "Our body is just a dress," and that is why the Rambam's and the Ramhal's philosophy is "basically intellectual."

Summary of Chapter 3

Rav Saadiah's twelve rival views: (1) Plato's eternal matter; (2) creation from God Himself; (3) both together; (4) dualism; (5) the four elements; (6) elements plus matter; (7) the heavens; (8) the atomists; (9) Aristotle's eternity; (10) the sophists; (11) the skeptics; (12) the deniers. Other opinions branch off these. "I've shown you why they're wrong," and we return to the three premises.

Today almost no one holds most of these. The real choice is between a Creator and a world that made itself, and Rav Saadiah's question stands: how did it come together "without a Director"? "I've never come across an argument to explain how that happened."

Chapter 4: Ten Questions

Chapter 4 brings ten questions people ask about creation: what was time and space "before," and above all why Hashem created the world. Two were started tonight.

1. Kohelet: dor holekh vedor ba veha'aretz le'olam omedet. Does "the earth stands forever" mean the world is eternal? Rav Saadiah turns it around: the generations are finite, they are part of the earth, so the earth is finite too. The Rabbi's simpler answer: "forever" is relative to the generations that come and go.

2. How can something come from nothing? If we could explain it, we would not need a Creator. "If you're asking me to explain how I can create something from nothing, you're asking me to be God." God and His wisdom are one, which Essay 2 will discuss.

Summary

  • Every rival theory accepts something unseen to avoid creation from nothing, which is also unseen.
  • The atomists' chance is today's Big Bang without a Director, and it cannot explain how the world came together.
  • Aristotle's eternity has no proof, and science now shows a beginning, as the Rambam expected.
  • Relativism and skepticism are worse than error: reality is not shaped by opinion.
