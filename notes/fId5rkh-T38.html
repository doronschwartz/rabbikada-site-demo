Topic: Towards an understanding of why the righteous suffer, part one: what pain is for Sources: Tanakh, the Gemara and Pirkei Avot Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at a home gathering, Monday 3 June 2024 (the yahrzeit of Rav Saadiah Gaon and the Ramhal), the sixth class of a series on the fundamentals of Judaism

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why "towards an understanding"

Dayan Kada pointed out that he had titled the class "towards an understanding" on purpose. All we can offer on this subject is a step towards understanding.

He began by setting out the question. It rests on three premises, all fundamental to our faith:

  • God is good. At the end of Yom Kippur we say that God does not desire evil.
  • God is all-powerful. Some modern writers have suggested that God is good but cannot stop suffering. Dayan Kada said that such a view cannot be reconciled with the verses of Tanakh.
  • God is all-knowing. "Hashem looks down from heaven and sees all mankind." To deny that is to deny part of God.

If any one of these is removed, the question disappears. Together they give the classic dilemma: either God can prevent suffering and does not, in which case He is not good, or He wants to and cannot, in which case He is not all-powerful.

He called this the most serious obstacle to faith he meets, and "a very fair question." Avraham asked it about Sedom, Moshe asked it in Egypt, and Yirmeyahu asked "Why does the way of the wicked prosper?" "If you've never really asked this question, then you don't really think."

Four points before we start

1. Personal suffering, not national. He was speaking about the pain of individuals. National tragedy is a separate shiur, though philosophically, he noted, the question is the same: if God allows the pain of one person, it is the same question as the pain of millions.

2. Pain, not death. Death is a different topic, connected to Adam, and deserves its own treatment.

3. Never use these explanations on someone else. He told of someone who said to a theologian: come with me to the children's hospital, and then we will talk. Iyov's friends were guilty of onaat devarim, hurting with words, because they tried to explain his suffering to him. "When a person is suffering, you don't try to rationalise their pain. You help them." Pirkei Avot teaches that it is not in our hands to explain the suffering of the righteous or the ease of the wicked. These ideas are for reflecting on our own lives only.

4. No answer heals an open wound. In great pain the mind is overloaded, and no philosophy will help. "We are not just intellectual beings. We are also emotional beings."

Suffering from God and suffering from people

The question is mainly about suffering that comes from Hashem: illness, natural disasters, the suffering of children. Suffering one person inflicts on another is the result of free will. If someone tortures another and he cries out "where is Hashem?", the question is on the torturer. He again quoted Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, who answered "where was God in the Holocaust?" with "where was man?"

Free will works both ways: if Hashem stopped me from helping a poor person because he was "meant" to be poor, nothing I do would matter. That is determinism, which the Rambam and the medieval philosophers rejected.

Someone pointed to the curses in that week's parashah, Behukotai, where Hashem says He will bring enemies upon the people. Is that not God's doing? Dayan Kada said he struggles with this too. The basic principle is human free will, but in events of great scale Tanakh shows that Hashem may intervene. In such verses, "I will let the enemy devour you" means "I will not intervene" when people choose to do harm. A person on a very high level is protected, as the Gemara says, "the Torah protects." So the question of why Hashem does not always stop great evil remains part of the wider question.

First answer: we see through a keyhole

You see a bridge about to collapse on a man, run and push him out of the way, and he is bruised. If the bridge had not fallen he would be furious with you. A child cries bitterly when you pull him back from chasing his ball into the road. A woman in labour screams as though she is dying, yet she is bringing life into the world.

We see reality through a keyhole. What looks bad may be good in the larger picture. This is what Hazal meant by "everything the Merciful One does is for the good."

Then he asked what is wrong with this answer. Someone said it can explain anything. He agreed that we are tiny and cannot expect to grasp the infinite, but raised a deeper problem. Take the man who misses his flight, and the plane crashes. The person who pushed you from the bridge had no other way to save you. But Hashem can do anything. Why does He need to save you through pain?

People offered answers. Someone suggested that pain makes the good times sweeter, or that one person's suffering makes others appreciate their health. Dayan Kada rejected the second firmly: he could not accept that a just God would put a person through a lifetime of pain so that others could walk around feeling fortunate.

Second answer: pain is information

Put your hand on a stove you thought was off and you pull it away instantly. Without pain your hand would stay there and burn. Pain is a signal. A child's regular stomach aches tell a good mother that something needs checking. People usually discover what is wrong in their bodies because something hurts.

Spiritual pain works the same way, in two ways.

To wake us up. A loving parent gives a child who ran into the road a smack on the hand: what you did was dangerous. When we lie in bed with a bad flu, unable to get on with our day, we start to ask who we are and what life is for. Even a hurt little finger shows how much every small thing matters. You shake someone who has fainted to bring him round. "Pain is a signal, it's a trigger. Do something with your life."

To be transformed into good. He described three stages in facing great pain or loss. First, the crushing feeling, which is normal; this is the place of the seven days of shivah. Second, healing, which takes time. Third, which few reach: turning the pain into something good. He contrasted this with a culture that tells a person to live permanently as a victim.

Yosef is the model. Thrown into a pit, sold, enslaved, imprisoned, he could have lived as a victim. Instead he told his brothers, "You meant it for evil, but God meant it for good," and that he had been sent ahead for a purpose. Yosef's path took twenty-two years.

He shared an explanation from the Hasidic masters. Sarah, pain, is spelled צרה. Sohar, the light Noah was told to make for the ark, is צהר: the same letters. Our task is to turn the sarah into sohar, using what we have been through to repair a corner of the world.

He added that we never ask for tests. The Gemara relates that David HaMelekh asked to be tested and did not withstand it. "You don't ask for pain. If you're given the pain, you use it."

Questions from the class

Could God not have used pleasure instead of pain? He could not say why this system and not another. But if doing good always felt pleasant there would be no struggle, and without struggle there is no growth. A task that is too easy brings boredom and one too hard brings anxiety; growth comes in between.

What about someone who suffers all their life? He repeated that these explanations may not be applied to other people. Perhaps the message has not yet been heard. Sometimes the meaning appears only years later, and sometimes never.

Do we suffer for our ancestors? Only when we continue their ways. Whoever breaks the pattern is not bound by those before him.

Is God behind everything? He summarised the previous class: the more a person lives with Hashem in the forefront, with berakhot and the mezuzah, the less random his life is. One who lives without God may be left to chance, like the animal kingdom. The Gemara says that if suffering comes, a person should examine his deeds. Someone described a visit to Beit HaLohem in Israel, where badly wounded veterans were doing more than many healthy people; it had been uplifting, not depressing. Dayan Kada answered: "That's called using your pain."

Summary

  • The question of suffering rests on three beliefs we hold: God is good, all-powerful and all-knowing.
  • Never use these explanations on someone who is suffering. Help them; reflect only on yourself.
  • Suffering caused by people is a matter of their free will; the question is mainly about pain that comes from God.
  • What seems bad may be good from a wider view, but that does not explain why we need pain. Pain is information: it wakes us up and calls us to act.
  • The highest response is to turn pain into light, as Yosef did, using what we have suffered to help others.
