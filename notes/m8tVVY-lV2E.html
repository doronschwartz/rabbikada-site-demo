Topic: Why Musaf of Rosh Hashanah is built on the story of Hannah

Sources: The Gemara; Shemuel I (Hannah's prayer); the Ben Ish Hai; Abraham Lincoln's speech of 30 September 1859

Daily Halacha: Rosh Hashanah, Wednesday 2 October 2024

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

The points

  • Musaf of Rosh Hashanah is the longest Amidah of the year, with nine blessings: three at the start, three at the end, and Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot in the middle. A weekday Amidah has 19, while Shabbat, the festivals and even Yom Kippur have seven.
  • The Gemara explains that the nine correspond to the nine times Hannah mentioned Hashem's name in her prayer after the birth of Shemuel. Her story is also the Haftarah of the day.
  • The story of Hannah and Peninah shows that fortunes change: "the barren woman bears seven, and she with many children is bereft". Life is fickle and nothing can be taken for granted.
  • This past year showed it, for nations and individuals alike. Rosh Hashanah is a reset: one cannot enter it assuming life will continue as it is.
  • The Ben Ish Hai writes of a man whose ring was inscribed "Gam zeh yaavor", this too will pass: a comfort in hard times and a check on pride in good times. Abraham Lincoln told the same story in a speech on 30 September 1859, which happened to be the second of Tishri.
  • On Rosh Hashanah we rejoice outwardly with festive meals, while inwardly we acknowledge the Day of Judgment and the unity of Hashem. Living with that unity, we can be confident all will be for the best.

Sources quoted

  • The Ben Ish Hai: the ring inscribed "Gam zeh yaavor".
  • Abraham Lincoln: the saying is "chastening in the hour of pride" and "consoling in the depths of affliction".

In one line

Hannah's story, at the heart of Musaf, teaches that fortunes change, so we enter the year rejoicing outwardly and acknowledging Hashem within.
