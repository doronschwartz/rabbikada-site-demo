Siman 584: no Hallel on the Days of Judgment

With Rosh Hashanah three or four days away, the Rabbi reviewed the previous class (Selihot and fasting in 581, Kabbalah and halacha in 582, the simanim and sleeping in 583) and turned to the shofar. We say Hallel on every festival, even on Hanukkah, which is rabbinic. Purim is the exception, because reading the Megillah takes the place of Hallel. Why not on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur? The Gemara answers that the books of life and death are open: how can we sing while we stand in awe of the Day of Judgment? The chapter also sets the Torah readings and haftarot. If there is a brit milah in the synagogue it is done between the Torah reading and the shofar, so that the merits of Torah, milah and shofar come together.

Siman 585: standing for the shofar

The Shulhan Arukh rules that the shofar must be blown standing, and the Rema that it is blown from the tevah, where the Torah is read, so that the merit of the Torah rises with the blasts. The Gemara links "a day of blowing it shall be for you (lakhem)" to "you shall count for yourselves (lakhem)" of the Omer, which is counted standing. This is an asmakhta: a rabbinic law attached to a verse. So someone who sat through the shofar, through illness or ignorance, has still fulfilled the misvah. We hear 100 blasts: 30 at the blessing before Musaf, and 70 during Musaf. The misvah is fulfilled with the first 30, which is why someone blowing for a person at home blows only 30. The Hakhamim added the blasts in Musaf so the shofar is heard with Malkhuyot, Zikhronot and Shofarot, and that is the most beautiful way to do the misvah. Since we stand for the Amidah of Musaf anyway, they did not trouble us to stand twice, and the first 30 became known as the "seated blasts".

A question from the class

Someone asked whether "lakhem" teaches standing anywhere else. The Rabbi recalled that it does: for sisit (Siman 8), where the blessing and wrapping are done standing, and, as the questioner added, for the lulav. A second question: if the 30 seated blasts already fulfil the misvah, is it not odd to stand for the later ones? The Rabbi asked the class why we blow in Musaf at all and not first thing in the morning. The Gemara says that under a Roman decree they had to blow secretly, later in the day, and the practice stayed. Someone praying alone should not blow during the first three hours of the day. The first 30 fulfil the misvah; the blasts in Musaf are the hiddur. "It feels like you're fulfilling it twice," the Rabbi agreed: first the basic misvah, then the beautified one. He added that someone praying alone, as many did in the COVID year, may not blow during his own Musaf Amidah. The Hakhamim allowed it only with a congregation.

To hear, not to blow

The blessing is "lishmoa kol shofar", to hear the sound of the shofar, even for someone blowing for himself. The misvah is not the blowing: whoever hears only an echo, from a pit or a tunnel, has not fulfilled it. On a deeper level, the shofar must enter a person and wake him. "Listening" is not enough; it must be "hearing", something that penetrates. If the blower made the blessings and then could not blow, which happens with a "shofar block", someone else continues without a new blessing, as long as he was present to hear it. Only if he came from elsewhere and had not yet heard the blessings would he make them. Asked about a blower who had already heard the shofar at an earlier minyan, the Rabbi agreed there would be no one left to bless for.

Why exactly thirty

The Torah says "teruah" twice for Rosh Hashanah (Parashat Emor and Parashat Pinehas) and once for Yom Kippur of the Yovel, and a gezerah shavah teaches three teruot on Rosh Hashanah. "Vehaavartem shofar teruah" teaches that each teruah is surrounded by a straight tekiah before and after. From the Torah that is nine sounds. But the Gemara already doubts what a teruah is: a groan (what we call shevarim), a wail (what we call teruah), or both. So each version is blown three times with tekiot around it: - Groan and wail: tekiah, shevarim-teruah, tekiah, three times, 12 sounds. - Groan: tekiah, shevarim, tekiah, three times, 9 sounds. - Wail: tekiah, teruah, tekiah, three times, 9 sounds. Twelve, nine and nine make thirty. The Rema adds two customs: blow from the right side of the mouth, against the prosecutor who stands on the right, and point the shofar upwards, since "God goes up with the teruah", from the throne of judgment to the throne of mercy.

The Hazan, the prompter and payment

The Shulhan Arukh prefers that the Hazan of Musaf not blow, lest he lose his concentration. Today, with a siddur in front of everyone, a confident Hazan may. Some communities divide the sets among several blowers; the Mishnah Berurah allows it where that is the custom, or to keep peace between people who want the honour. The Rema records the custom of someone calling out each sound. It has no source in the Gemara, and calling out the first tekiah raises a question of hefsek, an interruption after the blessing. In Birkat Kohanim the Torah says "say to them", so prompting is part of the misvah, and most communities prompt even the first word; the Syrians and the Spanish and Portuguese do not prompt the first word, to avoid a hefsek. With the shofar there is no such verse, and the Mishnah Berurah says the cases are not comparable. The Arizal saw no need to prompt. His conclusion: if your community does not prompt, better not to; if it does, follow it. Can one be paid to blow or lead? Payment for work done on Shabbat is forbidden, even for permitted work like babysitting, unless it is absorbed into a larger payment. For misvot payment is allowed, but one does not see blessing from it. So a Hazan or blower should be paid for his preparation, travel and the whole package, not for the act itself.

Siman 586: which shofar

The misvah is a ram's horn, recalling the ram of the Akedah in place of Yishak, and bent, so that our hearts are bent and humble rather than proud. After the fact any shofar is kasher, straight or bent, from a ewe, a goat or an ibex. The order of preference is a bent ram's horn, then a bent horn of another animal, then a straight one. A cow's horn is invalid, because it is called a keren, not a shofar; so is a solid horn like a deer's, with no hollow. Keep it simple: do not use a cracked shofar that was stuck together. Last point: on Rosh Hashanah one may not even do something rabbinically forbidden, such as climbing a tree, to fetch the only shofar. The Hakhamim treated their prohibitions like the Torah's, which a positive misvah does not override. On the same reasoning, if driving an electric car were only rabbinic, it would seemingly still not be allowed to reach the shofar. One may, however, ask a non-Jew to bring it.

Practical summary

  • The misvah is fulfilled with the first 30 blasts; the blasts in Musaf are the hiddur.
  • Sefaradim sit for the first 30 because they stand in Musaf. Anyone leaving before Musaf should stand for them.
  • The blessing is "lishmoa kol shofar": the misvah is hearing the sound directly, not an echo.
  • If the blower cannot continue, another person present continues without a new blessing.
  • The ideal shofar is a bent ram's horn; a cow's horn is invalid, and avoid a cracked shofar that was repaired.
  • No rabbinic prohibition is set aside to fetch a shofar on Rosh Hashanah, but a non-Jew may be asked.
