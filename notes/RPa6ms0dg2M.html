Where we are

Last week we finished Siman 3. The Rabbi planned about half an hour on the status of Kohanim nowadays (Kohanim bizman hazeh), which he called an important topic that comes up in practice, and then a start on Siman 4. He said the first nine seifim of Siman 4 are fairly straightforward, and the real difficulty comes after that.

The Gemara and the Rambam

When the Jews came up from Babylon with Ezra, Kohanim who had no written proof of lineage (ketav meyuhas) were allowed to rely on a hazakah, a presumption, and to eat terumah. The concern was that they might then be assumed to be fully established Kohanim and come to serve at the altar. Two views in the Gemara:

  • First view: the hazakah lets them eat terumah by Torah law, but not the most holy offerings (kodshei kodashim). We are not worried that they will go on to serve, because their not eating the most holy offerings is a sign (reyah) that they are not fully established.
  • Second view: the hazakah lets them eat only terumah of the rabbis. There is no real worry of confusion with Torah terumah, and "great is the hazakah" (gadol hazakah), so they are allowed even though confusion is possible.

The Rambam rules like the second view. So Kohanim nowadays have a hazakah and may eat only rabbinic terumah, even in Israel.

The Aharonim

The Maharashdam (Salonika, 16th century). He was asked about a captive woman (shevuya). Normally we presume a captive woman was assaulted and is forbidden to a Kohen, but one witness is enough to say she was not. Here the witness had only heard it from someone else. The Maharashdam allowed a sfek sfeika, a double doubt: perhaps Kohanim nowadays are not true Kohanim, and perhaps she was not assaulted. The question for everyone after him is how far this goes. Does it apply only to this case, or to others, such as a divorcee?

The Yaaves. The strongest voice. He said Kohanim nowadays are a real doubt, so strong that a father redeeming his firstborn should in fact take the money back, because it may be theft.

The Mabit (Kiryat Sefer) and the Maharit. The other extreme, father and son, 17th and 18th centuries. They hold that the hazakah makes a man a full Kohen by Torah law. The requirement of ketav meyuhas is only a rabbinic rule, and without it we only withhold the most holy things and terumah by Torah law. For all other matters he is a full Kohen.

The Maharit adds shavya nafsheih. A man who has lived as a Kohen all his life has bound himself, so he cannot suddenly be lenient and marry a woman forbidden to him. The counter-argument, which the Rabbi said we will meet later, is that shavya nafsheih works only on what you yourself believe, such as "I believe my wife had relations with a non-Jew". It does not work on something you were told, such as that your grandfather did something. So a Kohen who simply inherited the name is not bound.

The Shvut Yaakov (17th century, Russia). A Kohen married a woman who turned out to have done halisah and had forgotten it. A Kohen who marries a halusah may stay with her, because it is only a rabbinic prohibition, and the Shvut Yaakov was lenient here, relying on the doubt about Kohanim nowadays.

The Hatam Sofer. He holds that they are Kohanim. The hazakah is enough by Torah law, which gives you a full Kohen. Why then did the Gemara say Ezra wanted a ketav meyuhas? Because those men had married non-Jewish women, or because of a special case such as Bnei Barzilai. This is a special case, and generally the hazakah is enough.

Why

The hazakah is enough by Torah law. The ketav meyuhas is only rabbinic. Also shavya nafsheih.

The Gemara shows the hazakah only permits rabbinic terumah. The sign (reyah) applies because there is no ketav meyuhas. Shavya nafsheih does not apply to something you were only told.

In practice. The Rabbi said the contemporary poskim, such as Rav Ovadia and Rav Vozner, agree that today we treat Kohanim as Kohanim. But a doubt about Kehunah can be added as a further reason to be lenient when there is another reason. Rav Vozner dealt with a family that was not observant for generations. A man became a baal teshuvah, and his name is Cohen (or a gravestone of his great-grandfather shows the Kohen's hands). Rav Vozner would allow leniency even for a Kohen to marry a woman otherwise forbidden, since there are also several generations of marrying non-Jews, and no shavya nafsheih after such a gap.

Questions from the class.

  • How many generations of non-observance are needed? The Rabbi said he did not know. A boy who stops being observant does not matter, since the grandfather was still a Kohen hazakah. A girl who left observance is the real question, since she may have had relations with non-Jews. He mentioned that Rav Moshe Feinstein holds that a Jewish girl who slept in a mixed dormitory is presumed to have had such relations, but said he did not know how common this is.

Siman 4: mamzerim and the forbidden nations

The Rabbi said we go through the siman directly from the Shulhan Arukh, since the first seifim are straightforward.

Seif 1. The Torah forbids a mamzer to marry into the community. This applies to men and women, and also to netinim. On netinim there is a mahloket, and the Beit Shemuel brings it: one view in the Gemara is that "do not marry them" applies only while they are not Jewish (the Gibeonites), and after conversion they are permitted. The Ramban and the Rashba hold it is forbidden by Torah law even after conversion.

Seif 2 and onward: the nations. The Torah forbids an Ammonite and a Moabite forever, and an Egyptian and an Edomite until the third generation. Converts: you convert (first generation), your child is second, and your grandson may marry in. The Gemara says that this forbids only males: the females are permitted immediately (nikvoteihem mutarot miyad). If a woman converts while pregnant, the child born after conversion counts as the second generation, because the Torah speaks of birth and not conception.

Seif 5: marriage before conversion. If a Jewish man has relations with one of these women while she is not Jewish, there is no kiddushin (the Gemara in Kiddushin), so the child follows the mother. If the mother was an Ammonite, the child is an Ammonite who must convert, and could never marry in. If she was an Egyptian, the child waits three generations.

The reverse: a male Ammonite, Egyptian or Edomite who marries a Jewish girl. There is no kiddushin, so the child follows the mother and is Jewish. The Shulhan Arukh says the child is kasher for the community (kasher lakahal), but is pagum lekehunah: a daughter may not marry a Kohen.

Why this matters today. A girl whose mother is Jewish and father is not cannot marry a Kohen. This happens nowadays. The Riff and the Ramban say it is a doubt (sfek) in the Gemara: she may not marry a Kohen at the outset, but if she did, she may stay, and the children are sfek halalim. The Rambam says the children are kosher, and the Rosh says they are pegumim lekehunah. The Bah and the Helkat Mehokek say the halachah is like the Riff, the middle opinion. The Shulhan Arukh clearly forbids it at the outset.

The class discussed whether the doubt about Kohanim nowadays may be added as a further reason where a Kohen and such a girl are already engaged. The Rabbi explained that you can still add a doubt even where the Shulhan Arukh rules otherwise, and gave the example of Rav Ovadia allowing Sefaradim to eat in Ashkenazi restaurants where Jews cook only a third, on a double doubt. He said the Shulhan Arukh rules clearly that she may not marry a Kohen, and the question is only whether you may allow it where they are already together. Rav Ovadia, he said, allows it where they have fallen in love or are engaged, and he promised to send the reference.

Seifim 6-8: a convert marrying a Jewish girl. If a convert Ammonite or Egyptian of the second generation marries a Jewish girl, the child is fully kosher: following the mother he is Jewish, and following the father he is a convert whose own daughters are permitted anyway. In a marriage between two converts of different nations, we follow the more disqualified side. For example, an Egyptian convert who marries a non-Jewish Ammonite woman has a child who follows the mother and is an Ammonite, though the daughter is permitted on conversion. On a second-generation Egyptian man and a first-generation woman, some say the child is third generation, but the Rambam combines father and mother, so the child is second.

Final question. The Rabbi said that nowadays we cannot identify who is an Ammonite, a Moabite or an Egyptian, so almost everyone is permitted. The only interesting question is the Egyptians, since the Rosh holds one may not marry a girl from Egypt today. He left this for the next class.

Practical summary

  • A Kohen nowadays has a hazakah of Kehunah. The Rambam holds it lets him eat only rabbinic terumah.
  • Some poskim (Hatam Sofer, Maharit) treat him as a full Kohen by Torah law. Others (Yaaves) see a real doubt. The contemporary poskim treat Kohanim as Kohanim.
  • The doubt about Kohanim nowadays can be added as a further reason to be lenient, especially where the family left observance for generations and there is no shavya nafsheih.
  • A girl whose father is not Jewish may not marry a Kohen at the outset. If they are already married, she may stay, since the Shulhan Arukh follows the Riff.
  • The child of a Jewish mother and a non-Jewish or convert father is Jewish. A child of a non-Jewish mother follows her.
  • In a marriage between two converts of different nations we follow the more disqualified side.
