Part 1: The Last Criticisms

Irrelevant points! Why is he discussing Mecca, or how many people Muhammad killed?

"Why am I so harsh?"

Hashem is my witness: had he spoken even worse of us, I'd have accepted it and said, "we have sinned," and judged him favourably, assumed he meant well…

Some academics use this line to argue the Rambam himself had once outwardly renounced his faith. "There's no proof at all, but it's interesting." More likely he speaks collectively, for the people he's defending.

What he can't stay silent about: the rabbi wrote that one who acknowledged Muhammad and then prays, even at home, sins and earns no reward.

Whatever is written in a book, true or not, spreads. That's why there are so many false opinions among people: once something is in a book, it's very hard to believe it untrue, and it becomes accepted. I feared this letter would reach the simple and they'd think: I said I believe in Muhammad to save my life, so my prayers are worthless; why pray, why do any mitzvah?

If Hashem rewards such people for one tiny mitzvah, how much more a Jew who acknowledged Muhammad under duress and keeps doing mitzvot at home?

No "bribe of mitzvot," in either direction

People imagine Rosh Hashanah as scales: more mitzvot than sins and you win. No. Every mitzvah is rewarded and every sin has its consequence: לא ישא פנים ולא יקח שוחד, Hashem takes no bribe of mitzvot. You can't say "knock this aveirah off against my mitzvot." So one wrong act, even if it was wrong, can't cancel the reward of every mitzvah after it.

Section 1: When Must You Give Your Life?

The three cardinal sins

Idolatry, forbidden relations, murder: יהרג ואל יעבור, die rather than transgress, always:

- in any period, persecution or not;

- in public or private, even one-on-one ("commit adultery with this woman or I'll kill you," with no one else ever knowing);

- whatever the coercer's motive: to make you sin, or for his own benefit ("kill my enemy for me so I don't have to").

"Everyone agrees to this."

Every other mitzvah: a flowchart

Step 1: why is he forcing you?

- For his own benefit → transgress and live, even in public and even in a time of persecution.

- Proof: Esther. How could she go to Achashverosh, publicly, in a time of decree? (1) She was passive, קרקע עולם, "like the ground," which removes the cardinal-sin problem. (2) He acted for his own pleasure, not to make her sin.

- To make you abandon your religion → Step 2: is it a time of shmad?

- Yes → give your life, even in private, even for a minor mitzvah (e.g. "I want to see you, a Jew, wear sha'atnez").

- No → Step 3: in front of ten Jews?

- In private (fewer than ten) → transgress.

- In public (ten) → give your life.

Example: a gentile in front of your whole community on Shabbat, even in a time of persecution: "My garden must be mowed today, guests are coming!" He wants his lawn mowed, so mow it.

Note: the letter cites neither the Mishneh Torah nor the Moreh, because he hadn't written them yet. This is the very start of his career. He codifies all this later.

"People think you only have to give up your life for the three cardinal sins. True, but there are many more times you have to."

Who are "the ten"?

  • Jews: men or women; ten Jewish women count.
  • Children of educable age: a dispute.
  • Does the victim count himself? He may die (so don't count him), or he may live (so do). The Minchat Chinuch (mitzvah 296, Kiddush Hashem) discusses it. "Who likes to go into these kinds of questions?"
  • Source for ten: a gezeirah shavah from בתוך בני ישראל ("I shall be sanctified among the children of Israel"), via תוך in Korach and עדה, "congregation," with the spies: עד מתי לעדה הרעה הזאת, ten men.

Was the Holocaust a "time of shmad"?

The Rabbi asked: if a Nazi said "shave your beard with a razor or I'll kill you," for no benefit of his own, just to make a Jew sin, is that a time of shmad? It was persecution of extermination, not mainly religious.

- Doron: the Purim decree was extermination, and it's still considered a time of shmad. "100% correct. Thank you." So the Holocaust would be too, and one would have to die rather than shave, even one-on-one. "Wow."

- Robert: does it all apply if the coercer is a Jew? Yes, the same halachot.

"Passive" isn't the same

The commentators note the Rambam requires martyrdom only for an active transgression. "If we see you wearing tefillin, we'll kill you" asks only that you refrain, which is passive, so you needn't die. (Brit milah is different; there's a discussion.)

Why does a time of shmad make it stricter?

Robert asked. The source is the Gemara in Sanhedrin; no explicit rationale, "but it's self-explanatory." In a time of national persecution, when the nations as a whole are trying to bring us down, that's when we must stand stronger, even unto death.

Chanah and her seven sons

A participant asked whether Chanah was right to let her young sons die. From all accounts the children chose themselves (as in the Kinot of Tisha B'Av).

The conversos?

"In those times, yes, they should have given their lives." But they didn't, and they're still Jews: "That's the mistake people make. They think if you were meant to die and didn't, you're out. The Rambam says: big mistake." That's Section 3.

Section 2 (beginning): Chillul Hashem

Two kinds apply to everyone, and two more to sages and leaders.

For everyone

  1. A sin with no benefit, done because you "don't care": not desire, not gain. Swearing falsely ("I didn't eat today" when you did): pure disregard. Done in public (ten Jews), it is chillul Hashem. A modern example: someone leaving observance who sins to make a point.
  2. The opposite, kiddush Hashem: doing a mitzvah for no reason but that Hashem commanded it, publicly, is the greatest sanctification.
  3. Actions that look like sin, though they aren't: where the onlooker will clearly conclude you're sinning. This is the root of mar'it ayin.

For sages and public figures: two further kinds, next week.

Summary

  1. The Rambam is harsh because the rabbi's letter was written and spreading, and would make the simple think their mitzvot are worthless.
  2. Achav, Eglon, Nebuchadnezzar, Esav: the wicked were rewarded for tiny deeds. No bribe of mitzvot: every act counts separately, so a coerced declaration can't wipe out one's prayers.
  3. When to die:
  4. Three cardinal sins: always.
  5. Other sins: if the coercer wants his own benefit, transgress. If he wants you to sin: in a time of shmad, die even in private; otherwise, die only in public (ten Jews).
  6. Only for active transgressions.
  7. Ten = Jewish adults (men or women); children and the victim himself are debated (Minchat Chinuch 296).
  8. A decree of extermination (Purim, and so the Holocaust) counts as a time of shmad.
  9. Chillul Hashem for everyone: sinning out of sheer disregard, publicly; and conduct that looks like sin (mar'it ayin).
