Topic: Converting a man's civil partner (lishem ishut), and what happens to a child who is in the womb when its mother converts Source: Gemara Yevamot 24b and 78a; Shulhan Arukh, Yoreh Deah, Siman 268 Shiur: Fifth class of Dayan Daniel Kada's conversion course for the Montefiore semikhah programme, on Zoom, June 2021 (exact date not recorded)

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

A man suspected of living with a non-Jewish woman

The Mishnah in Yevamot (24b) says that a man who is suspected of relations with a non-Jewish woman should not marry her after she converts. If he does marry her, he is not made to divorce her. The Rabbi started with a simple question: how can a Beit Din ever convert a woman whose purpose is to marry a Jewish man, if marriage afterwards is the very thing the Mishnah discourages?

The Rishonim give two reasons for the Mishnah:

  • Rashi: marrying her proves that the rumours were true. The marriage strengthens the "voice" that was already circulating.
  • Ramban: people will suspect that she converted only for the sake of marriage, even if her motives were genuine. Marrying him after the conversion creates that suspicion.

The practical difference is a couple who are already civilly married, or openly living together. On Rashi's reasoning the Mishnah's concern falls away, because everyone already knows and nothing new is confirmed. On the Ramban's reasoning the concern remains.

The Ahiezer and the Rambam's sauce

The Ahiezer was asked exactly this about couples who come to a Beit Din to make a civil marriage Jewish. The Ahiezer relies on a responsum of the Rambam (Pe'er HaDor, Siman 211) about a man suspected of misconduct with a woman who then converted and wanted to marry him. The Rambam allowed the marriage and gave the image that it is better for him to eat the sauce than the fat itself: better to allow a small breach and the gossip that goes with it than to have him live with a non-Jewish woman for the rest of his life.

A Beit Din that converts a civilly married couple may, on the Ramban's reasoning, be in breach of the Mishnah, but the alternative is worse.

He drew out a surprising point. A couple who are civilly married are in a stronger position from a conversion perspective than a couple who are only dating or living together informally. If the relationship is informal, he might leave her and marry a Jewish woman, so the Beit Din takes the concern seriously. If they are married with children, they will probably stay together, and refusing means refusing half-Jewish children and a Jewish person who may become more observant. Where a family is involved, the Beit Din will try to help rather than put up obstacles.

A woman who converts while pregnant

The main topic: a woman begins the process, becomes pregnant, and immerses while carrying a fetus. Does her immersion convert the fetus, or must the child convert after birth?

The Gemara (Yevamot 78a) says the child is considered to have converted with its mother. The Gemara gives two possible reasons.

  1. Ubar yerekh imo: the fetus is "the thigh of its mother", an extension of her body. When she becomes Jewish, it automatically does.
  2. The fetus converts in its own right. The Gemara asks whether the fetus is a chatzitzah, a barrier between the mother and the water. The rule is that something covering most of the body, or even a small part that a person is particular about, blocks immersion. The Gemara answers that the fetus is in its natural place and is not considered a barrier.

Tosafot ask why the Gemara needs the second answer if the first is available. They explain that the Gemara wants to account for the position that the fetus is not an extension of the mother. So there are two strong reasons, and the Rabbi called the differences between them "nafka minot", practical consequences.

Three (or four) differences between the reasons

  • Can the mother immerse for the child's sake alone? A Jewish father wants his child born Jewish, but the mother has no wish to convert. This works only if the child converts in its own right (children are converted without assessing motivation, a point the Rabbi said would come in the next class).
  • Must the Beit Din know about the pregnancy? If the fetus is an extension, it makes no difference. If the child converts in its own right, the Beit Din must know, since a conversion is done with the Beit Din's knowledge.
  • Can the child object at twelve or thirteen? A child converted by a Beit Din has a right to object at that age (to be studied in the next class). If the fetus converted in its own right, it keeps that right. If it was an extension of its mother, it cannot object any more than she can.
  • What if the mother is later found to have been insincere? If the fetus is an extension, it is not converted, because the mother was not. If it converted independently, it is.

What the Acharonim say

The Pitchei Teshuvah cites the Tiferet LeMoshe, who says the conversion works only if the mother converts at the same time, which fits the extension reason. In the next paragraph he cites the Nodah BiYehudah, who says that if the Beit Din did not know she was pregnant, it is not a conversion, which fits the independent reason. The Pitchei Teshuvah is already pulling in both directions.

The Aruch HaShulchan, the Rabbi said, follows the extension reason throughout: the mother could not immerse without converting herself, the Beit Din need not know, and the child could not later object. The Rabbi knew of no contemporary teshuvah on this. He expected that Batei Din today would take the stringent side on each point, that is, want to know about the pregnancy and treat the child as unable to object. He added: "I think it would be a bit more nuanced today."

A baby born to a convert: milah on Shabbat

A boy is born to a mother who converted while pregnant, and the eighth day falls on Shabbat. Does the brit milah override Shabbat?

  • If the child is an extension of its mother, he was born Jewish and the milah is an ordinary brit milah, which overrides Shabbat.
  • If he converted independently, the milah is the completion of his conversion. A conversion milah does not override Shabbat.

The Binyan Tziyon (Rav Yaakov Ettlinger) was stringent: the milah is done on Sunday. The Dvar Avraham (Rav Avraham Shapiro of Kovno) objected: no one would say that a baby born to a convert mother is a non-Jew who must be converted at eight days, and the law of a brother's wife applies to twin brothers born after their mother's conversion, which shows they are born Jewish. He then suggested a novel idea: a child who converted, say, in the fifth month was a non-Jewish fetus for part of the pregnancy and Jewish for the rest. The milah is therefore partly a milah of a Jew and partly of a convert, so it should not override Shabbat. Both authorities conclude that the milah is done on Sunday.

The Ahiezer holds that it does override Shabbat, and so does one other authority. The Rabbi said their proofs are quite weak, and that the poskim today are stringent.

A note on Kehunah

Families sometimes ask to speed up a conversion so that the child is born Jewish. The Rabbi explained that for the laws of Kehunah, what counts is the moment of conception. A child conceived while the mother was a non-Jew and born after she converted is still treated as the child of a convert for these purposes. A girl who converts is forbidden to a Kohen, and this applies here too.

Practical summary

  • A man suspected of living with a non-Jewish woman should not marry her after conversion (Mishnah, Yevamot 24b); Rashi and the Ramban differ on the reason.
  • For a civil couple, the Rambam's responsum (sauce rather than fat) and the Ahiezer allow a Beit Din to convert, since the alternative is worse. A family situation is treated more generously.
  • A child in the womb when its mother converts is Jewish, either as an extension of the mother or as an independent convert.
  • The two reasons differ on whether the mother can immerse for the child alone, whether the Beit Din must know of the pregnancy, and whether the child may object at twelve or thirteen. The Rabbi expects Batei Din today to be stringent on each.
  • A boy born to such a mother is circumcised on Sunday rather than on Shabbat if the eighth day falls on Shabbat.
  • Conception, not birth, decides the child's status for Kehunah.
