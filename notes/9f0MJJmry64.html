Beginning with the Seder night

A month before Pesah, the class began the laws of Pesah. They span 66 chapters, the longest of all the festivals, which is why the Gemara says we begin discussing them thirty days before. Rather than start at the beginning, with less practical matters such as baking massot, the Rabbi chose to start at Siman 472, the Seder night itself, and work through it from beginning to end.

Be ready, but do not start too early

Paragraph 1. The table should be set before Yom Tov so that the Seder can begin as soon as one comes home. Do not come home and only then start laying the table and finding the cushions. Even someone who usually gives or attends a class after Arvit should cancel it that night. Erev Pesah, like Erev Yom Kippur, is one of the two days when the study hall closed.

The Shulhan Arukh says it is a misvah "to hurry and to eat". Since nothing is eaten for a long time, the Mishnah Berurah explains: hurry so that the children stay awake. In the Shaar HaSiyun he adds that the questions are meant to come not only at Mah Nishtanah but throughout: why massah? why maror? Children should be awake for Mosi Massah, Maror and Korekh, not just the four questions.

But Kiddush is not said until nightfall. On Shabbat and Yom Tov one may usually add from the weekday and begin early. On Pesah night the massah is compared to the Pesah offering, which was eaten only at night, and the Kiddush is tied to the meal.

This is not a Torah requirement, though. The Rabbi has said many times that someone ill, elderly or with young children, especially when Pesah is late and nightfall is around 8.40pm, may start Kiddush and the early steps before nightfall, as long as Maggid and the massah are after nightfall. Kadesh, Urhas, Karpas, Yahas and the customs that go with them can take a good hour. He was once asked by an old age home what to do; his advice was to say only the essentials of Maggid, a question and its answer, and eat the massah after nightfall, so that at least the Torah obligation is fulfilled properly.

Reclining

The table should be set with one's best utensils, and the seat prepared for reclining. Many think one only reclines for the massah, the four cups and Korekh. In fact one should be comfortable the whole night, apart from where the halacha says not to recline, such as Karpas and Maror. Even on a bench, lean.

The Mishnah Berurah brings that the Maharil would put out on his Seder table the silver and gold pledges left with him as collateral by non-Jews. He could not use them, but he enjoyed seeing them and displaying fine things on Pesah night.

How to recline: to the left, on something. Not forward (like someone worried), not backwards, and not to the right, in case food goes down the wrong way. This applies to left-handed people too. "Leaning in the air is not called leaning." Lean on the back or arm of your chair or the chair beside you, at an angle.

Women and reclining: a reversal

The Gemara says a woman need not recline unless she is an ishah hashuvah, a prominent woman. Partly reclining was not the custom of women, and partly because a woman was in awe of her husband and would not recline in front of him. "We can look back at it and think that it's backwards, but that's how things were."

Ruling

Practice today

Ashkenazim

The Rema quotes the Mordekhai that "all our women are prominent", but adds that they rely on the Raviah, who held that reclining no longer applies since we eat at tables

Ashkenazi women do not recline

Sefaradim

The Shulhan Arukh follows the Gemara; Sefaradi poskim never accepted the Raviah. About 300 years ago the Knesset HaGedolah wrote that our women are prominent

Sefaradi women do recline

So, the Rabbi noted, although the rulings would lead you to expect Ashkenazi women to recline and Sefaradi women not to, in practice it is the opposite. Since Sefaradim never accepted the Raviah's view, "leaning is leaning" for them. Only a Sanhedrin could say that today we should eat as royalty eats now.

Who else reclines

  • A son reclines in front of his father, since a father is assumed to allow it.
  • A student may not recline in front of his teacher, even one who is not his main teacher, unless the teacher gives permission. A rabbi hosting people at his Seder should say explicitly that they may recline. The Gemara explains that a teacher can forgo his honour because, through toil, the Torah becomes "his own Torah".
  • A Jewish waiter must also recline and drink the four cups. The Mishnah Berurah is upset with people who give their waiters tiny cups and grumble when they drink too much: freedom is for everyone.

If you forgot to recline

The Shulhan Arukh says that whoever ate or drank without reclining must eat or drink again reclining. Combined with the next paragraph, which says the four cups must be drunk in order, this means that if you drank all the cups without reclining, you would need to repeat the cups in their places in the Seder. The Rema says that after the fact you need not repeat, relying again on the Raviah. Sefaradim never accepted that view, so it is crucial that whoever leads the Seder shows everyone how to recline properly before they begin.

The four cups

How much. Each cup must hold a reviit after any dilution. On a Friday night, the Rabbi explained, the cup must hold a reviit (about 86ml) and one drinks a cheekful, the majority of a reviit (about 44ml), "less than a third" of a small plastic cup. On Pesah night one should drink the whole reviit, because the misvah is to experience freedom, not merely to make Kiddush over wine. Several people may share a large cup, but one opinion holds that you must also drink the majority of your cup, so the poskim advise against huge goblets: use a normal cup and satisfy all opinions. Drink it in one or two goes, not sips.

Which wine. Red wine is preferred, recalling the blood, unless the white is better. In the Rabbi's view rosé counts as red, since it is not white. Pasteurised wine and wine with added sugar are acceptable.

Even the poor. Someone poor must sell his clothes or borrow to have the four cups, and charity should provide them. The Rabbi alluded to the well-known story of a rabbi who gave a large sum to a woman who asked whether she could use milk for the four cups: if she had no wine, she had no meat either.

Women are obligated in the four cups and all the misvot of the night, even though they are time-bound, because they too experienced the miracle.

Children, cups and nuts

The Shulhan Arukh says it is "a misvah" to give children who have reached the age of education their own cup, not an obligation. The question is what hinukh means: training to do exactly what adults do, or training in the spirit of the misvah? For a child, freedom is not four cups of wine but sweets and nuts. So Rav Ovadia Yoseph rules that strictly children need not drink the four cups. If the choice is between expensive grape juice for many children over two nights, or chocolate to keep them awake, buy the chocolate. Children should not be forced to drink the four cups.

Children should be given nuts so they notice the changes and ask questions. The Mishnah Berurah adds that they must stay awake at least until Avadim Hayinu. Too often children sing Mah Nishtanah, get their treat and go to bed, "and you have missed the whole point. The whole point is the answer." In the Syrian custom the leader announces in Arabic before Avadim Hayinu: "This is the answer."

Practical summary

  • Set the table and seats in advance and begin as soon as you come home. Kiddush is ideally after nightfall, but those who need to may start earlier, provided Maggid and the massah are after nightfall.
  • Recline to the left, leaning on something, for the massah, the cups and Korekh, and stay comfortable all night.
  • Sefaradi women recline; Ashkenazi women generally do not. Students need their teacher's permission.
  • Sefaradim who drank or ate without reclining must repeat; show everyone how to recline before you begin.
  • Drink a full reviit for each cup, ideally red wine, in one or two goes, from a normal-sized cup.
  • Children need not drink four cups; keep them awake and asking, at least until Avadim Hayinu.
