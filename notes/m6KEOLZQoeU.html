These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Where we are

After a week's break, Dayan Kada recapped. The first two classes dealt with Olam HaBa in the Rambam's view: purely spiritual, with no body, an intellectual bliss of closeness to the Divine Presence, in proportion to a person's moral and intellectual perfection. Then karet: the person's identity ceases, though an element remains aware of what it is missing. Now came the resurrection, and he explained that he had chosen this order deliberately, as the evening would show.

Why we must cleave, not receive

To set the foundation, the class read the opening of the Ramhal's Derech Hashem (1:2), and he broke the argument into premises:

  1. Hashem is good.
  2. The nature of the good is to give good to others.
  3. Hashem is perfect, so the good He gives must be perfect good.
  4. Perfect good is found only in Hashem Himself.
  5. But Hashem cannot give Himself to others: by definition He is one and unique. The Christian idea of God entering a human being contradicts what God is.

So His wisdom decreed that creatures would be able to cleave to Him, as far as humanly possible, and so share in His perfection without becoming it. In the analogy he has used all series: a queen who wants to give her goodness cannot make everyone queen, since then there is no queen. What she can do is let you spend time with her.

This drew a long discussion about the soul. Is there something of Hashem inside us? Dayan Kada was emphatic that one must not think so: Hashem cannot be divided into pieces in each of us. What of "in His image"? God has no image. Selem Elokim means that, like Hashem, we have the power to choose, to build and to destroy, which is what distinguishes humans from animals. We can certainly reflect Hashem, as the moon reflects the sun. "That's precisely the point."

He also explained what the soul is: essentially consciousness, identity. The body is like an astronaut's suit on the moon. Wear it long enough and you might start to think the suit is you, but who we are is our thoughts, feelings, values and consciousness.

The Raavad's accusation

The Rambam writes (Hilchot Teshuvah 8:2) that in the World to Come there is no body, only the souls of the righteous, like the angels, with no eating, drinking, sitting or sleeping: "the righteous sit with their crowns on their heads and delight in the radiance of the Shekhinah".

The Raavad, the Rambam's great critic, who nonetheless valued him greatly, wrote sharply: "The words of this man seem to me close to one who says there is no resurrection of bodies, only of souls. By my life, this was not the view of the Hakhamim." He cites rabbinic statements describing the dead rising in their bodies and concludes that they will rise with their bodies, perhaps in strong healthy forms like the angels or Eliyahu.

Two pictures of the future

The root of the dispute, Dayan Kada explained, is whether Olam HaBa and the resurrection are one event or two.

The Raavad, the Ramban and many others. After death the soul waits in Gan Eden, also called the world of souls, Olam HaNeshamot; the two terms are synonymous. At the end of days the world is renewed, the dead are resurrected, and that ushers in Olam HaBa, which is physical, with refined bodies and souls together, for eternity. It is called "the world to come" because it does not exist yet. This, he said, is probably the mainstream approach.

The Rambam. Eternal bliss with God can only be spiritual, so Olam HaBa begins as soon as a person dies. It already exists; it is "to come" only relative to us. "Philosophically," he said, "it makes the most sense."

Several people were surprised to hear that Gan Eden is only a waiting place. Others asked why the Rambam should be right when he is in the minority. He replied that there is only one truth, that neither view can be proved, and that he believes the Rambam's is the most philosophically sound. Asked whether the resurrection is the same as Mashiah, he asked the group to keep Mashiah out of it until a later class: it is a different subject, and minor in the larger picture.

The Letter on Resurrection

The Rambam's difficulty is where the resurrection fits. He was accused in Damascus, Yemen and France of denying it, and finally wrote, reluctantly, his Letter on Resurrection. There he insists that people are distorting his words. The resurrection is one of the thirteen principles: at some future time those who have a share in the World to Come will return to life in their bodies, live, have children and die again, and then return to Olam HaBa.

For the Raavad and the Ramban, the resurrection is the gateway to Olam HaBa itself.

Living with the uncertainty

As the class ended, someone asked how to live if we cannot be sure which view is right. Whatever the answer, it is agreed by all that the way to prepare is the same: to be the best version of ourselves in this world. "I agree that is the best approach," he said. Reincarnation was promised for the following week and Mashiah for the week after.

Summary

  • Hashem gives His perfect good by allowing us to cleave to Him; nothing of Him is "in" us, but we can reflect Him.
  • For the Raavad, the Ramban and others, the soul waits in Gan Eden until the resurrection ushers in a physical Olam HaBa.
  • For the Rambam, Olam HaBa is purely spiritual and begins at death.
  • In his Letter on Resurrection the Rambam affirms a physical resurrection.
  • Whichever view is right, the task in this world is the same.
