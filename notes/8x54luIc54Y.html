Sefer: Shulhan Arukh, Orah Hayim, Hilchot Shabbat Siman: 263, seifim 1-4 (beginning) Shiur: Part of Dayan Daniel Kada's Thursday Hilchot Shabbat class, Thursday 12 October 2023

These notes are based on a transcript of the recording.

Back to Hilchot Shabbat

After the month of the Hagim, the class resumed Hilchot Shabbat: honouring Shabbat, what not to do on Friday, when to stop work, and preparing for Shabbat. Now siman 263, lighting the Shabbat candles. The class learned it about three years earlier, so this time it went a little faster.

A common misconception: lighting candles is not a "woman's misvah". It is an obligation on every adult Jew, man and woman. It has become customary for women to light, as we will see, but at its core the misvah belongs to everyone.

Seif 1: a nice candle

The Shulhan Arukh says: be careful to light a nice candle. This is not the general hiddur misvah of buying a beautiful etrog or tallit. It means the candle should burn well. The only other place the Shulhan Arukh says this is Hanukkah, because the Gemara says that one who is careful with nice Shabbat and Hanukkah candles will merit children who are talmidei hakhamim. That is why women everywhere pray after lighting, and the Mishnah Berurah says she should pray for children who learn Torah, since "the candle of the misvah brings the light of Torah".

How many candles? - The halacha requires one candle. Two, for "Zakhor" and "Shamor", is a nice custom. - The Rema: one may light more, three or four. The Ben Ish Hai mentions seven or ten, and some Iraqi families still do this. - Better one nice candle than two poor ones. In the past people really asked whether to buy two wax candles or one olive oil candle: the olive oil, because one is what halacha requires.

Practical oil tip: olive oil is expensive, and some fill large glass holders to the top. You do not need to. Fill the glass about three quarters with water and a quarter with oil; it lasts a good three hours, more than enough.

The candle for each child. The Rabbi said he researched this custom many years ago and found it is not more than about 120 years old. It is a beautiful custom and has spread everywhere, but Sefaradim never had it. When he married he lit an extra candle for their first child, then found no source for it in his learning and stopped, doing a hatarat nedarim to be safe. "It's a nice thing to do, not a misvah."

Two obligations: the person and the house

This principle runs through the whole siman: - Hovat gavra, the obligation on the person. - Hovat bayit, the obligation on the house.

Person without house: a guest sleeping in the same room as a member of the host family. The house is lit, but the guest still has a personal obligation. If he is part of his parents' household, their lighting covers him. A single adult who supports himself, living in his own flat and visiting his parents for Shabbat, is not part of their household and must light himself. If he has his own room, that room is his house.

House without person: the family has lit and fulfilled the personal obligation, but there are rooms or corridors that will be used and are dark. The Mishnah Berurah says there should be light, at least one candle, in every room one will use on Shabbat, so as not to trip, but the berakhah is said where one eats. A pitch-dark hallway that people will walk through is not acceptable. Today electric lights and street lights normally take care of this.

Someone asked about rooms deliberately left dark for sleeping. The Rabbi said that if it is a place where you do not want light, you do not need it.

Why do we light at all?

Lighting is not from the Torah and not exactly rabbinic. It is "Divrei Kabbalah", from the Prophets, a category in between. Why is it part of Oneg Shabbat? Two explanations among the Rishonim: - Shalom bayit, preventive: so that people do not trip over things, or over each other and argue. - Enjoyment: light makes the meal and the whole Shabbat more enjoyable.

The Rema: one who forgot

The Rema says a woman who once forgot to light adds an extra candle for the rest of her life. This comes from Ashkenazi Rishonim, not the Gemara: a kind of fine to make sure women remember. Sefaradim do not have it.

Even for Ashkenazim, most poskim say it does not apply today if there was electric light in the house. It would apply only if there was truly no light, no electricity and no light from outside. A little light from a street lamp is enough, since one candle also gives only a little light.

In one case raised in class, a family had added a candle for many years after the candles were missed one Friday in an emergency; the Rabbi pointed out that there had been electric light in the house. Another participant asked whether forgetting because one fell asleep counts as "forgetting" or as circumstances beyond one's control; the Rabbi found the question interesting.

Seifim 2-3: men, women and priorities

Seif 2: both men and women must have a light in their homes. Even a poor person who relies on charity for food must ask for money for a Shabbat candle.

Seif 3: women take precedence. Why? The Rabbi contrasted two kinds of explanation: - The rational reason, given by the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh: women are at home and run the house, while men came back from the fields at the last moment. - The mystical reason, from the Arizal and brought by the Mishnah Berurah: Havah extinguished the light of the world by causing Adam to sin, so women rectify it by bringing light.

"If your lens is very mystical you'll find the otherworldly reason; if it's more rational you find the rational reason."

The Mishnah Berurah adds that if the wife is running late, the husband should light, as can happen in winter when Shabbat comes in at 3.35. But if that will cause a fight, remember the whole point is shalom bayit.

If money is short: the Shulhan Arukh says a Shabbat candle comes before a Hanukkah candle, since the Shabbat candle is for peace in the home. The Rabbi gave the order as he taught it: Friday night Kiddush wine, then Shabbat candles, then Hanukkah candles, then wine for the daytime Kiddush. He explained that Friday night Kiddush is from the Torah, and although one could make Kiddush on bread, it is better to fulfil the Torah misvah properly.

Seif 4: not too early

Seif 4 was begun: do not light too early, or it is not recognisable as for Shabbat, and not too late. If you light early, you must accept Shabbat with it.

So someone afraid of forgetting cannot light at 7.30pm in summer and carry on cooking until 9. For Sefaradim lighting does not in itself accept Shabbat, but lighting early is only allowed together with accepting Shabbat.

A practical problem was left for next week: Plag HaMinhah in a London summer is about 7.37pm, but many shuls start Minhah at 7.15. A wife who wants to come to shul, or a person living alone, cannot light before Plag. There are opinions that one may light even before Plag if one accepts Shabbat immediately; the reasoning will follow next class.

Practical summary

  • Every adult, man or woman, has an obligation of Shabbat light; the wife normally lights for the household.
  • One good candle is the requirement; two or more is custom.
  • A guest who is not part of the household and shares a room must light himself.
  • Make sure rooms and passages used on Shabbat are not left in darkness.
  • If the wife is late, the husband should light, without causing strife.
  • Do not light very early unless you accept Shabbat at the same time.
