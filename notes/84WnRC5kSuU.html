Where we are

The Rabbi went back over Seifim 2 and 3 to fix the three shitot from last time, then the Rambam and the Shulhan Arukh. In the second half he turned to a related question that has no explicit Gemara: what is the status of a person who arrives and says he is Jewish? He stressed that the two questions are different. The first is whether a Jew has a pesul (a mamzer, a halal and so on). The second is whether the person is Jewish at all.

Review: three shitot on checking a family

The Gemara in Kiddushin says that, according to Rabbi Meir, one checks four generations, while according to the Hakhamim "all families are presumed kosher" (kol hamishpahot bechezkat kesherot) unless there is an irur. The Gemara in Ketubot seems to require a check, so how do we understand it?

Rabbenu Tam is the most lenient, Rashi is in the middle and the Ramban is the strictest. According to Rabbenu Tam, the mishnah is about a Kohen's yihus for the altar. The Beit Yoseph and the Bah disagree about whether, nowadays, with no avodah in the Beit HaMikdash, one still checks even for a Kohen. The Bah says one does not.

The Rambam, and how the Shulhan Arukh rules

The Rambam says everyone may marry, but if he sees people fighting with each other, one must be concerned (hashash). Then: "a family that has an irur, and two witnesses testify" that a mamzer or halal has mixed in, one must check.

The Maggid Mishneh gives two ways to read the Rambam:

  1. Like Rashi. The Rambam speaks of someone unknown, and then one must check and cannot rely on the fact that he is called up to the Torah.
  2. Like Rabbenu Tam. The Rambam speaks of Israel, where "all families are presumed kosher" unless there is an irur, and only where a Kohen is involved must one check.

The Shulhan Arukh rules like the Rambam, so the question is which reading he follows.

Seif 2: quarrelsome families

The Arukh HaShulhan says that nowadays there is no family that does not have a quarrel with another family, so we do not take this to heart so much, unless it is exceptional. On the wording of the Shulhan Arukh, a person who has "brazenness, cruelty and hatred of others" is suspected not to be of Israel. The Beit Shemuel says all three signs are needed, based on the Gemara (a person who has these three signs is of Israel; one who lacks them is not necessarily not). The Rambam's wording, he observed, seems different, and it is not clear whether one sign or three are needed. The Rabbi said this was his own reading, which he had not seen elsewhere.

What does it mean to be suspected? The Rabbi noted the seif does not require the four-generation bedikah. It is found only in Seif 3. The Beit Yoseph asks why the Rif and the Rosh omitted this whole mishnah, and explains that for them, once there is an irur, only a bedikah kolshehi (any check) is needed. So there are two levels:

  • The formal bedikah of four generations (the mishnah in Kiddushin).
  • A bedikah kolshehi, enough to remove the doubt, with no formal criteria and depending on the circumstances. This is what Seif 2 means: if you see a quarrelsome or cruel person, any reasonable check will do.

Seif 3: what is an irur?

The Shulhan Arukh (following the Rambam) says an irur is when two witnesses testify. But then how would a bedikah help, if two witnesses say the father married a halalah? The Beit Shemuel brings Rashi: it is not a pinpoint testimony. It is two people saying that there is a rumour, a problem in this family, though they cannot say where. The Helkat Mehokek says this is also the Rambam's meaning. Rashi and the Rambam, he said, go together. Even so, it must be more than a general kol: it must be witnesses who come forward and say "we know there is a shemets here". The Shulhan Arukh's wording does sound like actual testimony, and the Beit Shemuel reads it that way.

Reading of the Shulhan Arukh

Beit Shemuel

Follows the first reading of the Maggid Mishneh (like Rashi). If someone unknown comes: check for pesulei kahal, and for a Kohen also pesulei kehunah, both men and women. If the family is known: no check, unless there is an irur. Then an Israelite checks only the man, and a Kohen checks for kehunah too.

Shaar HaMelekh (in the Pithei Teshuvah)

Follows the second reading, close to Rabbenu Tam. A person who just turns up and wants to marry need not be checked for pesulei kahal. Only if there is an irur must we check.

In practice, per the Beit Shemuel: unknown person means check. Known family means no check, unless there is an irur. Per the Shaar HaMelekh, no check for a person who arrives unless there is an irur.

Status of a person who says he is Jewish

The Rabbi turned to the first question: someone arrives in the community or at a Beit Din and says he is Jewish.

Tosafot, in two places. The Gemara in Pesahim tells of a non-Jew who boasted that he ate from the Korban Pesah, until they tested him by asking for the tail (which goes on the altar). From this Tosafot learns, as a first proof, that a person who says he is Jewish is believed, for the Korban Pesah and so also for marriage. A second proof is from Yevamot: a person who came forward and said he was Jewish was accepted.

Each of the two Tosafot questions one of the proofs. In Pesahim, Tosafot notes that in Jerusalem everyone is Israel, so the assumption there does not prove much for elsewhere. The Tosafot in Yevamot, as the Rabbi presented it, takes the proofs differently, and the two Tosafot disagree on whether each proof is enough alone. But they share a reasoning: most people who come to us wanting to marry or to join the community are Jews, so anyone who comes is presumed to be among that majority.

The Rabbi stressed that this reason depends on circumstances. We can argue today whether it is still true. The Ritva gives another reason: avid inish dilagluyei, a person does not lie about something that can be exposed. The Rabbi noted that nowadays this is also debatable, since forgery is easier.

The Rambam. He says that a person who says "I converted in a certain Beit Din" is believed, since he could simply have said "I am Jewish" and been believed. But the Rambam adds that this is only in Eres Yisrael, where everyone was kosher. Outside it, one must bring proof. The Rabbi explained this seems to say that outside Israel someone who just says "I am Jewish" is not believed. The difficulty is another passage, which says any Jew who comes and looks like a practising Jew is accepted.

The resolution (Maggid Mishneh and Shakh). A person who arrives and says "I am Israel" and acts like one is believed: he goes to shul, keeps Shabbat, dresses like a Jew. Proof is needed only if he says "I converted in a certain Beit Din", since not every Beit Din outside Israel is reliable. The Rabbi noted that the criteria of noheg bedarkhei Yisrael are somewhat different today, but a person who has gone to shul on Shabbat for six months would seem to count.

The modern shift

The Rabbi described how the rulings of the Batei Din shifted. When many Jews came from the former Soviet Union, there were no archives or communities, and at first Batei Din were lenient, accepting a person who said "I am from Russia and my father is Jewish". Over time it became clear that the system was being abused, since being Jewish today carries advantages. In the past, he said, why would anyone pretend to be Jewish when Jews were only persecuted? So they became stricter and asked for more proof, which is how the ketubah of the parents came to be used as evidence of Jewish identity. This is a modern development.

Since the reasons for leniency no longer apply in the same way, the Rabbi cited Rav Moshe Feinstein and the rulings of the Batei Din as the governing authorities: today some kind of proof is needed. Batei Din accept even weak proofs, such as a Jewish gravestone showing a mother is buried in a Jewish cemetery, because the basic rule remains that a person is believed. He also noted the new problem of Reform conversions, where a person may be a practising Jew in some sense without being Israel.

The basic law, as the Shulhan Arukh and the Rambam give it, is that anyone who says he is Israel is believed. Today one should not rely on that alone.

A parent's ketubah helps but is not foolproof: for example it may not show that a mother was divorced, and there are forgeries. It is the best available.

Practical summary

  • When a person is known and there is no irur, no formal check of four generations is needed, according to all the shitot. The disagreement concerns unknown families and a Kohen.
  • Quarrelsome or cruel families need only a bedikah kolshehi, and the Arukh HaShulhan says we do not take it to heart today.
  • An irur means witnesses who say there is a problem in the family, without pinpointing it. The Beit Shemuel and the Shaar HaMelekh disagree on how far the Shulhan Arukh requires a check of an unknown person.
  • According to Tosafot, a person who says he is Jewish is believed, since most who come are Jews. This depends on circumstances and is debatable today.
  • Nowadays, Batei Din ask for some proof, such as a parent's ketubah, though even weak proofs are accepted.
