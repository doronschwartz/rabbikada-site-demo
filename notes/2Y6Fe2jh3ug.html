Part One — Pickling (כבישה): Seifim 2–6

Introduction: The Prohibitions

The Gemara gives two explicit prohibitions:

  1. Making a salt-water solution (מי מלח).
  2. Salting many pieces together of vegetables that are normally pickled, such as cucumbers, onions and cabbage. Think of חמוצים, the cans and tubs of pickled vegetables.

A third prohibition (below) follows from these: salting even one piece in order to pickle it.

Why? A מחלוקת ראשונים

It is definitely a rabbinic prohibition. The question is what the Chachamim modelled it on.

Rashi and most Rishonim: דומה לעיבוד, similar to tanning

  • Me'abed (tanning) is one of the 39 melachot:
  • The first 11 melachot are the process of making the dyes: the land, ploughing, through to kneading and baking.
  • The next set deal with animals: trapping, slaughtering, skinning (מפשיט) and tanning (מעבד). One of the roofs of the Mishkan was made of hides.
  • Salting is an essential part of the tanning process. The Rabbi read up on this online before the shiur. Salt both begins the tanning and makes the hide durable for storage.
  • The Gemara says explicitly אין עיבוד באוכלין: there is no tanning with food, so there is no Torah melacha here.
  • Even so, salt completely changes the texture of the food (think of a cucumber becoming a pickle), just as salting a hide changes its texture and makes it durable. So the Chachamim forbade pickling rabbinically, as a corollary of tanning.

The Rambam: דומה לבישול, similar to cooking

The Rambam was presumably not happy linking hides with food. Which melacha would you tie it to instead?

Robert: "Isn't it a way of making food edible? So you might say it's some sort of cooking."

The Rabbi: "Exactly, perfect, very good."

  • The Rambam says salting makes food edible and is דומה לבישול, similar to cooking.
  • Of course it isn't actual bishul: there is no fire, and as we learned in Siman 318, fire is essential to bishul. But by changing the texture through salt, you are cooking in a sense, on a rabbinic level.
  • The Rabbi finds this the more straightforward explanation.

Mark: "I read somewhere that salt gives off heat, so from that point of view that's where the cooking occurs."

The Rabbi: "Interesting, I never thought of it like that." He had understood the Rambam as comparing the change in texture (uncooked → cooked; unpickled → pickled). The heat may also be part of it. "Maybe it's a combination."

Follow-up later in the shiur: a participant looked it up. Dissolving table salt (sodium chloride) in water is not exothermic. Other salts (calcium chloride, magnesium chloride) do give off heat, but we don't put those in food. Himalayan salt is still sodium chloride, and kosher/coarse salt is just a coarser grain. Perhaps older sources didn't know the science, or perhaps the heat comes from salt reacting with the proteins in a hide during tanning. That part of the tanning process does involve some heat, which may be where the idea came from.

The Shulchan Aruch brings both

Seif 2 gives Rashi's reason (משום דמי לעיבוד), and Seif 3 gives the Rambam's (מפני שהוא כמבשל). We have seen before that the Shulchan Aruch sometimes combines reasons when it finds both convincing. There are few, if any, clear practical differences between them.

Robert's question: a decree on a decree?

Robert: I can understand why you can't pickle. But why can't you add salt to water? Pickling itself is only rabbinic, so banning the salt water looks like a second derabbanan on top of the first.

The Rabbi: It isn't a gezeirah l'gezeirah. This is הכל גזירה אחת: it was all one enactment, made in one go. When the Chachamim banned pickling, they banned the things that make up pickling, such as salt-water solutions, together with it.

Seif 2 — Making salt water

אין עושים מי מלח הרבה ביחד לתת לתוך הכבשים משום דמי לעיבוד. אבל יכול לעשות ממנו מעט לתת לתוך התבשיל. אם נתן לתוכו שני שלישי מלח, אסור לעשות ממנו אפילו מעט.

Translation: One may not make a large amount of salt water to put into pickles, because it resembles tanning. One may make a small amount to put into a dish. If two-thirds of it is salt, even a small amount is forbidden.

Solution

Ruling

Two-thirds salt (extremely salty)

Forbidden in any quantity. Something that salty must be part of pickling.

Less than two-thirds, large quantity

Forbidden. A large amount suggests pickling, even if you only dip and eat.

Less than two-thirds, small quantity

Permitted. It is part of eating (צורך אכילה).

What is "a little" and what is "a lot"?

  • The logic: a small amount is clearly for dipping and eating, while a large amount suggests you're coming to pickle.
  • The Shulchan Aruch HaRav (the first Lubavitcher Rebbe, about 250 years ago) wrote that he doesn't know the difference between מעט and הרבה. Others have tried to give measurements.
  • The Rabbi: "The answer I take with all of this is very simple: common sense." It depends on the family, the context, the se'udah and what's going on. "You can't define these things." (The same issue comes back in tochen tonight.)

Practical case: salt water for karpas at the Seder

  • "We Syrians use lemon water," but Ashkenazim, and some Sefardim, use salt water.
  • A Seder salt water won't be two-thirds salt, so that isn't the issue. But if you have 50 guests and need a large amount for bowls across the table, that is a large quantity.
  • That's why the Rema writes in Hilchot Pesach that the salt water should be made beforehand.

Seif 3 — Salting many pieces together

אסור למלוח חתיכות צנון ד' או ה' ביחד, מפני שהוא ככובש כבשים, והכובש אסור מפני שהוא כמבשל. אלא מטבל כל אחת לבדה ואוכלה. אבל ביצים מותר למלחן.

  • You may not salt four or five pieces together of radish (or cucumber, cabbage, onion, or anything normally pickled). Done together, it looks like the start of pickling, even if you intend to eat it immediately.
  • Two or three pieces may be salted and eaten.
  • Here the Shulchan Aruch gives the Rambam's reason, כמבשל.
  • אלא מטבל כל אחת לבדה ואוכלה: dip each piece individually, either into an existing solution or into one made under the conditions of Seif 2.
  • The poskim: the Shulchan Aruch's "dip" is just אורחא דמילתא, the normal way of saying it. According to the Shulchan Aruch it is equally fine to salt two or three pieces.
  • אבל ביצים מותר למלחן: eggs are just the Gemara's example. Anything not normally pickled may be salted.

The Ashkenazi minhag (Magen Avraham)

  • Ashkenazim recorded a minhag never to salt vegetables on Shabbat at all. If you want salt on a vegetable, dip the vegetable into the salt, so the question never arises.
  • A parallel: the Magen Avraham also records the Ashkenazi minhag not to bathe even in cold water on Shabbat (because of squeezing, heating and other concerns). That is one of the reasons Ashkenazim don't swim on Shabbat.
  • Me'ikar hadin, for Sefardim, swimming in a swimming pool on Shabbat is permitted under certain conditions. Not the sea.
  • "Unless you know those conditions, don't do it."
  • For Sefardim: you may dip your vegetables into salt or a salt-water solution, or salt two or three pieces.

The third prohibition: salting in order to pickle

Even one piece, if you're salting it to pickle it, is forbidden. This applies even on Friday night for Shabbat afternoon.

The Rabbi's own example: you finish a tub of chamutzim, and a strong solution is left at the bottom. During the week he cuts up carrots or cabbage and drops them in. After a day or two they're pickled again, not as strong as the original but pickled. You can't do this on Shabbat, even with a single piece, because that is pickling.

Summary: the three rabbinic prohibitions

#

Prohibition

What is permitted

1

Making salt water

Only a small amount, less than two-thirds salt

2

Salting many pieces together (4–5+) of normally-pickled vegetables

Salt 2–3 pieces, or dip each piece (Ashkenazim: dip only)

3

Salting even one piece in order to pickle it

Not permitted

All three are rabbinic, rooted in either me'abed (Rashi) or bishul (Rambam).

Practical Questions

1. Salting a salad

Suppose the salad is cucumbers, carrots and cabbage, all commonly pickled. Based on what we've learned, you can't put salt on that by itself.

The halacha: the salt must come together with oil or lemon.

  • Some say to put the oil in first.
  • What's clear: if you dress the salad in one go (salt, then a second later oil, then lemon), that is fine. "I don't do a lot in the kitchen, but I do know how to spice a salad: salt, oil and lemon."
  • Salt alone, for a salty salad, is a problem.
  • If you salted it, went out to a friend, and came back to add the oil and lemon, that is asur.

Which vegetables count?

  • Red tomatoes aren't pickled. Chacham Ovadia writes specifically about green tomatoes, which are pickled: be careful not to salt them.
  • A normal mixed salad (red tomatoes, cucumbers, carrots, lettuce) combines pickled and unpickled items.
  • לעניות דעתי, you may put the salt on by itself. You're salting a salad as a whole, and that is not דומה לכובש כבשים. "The whole salad is clearly a salad."
  • Some disagree, because part of the salad is commonly pickled.
  • If the salad is primarily commonly-pickled vegetables, put the salt on together with oil or lemon, not salt alone.

2. Marie: sugar on fruit

Marie: Instead of salting, may you sweeten fruit on Shabbat, putting sugar on it and leaving it to absorb?

The Rabbi: "That's so funny you ask that." Chacham Ovadia was asked exactly this, about putting sugar on strawberries.

  • Permitted:
  • It is not bishul: fruit isn't cooked, vegetables are. We find the same principle in Hilchot Bishul.
  • It is not ibud: fruit isn't "tanned".
  • Caveat: it must be for Shabbat. You may do it Friday night for Shabbat afternoon, but not Shabbat afternoon to eat on Sunday, since that is preparing for after Shabbat. For Shabbat itself, it is permitted "100%, according to all opinions."

3. Robert: other pickling agents, such as vinegar

Robert: What about pickling with things other than salt, like vinegar or lemon? If the reason is tanning, you would never use vinegar on a hide.

  • The Rabbi: the Chachamim made a comprehensive ban on pickling, whatever does the pickling. Tanning is only Rashi's account of why.
  • The Mishnah Berurah (ס"ק ח') states explicitly that vinegar is included.
  • So putting garlic into vinegar to pickle it is the same problem.
  • Lemon juice alone doesn't pickle. Salting is the main part of the pickling process, and that was the Chachamim's concern. So squeezing lemon onto food isn't comparable.

4. Chacham Ovadia: אין כובש אחר כובש?

The case: you pickled vegetables during the week and serve them on Shabbat. At the end of the meal you realise some are only half pickled. They should have stayed in the solution longer. Can you put them back in? (Chacham Ovadia also asks whether something already pickled in salt may go into vinegar, or vice versa.)

If pickling is like…

Then…

Bishul (Rambam)

In bishul there is a מחלוקת ראשונים, as we saw in Siman 318: the Rosh holds that once food is מאכל בן דרוסאי (half-cooked, barely edible), further cooking is not Torah bishul. With bishul we're machmir because it's a safek de'oraita. Pickling is derabbanan, so we could say אין כובש אחר כובש and put it back.

Ibud (Rashi)

Who says there's an אין עיבוד אחר עיבוד? Who says half-tanned leather may be finished?

Chacham Ovadia's conclusion: uncertain, so better not to put it back.

Seif 4 — Dipping individually, then gathering

יש מי שכתב שמותר לטבול כמה חתיכות צנון אחת אחת לבדה ולהניחם יחד לפניהם כדי לאוכלם זו אחר זו.

  • This comes from the Terumat HaDeshen.
  • Dip one piece, put it down; dip the second, put it next to it; then the third, fourth and fifth. Then eat them.
  • Even though the end result is 4–5 salted pieces in front of you, this is permitted.
  • Why: the Chachamim forbade the act of salting (מליחה), and you're dipping.
  • The Mishnah Berurah discusses whether the pieces may touch, and says they should be kept separate.

Seif 5 — Salting cooked meat or eggs to keep them

אסור למלוח בשר מבושל או ביצה מבושלת להניחה.

  • You may not salt cooked meat or a cooked egg להניחה, to preserve it for later. Preserving falls under the same prohibition.
  • What does להניחה mean?
  • Some poskim say even a later meal on the same day.
  • The Rabbi's understanding of the Shulchan Aruch: it means for later on, i.e. for a long time. That is what makes it pickling or preserving.

Robert: Surely "for later" must mean later in the same day. For a later day it's already forbidden as hachana, preparing for another day.

The Rabbi: The hachana prohibition is a very minor rabbinic prohibition, not comparable to the others, so it isn't what this seif is about.

Robert: I didn't know there were different levels of rabbinic prohibitions. Isn't the punishment the same, e.g. lashes (מכת מרדות)?

The Rabbi: "Of course there are different levels." How can you compare a מלאכה שאינה צריכה לגופה to hachana? Incomparable. Makat mardut isn't for every rabbinic matter, and you definitely wouldn't get it for hachana. The poskim weigh one derabbanan against another when one must be violated, considering how close each is to the de'oraita, among other factors. "That's a whole shiur for another time."

Seif 6 — Beans and lentils

אין למלוח ביחד הרבה פולין ועדשים.

This is another example: you may not salt many beans or lentils together, because these are commonly pickled.

Summary of Pickling (Seifim 2–6)

  • The prohibition is rabbinic. Rashi says it resembles tanning; the Rambam says it resembles cooking. The Shulchan Aruch brings both.
  • Two Gemara prohibitions:
  1. making salt water (small amounts under two-thirds salt are permitted);
  2. salting many pieces together (2–3 pieces or dipping is permitted).
  • A third prohibition: salting even one piece in order to pickle it.
  • Practical rulings:
  • Salads: salt with oil or lemon. A mixed salad may be salted alone, according to the Rabbi.
  • Sugar on fruit is permitted for Shabbat.
  • Vinegar is included in the ban.
  • Half-pickled food: better not to put it back in the brine.
  • Dipping pieces one by one and then gathering them is permitted.
  • You may not salt cooked meat or eggs to preserve them.

Part Two — מלאכת טוחן (Grinding / Crushing): Seifim 7–12

Tochen runs from Seif 7 to Seif 12 inclusive, plus Seif 1, which we'll come back to at the end. We made a start tonight with about 20 minutes left.

What is Tochen?

  • Tochen is the ninth melacha. It is still within the first 11, the process of making the dyes. We ploughed, harvested and threshed, then selected the good seeds and removed the bad (borer) and sifted. Now the seeds must become a paste:
  • 9. Tochen: crush the seeds;
  • 10. Lash: knead them (later in this siman);
  • 11. Bishul / Ofeh: cook or bake them (already learned).
  • Three forms of tochen are definitely forbidden from the Torah:
  • grinding, e.g. salt;
  • crushing, e.g. pepper;
  • mashing, e.g. a banana.

Principle 1 — A שינוי גדול: the back of an instrument

The normal rule: a melacha done with a shinui (an unusual way) is rabbinically forbidden, though not de'oraita. The classic example is writing with your left hand.

The exception: there are three places in the melachot of Shabbat where the Chachamim permitted a Torah melacha done with a שינוי גדול, a very great deviation. The Rabbi remembers two offhand; he needs to think about the third. Two of them are in this siman: tochen (now) and lash (later).

  • The term שינוי גדול is the language of the Acharonim. It isn't found in the Gemara, and the Rabbi doesn't know if it's in the Rishonim. "This is at least my understanding. There may be another."
  • What counts as a שינוי גדול for tochen: crushing with the back of an instrument, meaning the handle of a knife, fork or spoon, rather than the working end. This is so unusual that the Chachamim permitted it, because it is needed for eating.
  • Normal grinding instruments:
  • a mortar;
  • a cheese grater;
  • vegetable/carrot graters.

All of these are forbidden for tochen. Most graters, certainly specialised vegetable graters, are muktzeh. Exception: a cheese grater is not muktzeh. We'll learn why later.

Practical: you want to make banana purée for a baby or mash an avocado. Mashing normally would be forbidden from the Torah, with some nuance we'll discuss. But you may mash it with the back of a fork or knife and eat it.

Seif 7 — Pepper

מותר לדוך פלפלין אפילו הרבה ביחד, והוא שידוכם בקתא של סכין ובקערה.

  • You may crush peppers, even many together, using the handle of a knife, onto a plate.
  • "Onto a plate": there are instruments you grind with and instruments you grind into (a mortar bowl, a grater's base). Neither specialised kind may be used. A normal plate is fine.

Side point: crushing pepper inside a cloth (the Maharshal)

The case: you put a lot of pepper in a cloth, wrap it up, and crush it with the back of a knife. Why not?

  • The Maharshal (about 500 years ago) first said no, because of מוליד ריח, creating a new smell in an item. We'll learn this properly later.
  • You may spray perfume or deodorant on your body on Shabbat.
  • You may not spray perfume on your clothes, or a lavender spray on your pillow before bed. The fabric takes on a new smell, which the Chachamim forbade as making something new.
  • Here, the cloth would absorb the pepper's smell.
  • The Maharshal later retracted and permitted it. There are several possible reasons:
  1. פסיק רישא בדרבנן מותר: the smell is an inevitable side-effect (psik reisha), and the prohibition it touches (מוליד ריח) is only rabbinic.
  2. Following the Maggid Mishneh's line we've seen before: the result is so far removed from the act that it isn't even a psik reisha. "If you got that, you got that. If not, don't worry about it."
  • Bottom line: you may crush pepper in a cloth if you're doing it to crush, not to scent the cloth. If you intend to give the cloth the smell, it is forbidden.

Seif 7 (continued) — Salt

אין כותשין מלח במדוך של עץ, אבל מרסקו ביד של סכין או בעץ הפרור, ואינו חושש.

This is the same principle, with salt instead of pepper. You may not crush salt with a specialised wooden (or stone) pestle. You may crush it with the handle of a knife or a spoon, "and there's no concern."

Principle 2 — אין טוחן אחר טוחן: no grinding after grinding

Rema: ודוקא מלח הגס, אבל מלח שהיה דק מתחלה ונתבשל ונעשה פתיתין, מותר לחותכן בסכין כמו שחותך הפת.

  • The ban applies only to originally coarse salt, salt that has never been processed. Salt that was fine to begin with and then cooked into flakes may be cut with a knife, just like cutting bread.
  • The principle: something that was already ground may be ground, crushed or mashed again.
  • Bread: flour was ground from wheat and then reconstituted into bread or a cracker. So you may crumble bread into tiny, tiny pieces.
  • These first two principles are unanimous and accepted.

Salt today:

  • Most salt today has already been processed, so it's already been ground. "Someone can check this."
  • Very coarse, natural salts would still be a problem.
  • Himalayan salt grinders:
  • These are forbidden: at least rabbinically, since you're using a specialised instrument.
  • Whether it's de'oraita depends on a principle still to come: whether you're grinding for immediate consumption.
  • The grinder itself is muktzeh. You can't even touch or move it on Shabbat.
  • Lumps in ordinary salt (e.g. water got in): you may crush them. The salt was already ground, so אין טוחן אחר טוחן.

Seif 12 — Cutting very fine: the fourth form of tochen

The Rabbi jumped ahead to Seif 12 before Seif 9, because Seif 9 only makes sense after it.

המחתך הירק דק דק חייב משום טוחן.

  • We had three forms so far: grinding, crushing and mashing. Some Rishonim (the Rabbi thinks the Rashba) understood the Gemara to mean that cutting into very small pieces is also tochen, a fourth form.
  • So even without grating, crushing or mashing, cutting a vegetable into minuscule pieces makes you חייב משום טוחן.
  • Practical: you can't make a salad cut into tiny pieces.
  • "I remember Rabbi Falk in Gateshead telling us you can't make an Israeli salad on Shabbat."
  • The Rabbi isn't sure: an Israeli salad (finely diced tomatoes and cucumbers) is quite small. Is it דק דק?

What is דק דק?

  • The Rishonim already write: שיעור דקותו לא נודע לנו, "we do not know the measure of its fineness."
  • So the Beit Yosef writes: don't cut too small, since that might be the size meant.
  • What's clear: regular small pieces are fine. The prohibition is about very small pieces.
  • "Whatever each person thinks is very small." But you definitely cannot cut a carrot into very, very small pieces.

Seif 9 — Cutting cooked meat finely

מותר לחתוך בשר מבושל או צלי דק דק בסכין.

You may cut cooked or roasted meat (and cooked fish) into extremely small pieces with a knife. We just said דק דק is tochen, so why is this permitted?

Two factors combine:

(a) אין טוחן אלא בגידולי קרקע. Tochen applies only to things that grow from the ground.

  • Cheese doesn't grow from the ground, so you may crush or grate cheese on Shabbat. (Mushrooms were a "bad example": they are subject to the same machloket.)
  • What about meat, chicken and eggs? This is a מחלוקת ראשונים. Some Rishonim say that since animals feed from the ground, they and their products count as גידולי קרקע. That would include eggs, because chickens feed from the ground.

(b) Tochen applies only to inedible food. There was a major opinion, though not the halacha, based on the Gemara's case of סילקא. What exactly סילקא is is unclear, just as with the Rosh Hashanah simanim. On this view, tochen means making something edible that wasn't, e.g. something too large to eat. If a food is already edible (a carrot, for example), crushing or cutting it small adds nothing. We don't hold this way.

Together: cooked meat is already edible (b), and it doesn't grow from the ground (a), even though some Rishonim count animals as ground-produce. The combination permits it.

Practical:

Food

Ruling

Cooked meat / chicken / fish

May be cut very fine, mashed or puréed, e.g. for a baby

Cooked eggs

Summary of Tochen So Far

Three principles of tochen learned tonight:

#

Principle

Practical result

1

A שינוי גדול (the back or handle of an instrument) is permitted for any grinding

Mash banana or avocado, crush pepper or salt, with the back of a fork or knife, onto a plate

2

אין טוחן אחר טוחן

Bread may be crumbled; processed salt and salt lumps may be crushed

3

אין טוחן אלא בגידולי קרקע

Cheese is permitted. Cooked meat, chicken, fish and eggs may be mashed or cut fine, by combination with the "already edible" view.

Also: cutting vegetables דק דק is tochen (Seif 12). Regular small pieces are fine; "very small" is unknown, so don't go too fine.
