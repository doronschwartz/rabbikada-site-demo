Where we are

The earlier shiurim covered intention, pesik reisha and, last time, melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah: doing an act for a purpose other than the one it served in the Mishkan. Almost all the Rishonim hold it exempt, like Rabbi Shimon, but the Rambam holds one liable, like Rabbi Yehudah. The Shulhan Arukh rules it exempt, yet cannot ignore the Rambam. Whoever knows these principles has half the questions of Shabbat solved, since it is then a matter of applying them with confidence.

The severity of melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah

Though exempt from the Torah, this is the most severe kind of rabbinic prohibition, and the usual leniencies of a rabbinic prohibition do not apply to it.

  • A dead body. The Shulhan Arukh's main opinion allows moving a corpse that is causing a smell only to a karmelit, not into the public domain, though a second opinion allows even that.
  • Ben hashemashot. One may do a rabbinic act for a misvah at twilight, both Friday and Saturday night, such as preparing books and seats in the synagogue for Tisha BeAv. This does not extend to melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah.
  • Extinguishing a candle for someone ill. Putting out a flame is not the extinguishing of the Mishkan, which was done in order to rekindle. Even so, the Shulhan Arukh permits it only if there is danger to life. With electricity it is different: for a bedridden person, the best option is to ask a non-Jew, then to switch off with a shinui, such as the elbow, and if even that is impossible, to switch off directly.

How it differs from pesik reisha. The Aruch HaShulhan explains with misvot. There is a dispute whether misvot need intention; the halacha is that Torah misvot do, rabbinic ones do not. But all agree that a negative intention, such as lifting the lulav while meaning not to fulfil the misvah until after the berakhah, means you have not fulfilled it. In Shabbat, a pesik reisha is treated as if you intended the result: you cannot say you did not mean to kill the chicken. But if you intend the act and do it for a different purpose from the Mishkan, that is like a negative intention, and it is only rabbinic.

Mekalkel: destructive acts

The Gemara gives two reasons for the exemption of digging a hole when you want only the earth: melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah, or that it is destructive. The Rambam, who holds one is liable for the first, still exempts this one, since he writes in chapter 1 that someone who wounds, burns clothes or breaks vessels in a destructive way is exempt. Melekhet mahshevet means productive work.

The Magid Mishneh and the Beit Yoseph explain that this applies in a house, where digging damages the floor; in a field, where it makes no difference, it is not a kilkul.

Three things follow.

It must be positively destructive. Not merely without benefit. The Rambam says that someone who writes in order to spoil a hide is liable: the melakhah is the writing, and the writing itself is not spoiled.

A side benefit does not remove the kilkul, if it comes from outside the act. Breaking open a door to enter a room, breaking a barrel to get the wine (Shulhan Arukh 314), or tearing open a letter to read it are all kilkul. The benefit does not come from the act of destroying. These are forbidden only rabbinically. one may certainly ask a non-Jew to open a letter if there is a need, and some allow opening it directly, though that is for another time.

A benefit from the act itself is not a kilkul. The Rambam says that someone who injures a person or animal to vent anger is liable, because the act itself satisfies him. Likewise one who tears clothes in anger or grief is liable, since it releases the impulse.

The Biur Halakha applies this to erasing. Erasing in order to write in its place is the true melakhah. But erasing a contract that records a debt you owe is also liable according to the Rambam, since there is a benefit in the act: there is no longer proof you owe the money.

Mitkayem: it must last

The Mishnah says that whoever does a melakhah "umelakhto mitkayemet" on Shabbat is liable. The Rambam does not list this among the general exemptions in chapter 1, because it applies only to some melakhot: building and the final hammer blow, writing (ink or surface that is not permanent; if both are impermanent it is two rabbinic prohibitions, and one alone is still rabbinic), erasing, dyeing and demolishing. For most melakhot, such as borer or cooking, the principle does not come up.

What counts as lasting? The simple reading, and the Rambam's, is that it lasts over the course of Shabbat. The Magid Mishneh says 24 hours: it need not last beyond Shabbat, and if it lasts only a few days it is still liable.

Rashi and the Rashba read the Mishnah differently. The word "Shabbat" goes with the person doing the act, not with the melakhah. The melakhah must be permanent in the normal sense. The Rashba says that we do not require it to last forever, only for as long as people normally expect writing to last, months or years. Something shorter than that is not mitkayem, even if it lasts a few weeks. For the Rambam the same act is liable if it lasts past Shabbat.

Built into the melakhah. For some melakhot the Rabbis set a minimum duration, below which there is not even a rabbinic prohibition. The example is kosher, tying: for Sefaradim a knot tied for less than seven days is permitted, for Ashkenazim 24 hours. The Rabbi called kosher one of the most misunderstood melakhot and promised to return to it.

An external cause. Writing on your skin is liable, even though sweat will soon erase it. The ink and the surface are themselves permanent, and only something external removes it. It is not mitkayem only when the ink or the surface cannot last by itself.

Shinui: a changed way of doing it

Kil acharyad, "with the back of the hand", is the way of doing something unusually. A common mistake: not everything done with the left hand is a shinui. The Mishnah says that carrying in the right and left hand is liable, because that is normal. Carrying in the ear, the mouth, or upside down is exempt. So the left hand alone is not a shinui, whereas carrying with your feet or in your hair is. Throwing a clod at a date tree so that the dates fall is likewise only rabbinic.

Only on Shabbat. The shinui exemption applies to melekhet mahshevet. A get written with the left hand, legibly, is valid, since in Gittin there is no such principle.

"Not the way" does not always mean shinui. When Rashi says that lifting without putting down is exempt because it is not the way of carrying, he does not mean it is a shinui; it is just half of the melakhah.

A shinui so great that it is permitted outright. Grinding with the handle of a knife is not tohen at all, so one may mash an avocado with the knife handle. Mixing egg and mayonnaise with criss-cross strokes is permitted for Sefaradim; Ashkenazim are stricter. Putting a grape to your lips and sucking the juice is permitted for all fruits except olives and grapes, so one may suck an orange.

Other examples. Biting your nails is a shinui, and so is plucking feathers by hand, since the norm is a utensil. Contemporary poskim apply this to switching off a light with the elbow, but not the left hand.

Questions and next steps

Make-up. Someone connected this to make-up. The Rabbi agreed: regular make-up lasts on the skin and is at least rabbinic, but water-based make-up that comes off easily has no permanence. Shabbat make-up is dry powder, which is permitted; liquid make-up that sticks to the face is a problem, though only rabbinic, since it is not mitkayem.

Practical summary

  • Melakhah sheeinah serikhah legufah is only rabbinic for us, but a severe rabbinic prohibition: leniencies such as ben hashemashot and the ill person do not simply apply.
  • A destructive act is exempt from the Torah, but only if it is positively destructive and has no benefit from the act itself. Tearing a letter or breaking a barrel open is rabbinic; injuring or tearing in anger is not a kilkul.
  • Building, writing, erasing and dyeing need to be lasting to be Torah prohibitions. The Rambam says this means lasting through Shabbat; Rashi and the Rashba say as long as people expect it to last.
  • A shinui is a really unusual way of doing the act, such as the elbow or the knife handle. The left hand alone is not a shinui, and not every "not the way" in the Rishonim means shinui.
