Topic: The origins of rabbinic authority: the Beit Din HaGadol, the misvah of Lo Tasur, and what happens when the court errs Shiur: Part 1 of a four-part series given by Dayan Daniel Kada in person and on Zoom, Tuesday 2 May 2023

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Why this series

With Shavuot approaching, the Rabbi opened a new four-week series on "half of our Torah", the Torah SheBeal Peh. Earlier in the year the class had studied how the Hakhamim interpret the Torah and what makes a mahloket. Now the question was more basic: where does the authority of the Hakhamim come from at all?

Why are the rabbinic laws in the Gemara binding on us? Why do we light Hanukah candles, wash Netilat Yadayim, or keep a second day of Yom Tov when we know the calendar? And how far does any of this reach today, when we hear that "the rabbis say" one thing or another? Tonight's class went back to the origins.

The Beit Din HaGadol

The first source is the Torah itself. When Moshe tells Hashem that he can no longer carry the people alone, Hashem tells him to gather seventy elders, and "I will draw from the spirit that is upon you and place it on them, and they will bear the burden of the people with you" (Bamidbar 11). This is the first time we meet a body of leaders sharing Moshe's role, and the verse says clearly that some of Moshe's spirit rested on them.

The Rabbi pointed out that "Sanhedrin" is a Greek word. The original name was the Beit Din HaGadol, the great court. It sat in the Lishkat HaGazit, the chamber of hewn stone in the Temple, so called in contrast to the altar, which could not be built of cut stone.

The Sanhedrin did not depend on the Beit HaMikdash. It was exiled from the Temple even before the destruction and moved between Yavneh, Usha and other places, and it continued to sit, judge and enact laws after 70 CE, until about the fourth century. The best way to picture it, he said, is a parliament: the legislature of the Jewish people, interpreting the Torah and making enactments to guard it.

This raises a question for later in the series. The Gemara was completed around 500 CE, after the Sanhedrin had ended. So what gives the Gemara its authority?

Lo Tasur: do not turn aside

The Torah commands: when a matter of law is too hard for you, go up to the place Hashem chooses, to the judges of that time, "and you shall do according to all that they instruct you... do not turn aside from what they tell you, right or left" (Devarim 17). There is a positive command to do what they say and a prohibition against veering from it.

The Rabbi then posed three questions to think about: - Can the Beit Din HaGadol make a mistake? (Yes, the Torah itself says so.) - If I am convinced they have erred, must I still follow them? - How far does their authority reach? Only to questions of law, or to anything they declare?

Right is left and left is right

Rashi, quoting the Sifre, explains "right or left" famously: even if they tell you that right is left and left is right, you must listen to them, and all the more so when they tell you right is right. The Rabbi brought it to life: "This is my right hand. The Sanhedrin rule this is your left hand. I know it's not true. No, you have to listen."

The Torah itself shows that the court can err. In Vayikra 4, if the whole community sins because of a mistaken ruling of the court, for example eating helev that the court declared permitted, the individuals do not each bring a korban hatat. The court brings the offering for the community.

The Rambam rejects the idea that you must follow a ruling you know to be wrong. If a person ate the fat on the court's ruling while knowing it was forbidden, he must bring his own offering. The distinction is whether he was a hakham in his own right. An ordinary person who "feels" the rabbis are wrong may rely on them, and if it turns out they erred, the court's offering covers him. But a talmid hakham who knows the ruling is mistaken may not act on it. And if he goes further and rules against the Beit Din HaGadol publicly, he becomes a Zaken Mamre, a rebellious elder.

The Rambam's source is the Yerushalmi, which reads the verse the opposite way to the Sifre: you follow them when they tell you that right is right and left is left. "If you're arguing with the Sanhedrin and you don't know," the Rabbi added, "then you're not just a sinner." You need real learning even to be in a position to disagree.

Why follow a court that can err?

Someone suggested the reason is purely practical: otherwise everyone would make up his own interpretation. The Rabbi agreed that this is one reason, and noted that the court's ruling is already a majority vote, perhaps forty against thirty.

The Ramban gives two reasons for the Sifre's view: - Anarchy. People are diverse and will always disagree. Without one binding authority, the Torah would become many Torahs. - Heavenly assistance. "The spirit of Hashem is on those who serve His sanctuary, and He will not abandon His pious ones." They can err, but far more often than not, they are right and you are wrong.

The Kuzari says something similar: the Sanhedrin had the Shekhinah with them, and they had broad wisdom in many disciplines. The Rabbi flagged this Kuzari for later, when the series reaches the idea of Daat Torah, a term now used for almost any opinion a rabbi expresses.

Someone asked whether all this applies to a court of twenty-three or of three. No, he said. This is only the Beit Din HaGadol of seventy-one.

Rabban Gamliel and Rabbi Yehoshua

The strongest proof for Rashi's view seems to be the story in Rosh Hashanah. Rabban Gamliel, the Nasi, accepted the testimony about the new moon. Rabbi Yehoshua disagreed, which meant that by his reckoning Yom Kippur fell on a different day. Rabban Gamliel ordered him to come with his staff and money on the day that Rabbi Yehoshua believed was Yom Kippur, and he went.

But the Rabbi explained why this is not a real proof. Sanctifying the month and the festivals is unique: "Mekadesh Yisrael VehaZemanim." The Torah gave the court the power to fix the calendar, so whatever they decide, provided they follow the criteria, is the date. Rabbi Yehoshua would not have eaten forbidden fat on Rabban Gamliel's word.

The authority of the Gemara

Why must we follow the Gemara rather than our own reading of the Torah? Some things were passed down from Sinai: "peri es hadar" is an etrog, and no one may argue. But much of the Gemara is interpretation by rabbis sitting in Bavel, not rulings of the Sanhedrin.

The Rambam answers in his introduction to the Mishneh Torah. He codifies only what is in the Gemara, and marks anything later as a custom; Taanit Esther, for example, is a later minhag. The Gemara binds us, he explains, because it was the one time in history when all of Israel accepted its authority. Communal acceptance can do what a Sanhedrin does. "It was like a convention, if you like."

After the Talmud, that acceptance no longer existed. The Rambam clashed with the Gaon Shemuel ben Eli, head of the yeshivah in Bavel, who claimed his rulings bound all of Israel. The Rambam replied that the Gaon's authority extended to those who had accepted him in Bavel, not to Egypt, France, Spain or England. A local Beit Din binds its own community. Someone pointed to Beit Hillel and Beit Shammai: once the halacha followed Hillel, it bound everyone, including Beit Shammai. The Rabbi agreed.

The other side appears too. In the story of the oven of Akhnai (Bava Mesia 59b), Rabbi Eliezer ben Hyrcanus brought miracles to prove his view, the rabbis refused to accept them, and he was placed under a ban, although Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai had said he outweighed all his other students.

Summary

  • The authority of the Hakhamim begins in the Torah, with the seventy elders and the Beit Din HaGadol, which sat until about the fourth century.
  • Lo Tasur obliges us to follow the Beit Din HaGadol. Rashi and the Ramban, from the Sifre, say this holds even when they seem to say right is left; the Rambam, from the Yerushalmi, says a true hakham who knows they erred may not follow them.
  • The Ramban's reasons are to prevent anarchy and because heavenly assistance makes their rulings far more likely to be right.
  • According to the Rambam, the Gemara binds us because all of Israel accepted it; after that, each Beit Din binds only its own community.
