Topic: Divine providence, what it means that God knows, and how His foreknowledge fits with our free will Sources: The Rambam's Thirteen Principles (Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin), Moreh Nevuchim, Hilchot Teshuvah 5:5 with the Raavad, Pirkei Avot 3:15 Shiur: Given by Dayan Daniel Kada at Raleigh Close, Wednesday 13 March 2019

These notes are based on an automatic transcript of the recording.

Three questions

The Rabbi warned at the start that this would be "quite philosophical and theological". He set out three questions. What is Hashgahah Peratit, divine providence? What does it mean that Hashem knows things? And the ancient question, which Jewish, Christian and Islamic thinkers have all wrestled with: how can God's foreknowledge (yediah) be reconciled with human free will (behirah)?

The tenth principle: God knows what we do

He began with the Rambam's Thirteen Principles, in his commentary on the Mishnah in Sanhedrin. The tenth principle is that God knows the deeds of human beings and does not ignore them, as the verses say: "Your eyes are open upon all the ways of the sons of men" (Yirmiyahu 32:19), and "Hashem saw that the wickedness of man was great" (Bereshit 6:5).

This principle has to come before the eleventh, reward and punishment (Sakhar VaOnesh). There can be no reward or punishment unless someone knows what we have done.

The principle grows in the later liturgy. The Rambam speaks of our deeds. The Ani Maamin, which the Rambam did not write, adds our thoughts. Yigdal, a later poetic summary, adds the future: "He sees the end of a matter at its very beginning." So Hashem knows what we do, what we say and think, and what we will do.

This, he said, is a source of great strength. People crave recognition, and the greater the person, the more their recognition means. To know that the greatest of all is interested in what we do, say and think means that life is not meaningless. Much of what we do, good and bad, goes unnoticed by others. Nothing goes unnoticed by Hashem. As the Gemara in Hullin puts it, no one so much as injures his finger below unless it is decreed above.

Four views of providence

In Moreh Nevuchim the Rambam surveys the views on providence. The Rabbi summarised them as four:

  1. Epicurus (from whom the word apikoros comes): there is no providence at all. Everything is random.
  2. Aristotle: the world as a whole has order, but what happens within it is chance. Whether a leaf falls or a ship sinks is all the same.
  3. The Islamic school: everything is under providence, even a leaf falling from a tree or the wind blowing one way or another.
  4. The Rambam: there is providence over the world, but individual providence applies only to human beings, and to anything in nature that directly touches a human life.

So for the Rambam a leaf that falls on a person's head falls by providence. A leaf falling in the middle of nowhere, or whether a particular cat eats a particular mouse, is chance. For animals there is general providence over the species: the world needs cats and it needs mice. But if the mouse is running around your house, and your cat catches it, that touches your life. He noted that the Ramhal in Derech Hashem seems to take a similar line, and that other schools in Torah disagree. The Baal Shem Tov famously held that every leaf falls by providence, and that is the Hasidic view. "We're discussing the Rambam today."

Someone asked whether "random" was the right word. The Rabbi agreed that "without order" is closer. Hashem allows the natural world to run as it does. Where a human life is involved, there is a reason behind it and a closer involvement.

Why some philosophers denied that God knows

The Rambam quotes philosophers who denied that God knows human actions at all. Their reason was God's perfection. If He knows what people do, He has to wait for them to do it, so His knowledge now is incomplete and keeps changing. Others added that knowing many things means multiplicity (ribbui), which contradicts the absolute oneness of God (Yihud HaShem).

The Rambam's answer is that their mistake was to equate human knowledge with divine knowledge. In the Rambam's thought, God and His attributes are not two things. His knowledge is not something He has. It is His essence. Before showing how this works, the Rabbi turned to free will.

Free will and foreknowledge

Free will is a foundation of the Torah: "The Torah makes no sense if there's no behirah." He asked why the Rambam does not list it among the Thirteen Principles. One answer is that it is contained in the eleventh: you do not punish your microwave for breaking down, because it has no choice. He also suggested a deeper answer he had seen. The thirteen principles are about God and how He runs the world, while behirah is a principle about ourselves.

The question is simple. If God knows the future, how can we be free? And if we are free, how can He know the future? The Rabbi brought four approaches.

Rav Saadiah Gaon. God knows the final outcome of our choices, after all our deliberations, but His knowledge does not cause them. Several people found this unsatisfying, and the Rabbi agreed it leaves questions. Someone asked why we need to live our lives at all if God already knows how they end. The Rabbi answered that God knows what we would choose, but we still have to choose it.

The Ralbag. "You may be shocked to hear this." The Ralbag held that God gives up His foreknowledge of our free choices so that we can be truly free. He knows what we do when we do it, but not what we will choose. The Rabbi compared it to the Kabbalistic idea that God withdraws Himself to make room for the world, and said it is theologically very difficult, since it sacrifices God's perfection.

Rabbi Hasdai Crescas. He held determinism, which is different from fatalism. Fatalism says our choice is an illusion and everything is fixed in advance. Determinism says we really choose, but someone who knew everything about us, our genes, upbringing, culture and history, could predict our choice with certainty. God has that complete picture. Crescas himself asks how reward and punishment fit with this. Most Jewish thinkers did not accept it. The Rabbi allowed that there is an element of determinism in our choices, "but generally speaking, we have full free will."

The Rambam. In Hilchot Teshuvah (5:5) the Rambam raises the question himself and says its answer is "longer than the earth and broader than the sea". God does not know with a knowledge separate from Himself, as people do. He and His knowledge are one. Just as no person can grasp God's essence ("no man can see Me and live"), no person can grasp His knowledge: "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Yeshayahu 55:8). But this is known without doubt: a person's actions are in his own hands.

Did the Rambam dodge the question?

The Raavad, in his gloss, criticised the Rambam for raising a problem and then leaving it to faith. The Rabbi argued that the Rambam meant something deeper. All we can ever know of Hashem are His attributes and how He relates to us, never His essence. Since His knowledge is His essence, we cannot fully understand it. So we have full free will, and Hashem has full knowledge.

He offered an analogy. If I watch someone commit a crime in front of me, my seeing it does not make him do it. Hashem's knowledge is all in the present. Time itself is a creation, and there was none before the world. For us there is past, present and future ("Hashem melekh, Hashem malakh, Hashem yimlokh"). For Hashem it is all present. "We have an answer. But the depth of the answer we can't understand."

This also answers the philosophers. Their difficulty about change and multiplicity only arises if God's knowledge is like ours.

He closed with Rabbi Akiva in Pirkei Avot (3:15): "Everything is foreseen, yet permission is given." The Tosafot Yom Tov there explains it with this same passage of the Rambam.

Questions

Someone asked how the Torah can say that Hashem does not pull a person in any direction. The Rabbi explained that Hashem does not remove the opportunity. Someone set on eating non-kosher food will not find the restaurant closed that evening. He gave the example of Bilam: told not to go, he asked again, was allowed to go, and Hashem was then angry that he went. A person is led along the path he chooses.

Asked about verses that say Hashem "reconsidered", he gave the same answer. We see a change of mind because we live in time. Seen all at once, from Hashem's perspective, the question does not begin.

Summary

  • The tenth principle: Hashem knows our deeds, our thoughts and our future, so nothing we do is meaningless.
  • For the Rambam, individual providence applies to human beings and to whatever touches their lives. The rest of nature has general providence over the species.
  • Saadiah Gaon, the Ralbag and Crescas each resolve the conflict between foreknowledge and free will by limiting one side.
  • The Rambam keeps both: God's knowledge is His essence and beyond us, and our choices are truly ours.
