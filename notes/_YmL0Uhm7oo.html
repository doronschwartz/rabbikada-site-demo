The fifth letter of the series (after Ovadia, astrology, Ibn Jabbar and Kiddush Hashem): to Rav Pinchas, who came from Provence to become dayan of Alexandria. The Rambam was instrumental in his appointment, "but they didn't see eye to eye on many things."

Part 3: The Specific Questions

a) "Did you say in public that I know nothing?"

Pinchas, quoted verbatim:

You said in public about so-and-so, the dayan of Alexandria, that he knows nothing and is not proficient in Talmud. Why would you say such a thing? And I asked you many questions, and you never answered. (And in a later letter:) Please teach me everything I asked.

Listen carefully to my answer. I never said anything of the sort, because I don't lie, and it isn't true: you do know.

So what did he say? "He's admitting something." To understand it, some background.

Background: two kinds of property a wife brings into a marriage

נכסי מלוג, nichsei melog

נכסי צאן ברזל, nichsei tzon barzel ("iron sheep")

Ownership

Stays hers

Stays in her name

Income (e.g. rent)

Goes to the husband

Goes to the husband

Rise or fall in value

Hers. A house worth £100k at the wedding and £200k at a divorce five years later: the gain is hers; a loss is too

The husband is responsible. He must return the full value, even if it burned down or lost value

For the husband

Benefit, no responsibility

Benefit and responsibility

(Cash generally goes in the nedunya, the dowry; this is about property.)

The case of the gold jewellery

  • In a dispute over a piece of gold jewellery, Pinchas had ruled that it was tzon barzel: the husband is responsible for it.
  • Then the husband, the son-in-law of Abu al-Faraj, came back: I don't want responsibility. I want it reclassified as melog**.
  • The Rambam heard that Pinchas ruled he could, and could force it on the wife: "the best of both worlds" for the husband, all the benefit and none of the responsibility.

I told Abu al-Faraj, the father-in-law: I don't believe Pinchas would rule like that. If he did, bring me proof.

Then came a letter signed by two responsible witnesses: Pinchas had ruled publicly that any husband may convert tzon barzel into melog.

מיד תמהתי: I was astonished at once. That isn't the halachah. What comes in under one law stays under that law. If they both agree, that's different, but the husband can't force it on the wife.

"There's no discussion about this. He's wrong." With the Rambam were another dayan and three or four others; he showed them the letter, and everyone was astonished.

What the Rambam actually said

Don't be surprised: Pinchas comes from Provence, from France. All the Jews in Christian countries, even their great rabbis, aren't experts in practical law.

Islamic lands (Egypt)

Christian lands (Provence, France)

Jewish courts

Proper, permanent batei din

Not allowed; makeshift batei din

Monetary and marital cases

Daily: "bread and butter"

Rare; "not on their fingertips"

Result

Answer readily

Take a long time, must search and analyse, and can err

His analogy: "If the Temple were rebuilt today and you came running to me with a practical question about a sacrifice, I couldn't answer straight away. I'd have to search, think, analyse, because it isn't something we do regularly."

The Gemara's point: you can master all the books, but you can't compare even a sage who knows the whole Torah to someone who has faced practical questions. "Any rabbi will tell you they remember best what they've taught and what they've been asked." Practical questions force you to think things through.

"God testifies that this is exactly what happened. I'm not adding or subtracting."

So the rumour evolved from "he comes from a country without regular courts, so he struggled and got it wrong" into "the Rambam says he knows nothing."

Was it lashon hara?

The Rambam doesn't apologise. Why wasn't it lashon hara?

  • Saying a ruling is wrong isn't lashon hara. If someone says Rabbi X told me this is the halachah and you know it isn't, saying that rabbi is wrong is giving the correct ruling.
  • Adding "he's from France, where they have no batei din" depends on tone. Said derogatorily ("these Ashkenazim, they know nothing"), it might be. Said to explain, understand their culture; they lack the experience we dayanim in Fustat have, it isn't. "He's not saying it to demean Pinchas, just to explain."

b) "You never answer my questions"

I swear I don't know of any question you asked that I didn't answer, except one, about marriage, which came while I was ill. I'll answer it at the end of this letter.

The rest:

What Pinchas asked

The Rambam's response

One question, over and over (its content isn't stated)

"You harassed me with this question." I gave my answer. Do what you want. Stop asking

A question about a get

I answered with proofs; you came back: I still don't see why you said it's kosher. I kept quiet

Anything else

Perhaps lost in the post. Ask again

My way, always: when I debate with someone and see he won't budge or consider the other side, I fall silent and leave him to his view.

"An important principle in life. If you're trying to teach someone, you give your opinion, and they persist, leave them and move on. Don't waste your time."

The Rabbi's own pre-Pesach example:

"A week ago someone asked me: can you buy Coca-Cola? I said yes. Full stop. Last night he texted: I asked another rabbi and he was very surprised at your ruling. Can you explain your reasoning? I didn't answer. Every year I get this. You have to know when to move on."

c) The widow and the three signatures

A widow was disputing a property (a house or courtyard) with her children. The ruling: she needn't take an oath, because she's in possession, so the children can't claim it. The Rambam answered and had two other dayanim sign with him.

Background:

תשובה, responsum

פסק, ruling

What

A question sent from afar; you give your opinion

The parties appear before you

Who signs

The rabbi alone

All three dayanim

This was a responsum from Alexandria to Fustat, so Pinchas asked: why three signatures?

You're right; normally I'd sign alone. But I was told that if only I signed, you'd tell the secular judge: this rabbi says that, I say otherwise; who's right? and the case wouldn't come out correctly. With three rabbis, your view would be the minority. So I forwent my honour and signed with the others to make sure the ruling stood.

"Don't accuse her." Pinchas had suggested the widow wasn't afraid to swear, so of course she'd claim what she claimed.

How dare you say that about a Jew? How can a sage like you say it? Only the wicked aren't afraid to take an oath. Don't accuse this woman of being one of them.

d) The get sent by agent

Background 1: a get by agent.

- A get can be handed over directly or via a shaliach, "from one end of the earth to the other." "We do this nowadays all the time; we send gittin to Israel."

- She is divorced only when the get reaches her hand. Until then anything can cancel it: the agent resigns, dies, falls ill, loses it, or the husband retracts, without even telling the agent.

- "A terrible thing to do," so nowadays the husband takes a full oath not to retract the agency.

Background 2: conditions in a get. (Theoretical: "this is avoided nowadays for many reasons.")

"על מנת", "on condition that"

Other wording

Takes effect

Now. Once she fulfils the condition (say, delivering a parcel to someone), she is divorced retroactively from today

Only once the condition is fulfilled

Can she remarry meanwhile?

Yes

No

Formal requirements

None: very easy

Strict: double the condition (tenai kaful), state the positive before the negative, etc.

The case: the same son-in-law of Abu al-Faraj came to the Rambam: Abu al-Faraj treated me badly; he made me divorce his daughter, though she never asked for it. While talking with him, the Rambam realised the get was being sent by agent.

So, cleverly, he wrote to Abu al-Faraj: has your daughter received the get yet? Not yet, came the answer, with the text of the get and the agency. "Don't delay. Make sure she receives it quickly."

Two criticisms of Pinchas:

1. You dawdled. A get by agent can be annulled at any moment: the agent could die, be killed, drown at sea, be lost. "What use is a dayan who doesn't think proactively and tell her to go and receive her get?"

2. You mixed the formulas. You used al menat and doubled the condition with positive before negative. Unnecessary: al menat needs none of that. "It doesn't actually invalidate anything, but it's not appropriate for someone like you to write mixed messages."

"Chacham gadol." The Rambam calls Pinchas "a great sage" four or five times in the letter. "I don't know if he literally means it, or is saying how can a great person like you keep making these mistakes? Maybe you're not as great as people say."

Part 4: Pinchas's Troubles at Home

"Much shorter; we've done the main bits." Pinchas "seems to be suffering a lot."

Trouble

The Rambam's response

Avraham goes around Alexandria saying "the Rambam is right and Pinchas is always wrong"

God knows I scolded him, by word and in writing. "He thinks he's helping me. I told him clearly: I don't want your help. Don't speak badly about Pinchas." He made him swear to stop

Abu al-Faraj says Pinchas ruled for the son-in-law because they're friendly: they eat together, and Pinchas got benefits from him ("not exactly bribes, but close")

I wrote to Abu al-Faraj: that's not why. He made a mistake, and even the Supreme Court can err, as the Torah says. "I pacified him."

"You said in public that I have an angry disposition"

"Yes, I said it." (Below)

Rumours Pinchas wants to return to "Rome", to Christian Europe (Shilat's note)

Don't. Don't leave where you're established

Abu Zikri causing trouble

Don't worry; he's powerless

"Yes, I said you're angry", "the best bit of the whole letter"

"Everywhere else he defends himself robustly. Here it seems like checkmate: Pinchas says you called me an angry person in public, and the Rambam says I won't lie. I said it."

How he gets out of it:

The Gemara: when a talmid chacham gets angry, it's the Torah that heats him: הלא כה דברי כאש, "are not My words like fire?" I was praising you in the Gemara's words: your anger is the anger of Torah.

"Fascinating." "It is difficult, I agree."

Make peace with Rabbi Daniel

The context: Pinchas's feud with Rabbi Daniel (Part 1: Daniel had ruled leniently on washing, and Pinchas opposed him).

That's what upsets me. Why don't you get on with Rabbi Daniel? He's a great person, God-fearing, and he's bringing people to do good, in Fustat and in Alexandria. Be friends; study together. If you let him teach my Mishneh Torah to others (under your leadership, of course), you could ignite so many people with the depths of Torah. I wish it would happen. Make up with each other.

"Don't go back to Europe"

"We don't have Pinchas's letters, but from everything the Rambam quotes, he seems to be going through a lot: complaining about this person and that person, the Rambam, the ruling, the people who came to him." Don't leave your place under pressure. (One line, about going "to places with armies," is unclear.)

Was "Avraham" the Rambam's son?

The Rabbi's own idea ("I just realised this now; no one says this, I have no proof"):

- When the Rambam names people he usually adds "Mar" or "Abu"; here it's just "Avraham".

- Pinchas expects the Rambam to control Avraham, and the Rambam can order him to stop and make him swear.

- The Rambam's only son was Avraham (Rabbeinu Avraham ben HaRambam; he also had a daughter).

So perhaps Avraham was the Rambam's son, zealously defending his father.

Part 5: Is Kiddushin by Money Biblical or Rabbinic?

"I don't know if you could spend a whole shiur on it, but briefly." Pinchas's unanswered question, the one that came while the Rambam was ill.

The Rambam's answer

1. Go and read my Sefer HaMitzvot. Written in Arabic (we now have it in Hebrew); Sa'adia in Alexandria has a copy. It sets out 14 principles for which commandments count among the 613.

Learn those 14 principles and you'll see that everyone who counted the mitzvot before me got it wrong.

Background on counting: the Gemara says there are 613, but go through the Torah and you can find many more. So which count? Many tried, including Rabbi Abba Kira (7th–8th century) and Shlomo ibn Gabirol. The Azharot read at Minchah on Shavuot are poems counting the 613, "and they include things the Rambam says are completely wrong." (The Rabbi gave a Zoom shiur on this two or three years ago.)

2. The principle (Sefer HaMitzvot): of the 13 hermeneutic rules (said every morning at the start of Shacharit), what the Sages derive through them does not automatically become de'oraita. It counts as biblical only where the Sages say explicitly that it is.

So:

- Relations: explicit in the verse, so biblical.

- Money: derived by gezeirah shavah, with no explicit statement, so rabbinic.

- Document: also derived. "You're right; by my principle it should be rabbinic." But the Gemara clearly treats it as de'oraita, so he counted it biblical.

"This is a whole shiur on its own: what de'oraita and de'rabbanan mean here."

Did he retract?

Rabbeinu Avraham, the Rambam's son, says his father retracted and held that all three are biblical, as almost everyone does. "It's not clear he did. Much ink has been spilt over this."

The Close

My chieftain, don't worry about answering everything I've written. But equally, don't expect me to answer everything you write. I'm very busy and very weak. I haven't the time or strength even to read the letters I receive, let alone answer them. If you have practical questions, send them. Otherwise I have no time, because of my constant weakness, and because I need to learn a little myself.

Dated the 35th day of the Omer.
