Shavuot and the giving of the Torah

In the Torah, Shavuot is an agricultural festival. Over time it also became the anniversary of the giving of the Torah. The first place the Gemara links the two is a dispute in Shabbat: according to the Hakhamim the Torah was given on the sixth of Sivan, the day of Shavuot; according to Rabbi Yose, on the seventh. On Rabbi Yose's view, the Rabbi noted, Shavuot itself is not the day the Torah was given.

The question of the evening was: what do we mean when we say the Torah was given at Sinai? Only the Ten Commandments? The whole Torah? The Oral Torah too? All at once, or in stages?

First, a word about the word Torah. In its narrow sense it means the five books, Bereshit to Devarim. More widely it means all twenty-four books of Tanakh. In its loosest sense, "learning Torah" means any study that connects us to Hashem. We have to be careful which meaning the sources intend.

The Rambam's principle

In the eighth of his thirteen principles of faith, the Rambam writes that the whole Torah in our hands came to Moshe from the Almighty, and that Moshe was like a scribe taking dictation, writing down the events, the stories and the commandments. So it seems the whole Torah came at once. But the verses tell a more detailed story, and the Rabbi asked the group to follow them closely.

He mentioned the Spanish and Portuguese custom of reading two passages with a special ornate tune: the Revelation in Yitro (Shemot 19) and the covenant at the end of Mishpatim (Shemot 24). These two passages hold the key.

What happened on the day

In Shemot 19 the people arrive at Sinai on Rosh Hodesh Sivan, and Hashem prepares them. In chapter 20 come the Ten Commandments. According to Hazal, after the first two the experience was too intense, and the people asked Moshe to relay the rest.

Then the Torah goes straight into Mishpatim: three chapters of laws, from murder and manslaughter to slaves and witchcraft, ending with a warning against idolatry. In chapter 24, Moshe tells the people "all the words of Hashem and all the laws," they answer "all that Hashem has spoken we will do," and Moshe writes everything down and rises early the next morning to make a covenant.

Rashi reads chapter 24 as out of order, belonging before the Revelation. But almost all the Rishonim, the Rashbam, Ibn Ezra and the Ramban, read it in order. The Ramban says Ibn Ezra grasped it correctly: on the same day as the Revelation, Hashem gave Moshe the laws of Mishpatim, Moshe taught them to the people, and that day he wrote in a book everything from Bereshit up to that point. "It must have been a good few hours they were sitting and listening to Moshe."

Three ascents, 120 days

Next, Hashem says: "Come up to Me on the mountain, and I will give you the tablets of stone, and the Torah and the misvah." The Gemara in Berakhot (5a) explains: the tablets are the Ten Commandments, Torah is the written Torah, misvah is the Mishnah, and so on. Rashi on Behar says that just as the details of Shemitah were given at Sinai, so were the details of all the misvot. So Moshe went up for forty days to learn the explanation of what the people had received only in broad outline.

Then came the golden calf. Moshe came down, broke the tablets, and the next day went up again for a second forty days, this time to pray for atonement, not to study. When he came down, Hashem told him to carve new tablets and ascend a third time. He came down with them on Yom Kippur.

So in the 120 days after the Revelation, Moshe was with the people for perhaps a day in total. Only after the third descent, with his face shining, did he begin teaching. Rashi describes the order: Moshe learned from Hashem, then taught Aharon, then Aharon's sons, then the elders, then the whole people, each group hearing it more times according to its rank. And where were they? Still at Sinai. They did not leave until the twentieth of Iyar of the following year (Bemidbar 10:11).

The rest of the Torah

Even then the giving was not complete. The book of Vayikra, with all its laws of sacrifices, opens "And He called to Moshe": Moshe was being taught new material after Yom Kippur, over the following months, and passing it on. After they left Sinai, more was given on the journey. The last verse of Bemidbar says these are the commandments Hashem gave "in the plains of Moav."

So the Torah was not all given on Shavuot, nor all during the year at Sinai. Most was given at Sinai in the sense of that year, and the rest over the following decades. The Ramhal, in Daat Tevunot, says plainly that the whole Torah was not given at the Revelation. What happened there was a spiritual preparation: the people were made fit to receive the Torah over the coming time.

When was it written?

The Gemara in Gittin (60a) records a dispute. Rabbi Yohanan says the Torah was given "scroll by scroll": Moshe wrote each section as he received it, and joined them at the end. He learns this from David's words, "Behold I have come, in the scroll of the book it is written of me" (Tehillim 40:8), which the Rabbi connected to Shavuot: when Shaul and Doeg questioned David's lineage through Ruth, David found himself already hinted at in the Torah. Resh Lakish says the Torah was given sealed: written once at the Revelation up to Mishpatim, and once at the end of Moshe's life.

Questions from the class

  • Saadiah Gaon: someone asked about Saadiah Gaon's view, quoted by Rashi, that all 613 misvot are contained in the Ten Commandments. The Rabbi agreed it is true, but said it works after the fact. Once you know the whole Torah, you can find it in the Ten Commandments. No one suggests you could derive the Torah from them. At Sinai the principles were given; the details took time.
  • Events that had not happened yet: asked how the Torah could record later events, he answered that no one suggests anything was written in advance. What was written at Sinai was Bereshit to Mishpatim.
  • Torah as infinite: a participant said Torah is the living, infinite word of Hashem, so every new insight is already latent in it. He agreed, citing the Gemara in Megillah that every future insight was shown to Moshe, but said that was shown to Moshe on the mountain, not taught to the people on the day.
  • The last eight verses: on how Moshe could write of his own death, the Gemara in Bava Batra brings two views: Yehoshua wrote them, or Moshe wrote them in tears.

A participant closed with a story about Sir Moses Montefiore: when he commissioned a Sefer Torah, he would write not the last three words, as is customary, but the last four, because the fourth from the end is "Moshe."

Summary

  • On the day of the Revelation the people received the Ten Commandments and the laws of Mishpatim, which Moshe wrote down.
  • Moshe spent three forty-day periods on the mountain and began teaching only after Yom Kippur.
  • Much of the Torah, including Vayikra, was given in the following year at Sinai, and more on the journey to the plains of Moav.
  • The Ramhal: the Revelation prepared us to receive the Torah, rather than giving all of it.
  • Rabbi Yohanan and Resh Lakish dispute whether Moshe wrote section by section or all at the end.
